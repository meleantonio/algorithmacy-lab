# *Human–Computer Interaction*: what the journal publishes and how its papers are built

*Written by Claude on 2026-10-13. The author chose* Human–Computer Interaction *(Taylor & Francis; editor
Steve Whittaker) as the target. Sources opened this session: the journal's Crossref record (sixty most
recent items), its instructions for authors, its own "Guidelines for Submitting to HCI" document, its
metrics page, and the full text of four open-access articles read in the author's browser. Section word
counts were computed from the article pages by script and exclude tables, figures and references. No
full text is stored in this repo.*

## What the journal says it wants

From "Guidelines for Submitting to HCI" (the journal's public document, linked from its instructions):

- **A conceptual paper is in scope and named.** "Theoretical, 'framework,' review, and even essay papers
  are all similar in that they present arguments and evidence for scientific theories, analytic
  frameworks, or new perspectives." They "are evaluated on the insightfulness of their ideas and the
  rigor of their arguments and evidence".
- **The test such a paper must pass.** "It is essential that they show the value of their insights by:
  (a) demonstrating implications for design, (b) generating new areas of study, or (c) reconceptualizing
  existing data or theories. HCI strongly encourages these kinds of papers, but note that they are
  probably the most difficult to write and to evaluate."
- **The journal's own model of a framework paper** is Terveen and McDonald (2005), "Social matching: A
  framework and research agenda", *ACM TOCHI* 12(3), 401–434: "a review of relevant social science and
  systems work with a set of orienting questions for new research".
- **Length.** "Articles average 35 printed pages, and 90% of the articles are between 25 and 45 pages."
  No strict limit.
- **It must speak to HCI.** "The paper must be scholarly and not insular; it must establish its relation
  to the relevant HCI literature. Papers that present perspectives that are usually outside the field of
  HCI are encouraged, but clear links to HCI must be made."
- **Most submissions are rejected before review.** "Historically, a majority of submissions are rejected
  at this step." The listed reasons that bear on this paper: no relation to prior HCI literature, and
  "only preliminary research results". The guidelines also say "every paper should have empirical
  evidence that validates its results".
- **A proposal route exists.** "If you are still unsure about whether to submit a regular article, submit
  a brief Proposal to get a quick opinion from the Editor." There is an Article Proposal Template.
- **Prior posting.** "A substantial part of the work must not have been published previously", overlaps
  must be declared, and "you must withdraw, or at least begin the process of withdrawing, papers posted
  to open-access websites (such as arXiv) before submitting".

From the instructions for authors: one article type ("Research Article"); "an unstructured abstract of
200 words"; American spelling; double quotation marks; Word template; the publisher's reference style.

From the journal's metrics page: impact factor 5.8 (2025), 12 days on average to a first decision, 124
days to a first post-review decision, 2% acceptance rate.

## What it has published lately

Sixty most recent items in Crossref (August 2023 to October 2026). The journal publishes roughly fifteen
to twenty articles a year.

- **Six of the seven articles posted since August 2026 are about generative AI**: evaluating human–AI
  co-creation, cognitive friction in language models, dialogue patterns in co-creation, AI group-awareness
  tools, malleable writing environments, students using AI for course readings.
- **Conceptual and essay papers appear regularly**: Crabtree (2025) on evaluating qualitative research,

  "Clarifying and differentiating discoverability"; Dix (2024), "The future of PIM: pragmatics and

  - Disley et al. (2026), "Designing cognitive friction with concept suppression in large language

**Section sequences.**

- *Crabtree*: 1 Introduction · 2 Motivation and method · 3 Positivism · 4 Interpretivism · 5 Positivism
  and interpretivism · 6 Quantification and human science · 7 The unique contribution of interpretivism ·
  8 Quality assurance (five criteria, 4,650 words) · 9 Conclusion (862 words).
- *Dix*: 1 Introduction · 2 Deconstructing PIM? (three sub-terms) · 3 PIM in practice · 4 Using
  technology (five subsections) · 5 Understanding people (four subsections) · 6 Summary (588 words).
- *Özçetin et al.*: 1 Introduction · 2 Background (usability studies; sociotechnical background;
  technological mediation) · 3 Methodology · 4 Three posthuman moves, each ending in a "Design opening:
  What if…?" · 5 Revealing design as more-than-human design tactics · 6 Discussion · 7 Conclusion.
- *Disley et al.*: 1 Introduction · 2 Background (four subsections) · 3 Representational intervention
  (the conceptual contribution) · 4 System · 5 Methods · 6 Results · 7 Discussion (nine subsections,
  including Implications, Limitations, Further work) · 8 Conclusion.

**What the introductions do.** Each runs 500 to 830 words of continuous prose, with no subheadings and no
bullet lists except a closing list of contributions.

1. Open on the state of the field or the phenomenon, with citations from the first sentence.
2. Name the problem as a failure of a current approach.
3. State the paper's move in one sentence ("we propose that…"; "we use ToS as an entrance point…").
4. State the contribution explicitly. Crabtree: "The focus and contribution of this paper is on helping
   the HCI community remedy the problem of…". Disley et al. list "C1 (Conceptual)… C2 (System)… C3
   (Empirical)".
5. Close with a roadmap sentence or the contribution list. Dix adds a scope disclaimer: "This paper is a
   personal viewpoint; it is neither a complete review… nor a systematic analysis".

**House features.** Numbered headings to three levels (1., 2.1., 8.3.1.). First person throughout, "I"
for a sole author (Crabtree, Dix) and "we" otherwise. A Background or related-work section second. A
named conceptual section that carries the new term. A final section of 400 to 860 words that restates
the argument and ends on what the field should do. Published articles carry an "HCI editorial record"
(dates received, revised, accepted, and the action editor).

## What this means for the paper

**The paper is a "framework" paper in the journal's terms, and Terveen and McDonald (2005) is the model
the journal itself names.** Its formula, a review of outside literatures that yields a framework and a
research agenda, is what the outline already does: history and human factors, the disclosure literature,
a framework (algorithmacy and two conventions), and an agenda (the experiment). Section 8 should be
written as a research agenda with orienting questions. As a single unrun study it reads as "preliminary
research", which is a listed ground for rejection. Terveen and McDonald has not been read on this
project and should be before the paper is structured against it.

**Changes the current draft needs for this journal.**

1. **The introduction must become continuous prose.** The Google Doc's introduction has five labelled
   subsections and three bullet lists. No recent introduction in the journal has either. The five-Cs
   order can stay as the argument underneath.
2. **A background section on HCI's own transparency literature is missing.** The outline cites
   mechanism design, law and history and very little from CHI, CSCW, TOCHI or this journal. The card
   library already holds verified cards for much of it: Jhaver et al., Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al., Lukoff
   et al., Buçinca et al., Vasconcelos et al., Cabrera et al., Ehsan et al., Alfrink et al., Lyons et
   al., Crisan et al., Harper et al., Bansal et al. Eslami et al. and Rader et al. are in the
   bibliography without cards.
3. **Engage Özçetin et al. (2023) directly.** It proposes making platform terms "legible" through
   "revealing design", in this journal. The paper's thesis is a reply to it.
4. **Every framework section should end in a stated implication for design.** That is criterion (a) in
   the journal's test, and Özçetin et al. show the form.
5. **The abstract should be one paragraph of at most 200 words.** The current one is two paragraphs.
6. **American spelling and double quotation marks.**
7. **Say what kind of paper it is.** Crabtree and Dix each state the genre and its limits in the
   introduction.

**Risks.**

- The guidelines say "every paper should have empirical evidence that validates its results". A
  framework paper meets this through the evidence it reviews; the reviewed evidence on Convention B is
  thin and none of it is from a triad.
- A 2% acceptance rate and a desk screen that rejects most submissions.
- Prior posting: Part 1 of the DXD series is on Substack and this repo is public. The overlap must be
  declared, and the guideline on withdrawing open-access postings should be raised with the editor.

**Recommended first step: send the editor an Article Proposal.** The guidelines offer it for a
submission with "unusual aspects", it takes an abstract and short answers, and the journal's first
decisions come in about twelve days. It tests scope before the paper is written.
