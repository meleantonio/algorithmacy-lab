## What it argues
"Despite the shortcomings of human control over complex technical systems, human oversight is advocated as a solution against the risks of increasing reliance on algorithmic tools. For law, human oversight provides an attractive, easily implementable and observable procedural safeguard. However, without awareness of its inherent limitations, human oversight is in danger of becoming a value in itself, an empty procedural shell used as a stand-in justification for algorithmization" (abstract). The argument runs in four steps. Algorithmisation is "law's mirror": it makes visible that "humans are the implicit medium of law" (Introduction), and the risk is "throwing the baby out with the bath water" by automating human tasks without knowing which procedural redundancies create "a space for discretion" (Algorithmisation as law's mirror, citing Rouvroy). EU policy puts oversight at the centre: the White Paper's four forms are review before the output takes effect, review after, monitoring with "a stop button or procedure", and design constraints (Human oversight in EU regulation and policy). The GDPR's WP29 guidelines require oversight that is "meaningful in the sense that the overseer should have the authority and competence to change the decision" (same section).

The third section is the critique. "For various reasons, from boredom at routine monitoring to automation bias and alert fatigue, humans generally perform badly as supervisors of automated technical systems"; Elish's "moral crumple zone" misattributes responsibility to the human with limited control; Jasanoff's "human pretensions of control" are performative (Human oversight and the (false) promise of control). Yet oversight is easy to adopt because "through digital traces and metadata we can easily ascertain that the oversight requirement has been fulfilled, that a human user has indeed been in the loop, on the loop or in command, to have pressed the button, or ticked the box" (same section). Bainbridge's ironies are read into legal work: automation "makes their work more difficult by removing the routine tasks and allocating the harder monitoring and take-over tasks to them" (Decreasing human discretion). Algorithmic decision-making is "non-decision making": "the decision making is moved upstream, to the system architects", away from street-level bureaucrats (same section). The author asks "to which extent improved interaction design can mitigate the loss of discretion", leaving it open (same section). The conclusion: oversight "faces the risk of becoming a value in itself, turning human decision-makers into rubberstamps, providing a sound procedural mechanism that can easily be verified after it has been conducted yet being empty of meaning", and "oversight" carries a double meaning, the act of overseeing and the mistake (Conclusion).

## Relation to the argument
This is the legal-theory statement of what the abstract's second sentence calls checking by reading. Koulu's point that oversight is attractive because it leaves a record ("pressed the button, or ticked the box") is the argument-map's P13 turned around: Nadella's "tamper-proof human readable evidence" (`nadella2026models`, P15) is what makes oversight verifiable, and Koulu says that verifiability is why oversight is adopted even where it is empty. That supports the abstract and sharpens it: the record serves the organisation's proof of process, not the person's capacity to judge.

The paper also marks a limit the abstract must respect. Koulu's alternative is discretion and the human encounter, a remedy on the side of the human decision-maker, and she only poses the question whether interaction design can help. The abstract's "smoking gun" design change is an answer to the question she leaves open, not something her analysis supports. On parties, her structure is decision-maker, system and subject, with the subject's right to be heard carried by a human encounter; it is not two counterparts coordinating through a mediator, so it supports only the narrower dyad claim: the oversight act is one person and one system.
