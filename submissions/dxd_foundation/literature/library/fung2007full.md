## What it argues

Fung, Graham and Weil argue that mandated disclosure works only when its information enters the existing decisions of both the people who receive it and the organisations that must publish it. They model this as an "action cycle" (Figure 4.1, p. 54): users take in information, change what they do, and disclosers notice the changed choices and change their own practices. A policy "has effects" when users act on the information. It is "effective, however, only when discloser responses significantly advance policy aims" (p. 54).

The first half of the condition is user embeddedness. When new information "becomes part of users' decision-making routines, we say that it is embedded in user decisions", and that is "necessary but not sufficient" for effectiveness (p. 55). Three factors govern it: perceived value, compatibility with users' routines (format, and time and place), and comprehensibility (pp. 55–59). Their running example is the letter grade in a Los Angeles restaurant window, which wins on format and on being present "when and where they make dining decisions" (pp. 56–57). The comprehension failure is workplace chemical data sheets, of which "workers were able to grasp only about 60 percent" in one study (p. 59).

The second half is discloser embeddedness, through the same three factors applied to how users' responses reach the organisation (pp. 65–68). "Highly effective transparency policies, then, are doubly embedded" (p. 65). Two obstacles can still defeat an embedded system: goal conflict between policymakers and users or disclosers, and misreading through cognitive bias (p. 71).

The evaluation of eight policies (pp. 74–89; summary on p. 77) bears this out in the authors' reading: the highly effective policies "embedded information strongly into the decision-making processes of both users and disclosers", the moderately effective ones embedded it for "select groups", and the "Ineffective policies failed to embed information into the calculations of either disclosers or users" (p. 77). Two of the eight were ineffective: patient-safety disclosure and plant-closing notices (p. 77). The conclusion repeats the rule, "Transparency policies are likely to be effective when the new information they generate can be easily embedded into the routines of information users and when information disclosers, in turn, embed users' changed choices" (pp. 173–174). The first of ten design principles is that "The most important condition for transparency effectiveness is that new information become embedded in the decision routines of information users" (p. 177).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D3 and premise P8.** Fung et al. are on the disclosure side, but their condition is a condition about routines, not about reading. Information works when it arrives in the format, place and moment of an existing decision. The restaurant grade (pp. 56–57) is information compressed into a signal at the point of choice. That is close to the draft's Target 3 and to the paper's interest in conventions that remove reading from the decision. The book supports the draft's diagnosis that complex textual disclosure (Target 4) underperforms, but more weakly than "fails": workplace hazardous-chemicals disclosure (the data sheets) and toxic pollution reporting are rated *moderately* effective, not ineffective (p. 77), and their limits are put down to complexity of the information and the lack of a simple metric (pp. 59, 174). The two policies the book rates ineffective are patient-safety disclosure and plant-closure and layoff notification (p. 77).

**Ruling D8 and premise P12.** The double-embeddedness condition puts the discloser's response at the centre. Disclosure helps people chiefly when the discloser changes its practice because users' choices reach its goals (pp. 65–66). On a platform where the discloser is also the mediator and controls the interface, that loop is the thing in question. Fung et al. note that disclosers act only on responses that "substantially affect disclosers' interests" (p. 66). That supports P12's worry and does not resolve it.

**What it complicates.** The book is not a critique of disclosure as such, unlike Ben-Shahar and Schneider. It argues for better-designed transparency. Ch. 7 ("collaborative transparency", user-generated information through technology) was not read and may cut toward platform disclosure.
