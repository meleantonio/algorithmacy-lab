## What it argues
Butskhrikidze asks which languages can be written without word spaces and still read fast and clearly. Her answer is typological: languages that are "predominantly isolating/analytic or agglutinative constructions with tones and limited combinatory rules (restricted phonotactic constraints)" suit *scriptio continua* better than inflectional languages "with complex phonotactic rules and free stress" (Abstract, p. 821). The reasoning rests on her earlier work on "word boundary markers", sound patterns that signal where a word ends: tone, fixed stress, consonant clusters, vowel harmony, final devoicing. Isolating languages need few such markers; inflectional ones need many (Section 2, Table 3, pp. 823–825). She makes the unusual claim that lexical tone is itself a boundary marker (p. 823).

She then reads the three languages. Burmese is isolating with some agglutination, has tones, mostly one-syllable morphemes and strict limits on consonant and vowel sequences, which "facilitate unambiguous chunking of continuous strings" (Section 4.5, p. 828). Thai is isolating, with five tones plus restricted syllable-initial and syllable-final consonants (Section 5, pp. 829–831). She quotes Winskel (2011) that tone marks in Thai may be "functioning as salient word or syllable segmentation cues" because the script has no spaces (p. 831). Lhasa Tibetan is agglutinative, with tone and vowel harmony as markers (Section 6, pp. 832–835). Her summary table (Table 15, p. 835) and conclusion: in all three, "the absence of spaces between the words is compensated by the presence of word boundary markers" (p. 836). She also notes that Burmese writers generally put spaces after clauses "to enhance comprehension" (Section 4.2, p. 826). Her discussion weighs and sets aside two alternatives, historical inertia and Chinese cultural influence, and rejects the view that the common Indic descent explains the pattern by pointing to Sanskrit, an inflectional language in the same script family, which is not written continuously (Section 7, p. 835).

## Relation to the argument
This is not a source for the history or the analogy; it is the "scriptio continua" paper a reviewer might meet in a search, and it uses the term for living scripts. Reading it whole shows three points of contact.

First, it bears on P4 (readers fluent in an unspaced script gain nothing from spaces). Butskhrikidze gives a language-side reason why fluent readers of Thai, Burmese or Tibetan may not need spaces: the spoken language already marks the boundaries. This is a hypothesis, not a reading experiment; she tests no reader.

Second, it gives a possible reason, again untested, why Latin was a poor fit for unspaced writing. She grants that Latin and Greek were written continuously and are inflectional (nn. 3 and 12, pp. 822, 836), which on her own typology makes them the less suitable case. That is an extension by the card, not her claim. It would complicate any suggestion that the Latin scribes' problem was only one of training (D2) by adding a linguistic cost that sits in the language and not the reader. The paper should not state this as established.

Third, footnote 3 reports Saenger (1997) as arguing that writing without spaces was practised in England, the Low Countries, Germany and France "up until the Middle Ages", with its decline attributed "to the development of silent reading". That is her short paraphrase, not a check of Saenger, and it sits close to the claim the argument map has dropped (premise P1 and the "silent reading" row), so the paper must not take Saenger's position from here.

## Caution
Three languages picked by the author, desk-study evidence only, and the author's own "tentative conclusions"; she acknowledges the sample "might not be representative enough" (n. 5, p. 822). The paper says Chinese, Japanese and Lao also "meet the predictions" but omits them (p. 836), so that claim is unchecked here. The central claim about tone as a boundary marker is her own and she says she knows of no earlier statement (p. 823). Year: the PDF prints 2021 (vol. 72, no. 3); the bib and DOI say 2022. Check which volume the journal assigns before citing a year.
