## What it argues

In the black-box treatment subjects were told only that the black box "would convert their input (in coins) to an output (in coins) via a mathematical function with a random component" (p. 4); they were not told other people existed. The paper tests five "payoff-based" learning behaviours drawn from theory: asymmetric inertia, asymmetric volatility, asymmetric breadth, reversion and directional bias (p. 2). All five hold in the pure black box (eight sessions, 4,960 observations; p. 4). A payoff decrease "reduces inertia (i.e. increases search probability) by 12% (± 2%)" (p. 5). Table A1 (p. 8): probability of no adjustment 0.23 after a payoff decrease against 0.33 after steady or rising payoffs; mean absolute adjustment 11.4 against 9.2; standard deviation of adjustment 15.6 against 13.6; reversion 0.21 against 0.08.

The behaviours persist when information is added: "none of the hypotheses can be rejected" in the standard and enhanced treatments (p. 3). Two remarks matter for this facet. Under the high rate of return, where full contribution is dominant, "providing more information can actually lead to decreasing play of the dominant strategy … some subjects continue to act as if they were inside a black box even though they are not" (p. 7). And convergence slowed "especially under black box," which the authors attribute to the "payoff variability effect," by which "increased payoff variability may inhibit learning" (p. 7, with Fig. 4 p. 13 showing the realised payoff spread by contribution level).

## Relation to the argument

**Q4, what "ecological" feedback means when the environment is other people.** The black box is the limiting case of a triad with an opaque mediator: the person sees only what comes back. People do learn there, by a search rule keyed to whether the last payoff rose or fell (p. 5). The same rule governs behaviour when the game and the others are shown (p. 7). This supports a narrow version of the author's D7: people adjust by acting and reading the return even with no account of the mechanism. It also sets the ceiling. In the high-return game the return was noisy because other players were exploring, and many subjects did not find the dominant strategy in 20 periods (p. 7).

**P8, disclosure.** "Providing more information can actually lead to decreasing play of the dominant strategy" (p. 7) is a within-study case where adding a description of the mechanism did not improve, and in one condition worsened, convergence. It is weak evidence (an aggregate pattern, not a test of disclosure as such), but it is on the author's side of P8.

**Exposure 3 of the argument map.** The variance that hampered learning came from the counterparts' simultaneous search (Fig. 4, p. 13). In a platform that variance is compounded by the mediator's own optimisation, which the person also cannot see. The paper gives a template for measuring the effect and no measurement of it.

## Caution

Students in Oxford, groups of four, 20-period phases; the instructions did not say others were present, so the black box is a device, not a platform. Statistical tests are on adjustment behaviour, with session, phase, group, period and individual fixed effects; effect sizes are modest (a 10-point difference in inertia rates). The data were collected for another paper (Burton-Chellew & West 2013) and re-analysed here. The remark on "more information" (p. 7) is interpretive and the authors leave it "to future research."
