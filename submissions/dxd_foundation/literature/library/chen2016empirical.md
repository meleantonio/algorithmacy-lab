## What it argues

The paper audits how Amazon's marketplace chooses the "default" seller and who prices by algorithm. The Buy Box "determines, for a given product being sold by many sellers, which of the sellers will be featured" (pdf p. 1); "82% of sales on Amazon go through the Buy Box" (pdf p. 2, repeated p. 3, citing a trade-press figure); "The precise features and weights used by the Buy Box algorithm are unknown" (pdf pp. 1–2). Formally, "the Buy Box algorithm is a function B(P) → p_i" over the sellers' prices (pdf p. 3), but price is not the whole of it: "only 60% of the top-ranked sellers win the Buy Box" (pdf p. 5). A random-forest classifier predicts the winner with "75–85% accuracy even in the most challenging cases" (pdf p. 6) against 50–60% for price-only baselines (pdf p. 6). Feature weights (Table 1, pdf p. 6): price difference to the lowest 0.36, price ratio 0.33, positive feedback 0.10, "Is Amazon the Seller?" 0.10, feedback count 0.06, average rating 0.03, FBA 0.02. The authors caution that Amazon's advantage "does not necessarily mean that Amazon has tilted the Buy Box algorithm in their favor," since unmeasured features such as sales volume may load on that indicator (pdf p. 6).

On algorithmic pricing: with a change threshold of 20, they detect 543 sellers, "2.4% of all sellers in our dataset," but "38% of all sellers that have ≥20 changes" (Table 2, pdf p. 6; text pdf p. 7); 70% of sellers matching the lowest price sit within $1 of it (pdf p. 7), and some sellers price "15–30% above Amazon's price" (pdf p. 8); Amazon itself "appears to be employing algorithmic pricing" (Figs. 16–17, pdf p. 8). Algorithmic sellers list fewer products, stay longer (median lifetime 30 days against 15, pdf p. 8), receive far more feedback, and "are more likely to win the Buy Box at all ranks except for the top one" (pdf p. 10). Products with algorithmic sellers see many more Buy Box changes (2% with zero price changes against 20%, pdf p. 10). Conclusion: "The Buy Box algorithm exacerbates the disparity between algorithmic and non-algorithmic sellers, as it creates a largely winner-take-all marketplace," and the result "suggests an arms race" (pdf p. 10).

## Relation to the argument

**Question 4 (a mediator that binds and optimizes: Amazon Buy Box).** The best-documented case in the library. The mediator *binds* by choosing the default seller for most sales; it *optimizes* by an undocumented function of price, feedback and its own status; and the sellers' response is exactly the abstract's "gaming the mediator": repricing bots tuned to the lowest price, the second-lowest or Amazon's price (pdf pp. 6–7). The buyer, the other counterpart, sees only the chosen offer.

**P8 (disclosure).** Amazon "has released some information about the features used" (pdf p. 3), and the audit still had to reverse-engineer the weights. This fits the author's claim that disclosure does not give the party what it needs; what sellers acted on was behaviour (observed prices and wins), not text.

**Where it cuts against the author.** The parties who game are sellers; the buyer's task is unchanged, and the authors can only speculate on buyer harm ("it is not clear what the impact of dynamic pricing is on customers," pdf p. 10). The triad here is asymmetric: one counterpart games, the other reads a price.

## Caution

Data from 2014–2015, crawled from browsers not logged in to Prime (pdf p. 4); best-seller bias by design. Buy Box features such as sales volume and shipping time were unobservable. Detection of algorithmic sellers is by correlation and change counts, with no ground truth. The sales-share figure (82%) is a trade-press number.
