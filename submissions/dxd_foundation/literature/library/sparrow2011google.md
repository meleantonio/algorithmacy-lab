## What it argues

The report tests whether the Internet has become "a primary form of external or transactive memory" (abstract, p. 776). Experiment 1: after a block of hard trivia questions, computer words slowed colour naming more than general words, RT M = 712 ms (SD 413) against 591 ms (SD 204), t(68) = 3.26, P < 0.003 (p. 776). Experiment 2: participants typed 40 trivia statements; half believed the computer saved them. Recall was higher when they believed the statements erased (Erase M = 0.31, Erase-Remember M = 0.29) than saved (Save M = 0.22, Save-Remember M = 0.19), omnibus F(3, 56) = 2.80, P < 0.05; the instruction to remember had no significant effect (p. 777). Experiment 3: recognition of exact wording was best for statements believed erased (M = 0.93) against saved generically (0.88) or to a folder (0.85), F(1, 27) = 4.01, P < 0.03, while memory for whether a statement had been saved was better for saved items (0.61, 0.66) than erased (0.51) (p. 777). Experiment 4: participants recalled the folder where a statement was kept (M = 0.49) better than the statement itself (M = 0.23), t(31) = 6.70, P < 0.001; the if/then breakdown was 0.38 recalled neither, 0.30 folder only, 0.17 both, 0.11 statement only (p. 778). The authors note that folder recall was cued and statement recall was not, and that the two could not be counterbalanced (p. 778). Their conclusion: people are "growing into interconnected systems that remember less by knowing information than by knowing where the information can be found" (p. 778).

## Relation to the argument

**Costs of offloading (exposure "lowering a cost has costs").** This is the first and most-cited demonstration that expecting access to an external store lowers recall of the content. For the abstract's sentence it bears on the word "internalize": if offloading to a page works as offloading to a file does, the representational change should reduce what readers hold in memory, not increase it. The paper measures recall of facts, not reading skill, so it cuts against the abstract only if "internalize text" means remember what was read; if it means read silently and fluently, the paper is silent.

**D3 and P13.** The folder result (Exp. 4) is relevant to any design that gives a person a record to consult: people learn where, not what. An audit log read on demand would, on this evidence, be remembered as a place rather than as content.

**Limits.** The experiments involve a person and a passive file. No counterpart, no mediator with interests, no feedback on decisions.
