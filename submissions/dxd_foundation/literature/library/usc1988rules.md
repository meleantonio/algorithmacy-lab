## What it argues

The four sections set up a layered process for making federal procedural rules.

- **§ 2072 (the grant).** "The Supreme Court shall have the power to prescribe general rules of practice and procedure and rules of evidence" for the district courts and courts of appeals (§ 2072(a)). Such rules "shall not abridge, enlarge or modify any substantive right" (§ 2072(b)).
- **§ 2073 (the method).** The Judicial Conference prescribes and publishes its procedures (§ 2073(a)(1)) and may appoint committees "of members of the bench and the professional bar, and trial and appellate judges" to recommend rules (§ 2073(a)(2)); a standing committee reviews their recommendations (§ 2073(b)). Each committee meeting "shall be open to the public," unless closed by a majority in open session with reasons stated, and minutes are made public (§ 2073(c)(1)). Each meeting "shall be preceded by sufficient notice to enable all interested persons to attend" (§ 2073(c)(2)). A recommendation comes with a proposed rule, an explanatory note and a written report including minority views (§ 2073(d)). But "Failure to comply with this section does not invalidate a rule" (§ 2073(e)).
- **§ 2074 (Congress).** The Supreme Court transmits a proposed rule to Congress by 1 May; it takes effect no earlier than 1 December of that year (§ 2074(a)). A rule touching an evidentiary privilege needs an Act of Congress (§ 2074(b)).
- **§ 2071 (local rules).** "The Supreme Court and all courts established by Act of Congress" may prescribe rules for the conduct of their business, consistent with Acts of Congress and § 2072 rules (§ 2071(a)); a rule by any court other than the Supreme Court is prescribed "only after giving appropriate public notice and an opportunity for comment" (§ 2071(b)); district-court rules can be modified or abrogated by the circuit judicial council (§ 2071(c)). Where a court finds "an immediate need," it may act first and give notice and comment afterwards (§ 2071(e)).

Statutory history (LII notes): the current §§ 2072–2074 were enacted by title IV of Pub. L. 100–702 (Nov. 19, 1988), effective 1 December 1988, which also repealed the former § 2072 (LII note under § 2071); the 1934 Act had no § 2073 (an earlier, unrelated § 2073 on admiralty rules ran from 1948 until its repeal in 1966, LII prior-provisions note). S2 addition from the Statutes at Large (govinfo, 102 Stat. 4642 ff.): Pub. L. 100–702 is the "Judicial Improvements and Access to Justice Act" (§ 1), and its title IV, which enacted §§ 2072–2074, is itself headed "TITLE IV—RULES ENABLING ACT" ("Sec. 401. Rules Enabling Act amendments"). § 2073 begins on 102 Stat. 4649.

## Relation to the argument

**P12 and ruling D8.** The provisions support the draft's courts comparator: the convention (procedural rules) is made by a body other than the one applying it to a case, through public, noticed meetings, with written reasons and a delay before rules take effect, and with Congress able to intervene. That is the opposite of the platform pattern in Rahman (2019) and P2B Art. 3, where the interface owner changes the rules alone. It gives D8 a concrete picture of where "the convention bends."

**What complicates it.** Two provisions weaken "insulated." § 2073(e) says breach of the open-meeting and notice duties does not invalidate a rule. § 2071(e) lets a court make a local rule first and take comment afterwards when it finds an immediate need. Neither lets a judge change rules mid-trial for one side, but both show that the insulation is procedural and partly unenforceable, not absolute.
