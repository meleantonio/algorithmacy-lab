# Quandt, M., & Freitag, M. (2021). A systematic review of user acceptance in industrial augmented reality. *Frontiers in Education, 6*, Article 700760. https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2021.700760

**Identifier:** doi:10.3389/feduc.2021.700760 · **Read depth:** full_text (the publisher's open-access PDF, CC BY, 17 pages with the article's own pagination 1–17; local copy dxd_foundation_local/pdfs/S/quandt2021.pdf; whole text, Tables 1–4 and reference list read; the supplementary tables were not opened) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed systematic review · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** systematic review (Booth et al. method, PICOC) of Scopus, Web of Science, ACM, IEEE Xplore and ScienceDirect, 2011–2020, searched 8 December 2020; 504 titles, 109 screened on introduction and conclusion, 33 analysed, of which 28 had user studies · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (an industrial worker and an AR system; the robot or machine behind the display is not a party) · **Facets:** S · **Read:** 2026-10-15

## What it argues
Industrial AR is framed as information delivery: "In the industrial work context, Augmented Reality (AR) can support work processes and employees' cognitive relief through the location-specific and context-related superimposition of real objects with virtual information" (abstract); "Compared to the paper-based provision of information in the work environment, AR can reduce employees' cognitive load by providing context-sensitive virtual information" (p. 2). Despite the number of prototypes, "there is no widespread use of these solutions in industrial practice" (abstract, p. 1). Earlier reviews found formal usability studies in "less than 10% of all papers" (Dey et al. 2018, p. 3) and user studies in 18 of 67 technical papers (Bottani and Vignali 2019, p. 11).

Of the 33 papers, 8 are maintenance, 8 training, 5 assembly, 2 order picking and 10 other (Table 3, p. 9); 11 used handheld devices, 14 head-mounted displays, 3 spatial projection and 2 screens (p. 9). Nineteen papers measured acceptance inside performance-oriented user studies, mostly with Davis's TAM constructs, perceived usefulness and ease of use; eight discussed acceptance without a study; six adapted acceptance models (pp. 10–12). By product-lifecycle phase, 20 studies were prototype tests in the realization phase, 8 requirements studies, 1 in use (Table 4, p. 12). Reported findings are mixed: Rapaccini et al. found intention to use depended on usefulness more than ease of use; Loch et al. found ease of use and enjoyment mattered; Terhoeven et al. found older employees expected less usefulness (pp. 12–13). Hardware ergonomics, discomfort and narrow field of view recur as limits (p. 13). "Despite the predominant high acceptance of the tested AR systems by users, there is no widespread adoption of AR technology in the industrial application fields" (p. 14), and "studies generally do not continue after a user study has been conducted to test a prototype" (p. 14), which the authors attribute to short hardware cycles. No standard procedure for acceptance exists (p. 15).

## Relation to the argument
A supporting but modest source for the abstract's first and second sentences. It documents that the industrial XR literature defines the headset's job as presenting "context-sensitive virtual information" so that the worker is relieved of load, which is the read-to-work model the abstract names; and that the field measures acceptance of being given information, not what the worker can decide. The parties are a worker and a display. It says nothing about checking a machine, robots as agents or mediators, and its evidence is prototypes in the laboratory, so the abstract should not lean on it for more than the framing.

## Caution
A review of acceptance, not performance; 33 papers; search limited to title, abstract and keywords in five databases; the authors note their own subjectivity in screening (p. 7). Published in an education journal. Do not cite it for effects of AR on error or time, which it reports only as other studies' secondary measures.
