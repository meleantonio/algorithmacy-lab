## What it argues
Li asks "what makes a mechanism simple? When is it easy for participants to see that a mechanism is incentive-compatible?" (p. 2). He first separates two readings of a mechanism: as a metaphor for decentralised dealings, where it "does not matter whether participants understand that the mechanism is incentive-compatible", and as the literal rules of a formal process, where it does (pp. 2–3). Three reasons it matters: novel designs are unfamiliar or "deceptively familiar"; some people play once and cannot learn; and participants may distrust the designer's claim, sometimes rightly—the NRMP once "(mistakenly) claimed" its algorithm was incentive-compatible, and Google "incorrectly claimed that its auction format was strategy-proof" (pp. 2–3, citing Williams 1995 and Edelman et al. 2007).

He then lists the field record of misplay under strategy-proof rules: 17 percent of NRMP applicants surveyed said they submitted a non-truthful list (Rees-Jones 2018); 23 percent did so in an incentivised replica (Rees-Jones and Skowronek 2018); and applicants in Israel and Hungary "rank a program without a scholarship above the same program with a scholarship" (p. 3). "Consequently, it is often not enough that a mechanism is theoretically incentive-compatible. Participants have to see for themselves that it is incentive-compatible; the mechanism has to be simple. Simplicity bypasses the need for participants to trust the mechanism designer" (p. 3).

Three challenges structure the essay (p. 4). (1) *Contingent reasoning about others' unobserved moves*: the second-price auction needs case-by-case comparison, the ascending auction does not; Kagel et al. (1987) found clearing prices at the second-highest value in 76 percent of ascending auctions against "no more than 20 percent" of second-price auctions, a result "well-replicated" (pp. 5–6). Obvious dominance formalises this (p. 6), and OSP mechanisms are those where "truth-telling can be seen to be dominant from a certain kind of partial description" that omits how the outcome depends on opponents' moves (p. 8). Dynamic random priority gave the dominant-strategy outcome in 93 percent of games against 64 percent for the static version (p. 9, citing Li 2017, p. 3282). (2) *Planning one's own future moves*: chess shows OSP is not enough—White's win-forcing strategy is obviously dominant yet not simple; Pycia and Troyan's partial strategic plans and one-step simplicity address this (pp. 9–11). (3) *Reasoning about others' beliefs*: Börgers and Li's strategically simple mechanisms (pp. 12–14).

Two caveats he states himself. Footnote 5: Breitmoser and Schweighofer-Kodritsch (2022) found subjects bid more truthfully in second-price auctions "when the mechanism is framed in a way that makes the isomorphism salient. But why does the ascending auction framing make it easier to see the dominant strategy?" (pp. 5–6). Footnote 8: the 93 against 64 percent gap "might be due to loss aversion rather than strategic mistakes" (p. 9, citing Dreyfuss et al. 2022 and Meisner and von Wangenheim 2023). The conclusion calls for experiments and says a further direction is "to study how to describe and explain mechanisms", noting that the Israeli match "even provided participants with a general-audience lecture demonstrating that its mechanism was strategy-proof, but some participants were clearly not convinced" (p. 15).

## Relation to the argument
This is the clearest statement by the originator of OSP of what the author's phrase "strategic offloading (Li, 2017)" would have to mean: a rule is simple when the person can see it is safe to be truthful without doing the contingent reasoning. Li never uses "offloading"; his vocabulary is "simplicity", "cognitive cost of participation" and "bypasses the need for participants to trust" (p. 3). The essay supports the abstract's move in a narrower form: the mechanism can relieve the person of strategic work only if the person can see that it has. It cuts against a stronger reading in two places. First, Li now lists three separate kinds of strategic burden, and OSP removes only the first; a mechanism can be OSP and still require long forward planning (the chess example, p. 9). Second, the Breitmoser footnote concedes that the evidence Li 2017 used to support OSP is confounded by presentation. For P10 (a rule in the mediator that removes a strategic task), the essay is a source for the claim that the rule's form, not only its incentive property, decides whether the task is removed for the person. For D3 (disclosure as a literacy-side intervention), Li's trust point is relevant: simplicity is offered as a substitute for believing the designer's description. Note that every mechanism he discusses has a neutral designer; the "mediator that binds and optimizes" in the author's triad has no analogue here.

## Caution
A preprint "preliminary draft"; quotations should be checked against JEP 38(4) before use. It is a selective essay "shaped by my idiosyncratic tastes" (p. 4), not a systematic review. It reports no new data; every number is a citation of another study. It does not discuss platforms, two-sided coordination through an interested intermediary, or any notion of offloading.
