## What it argues
Barrett and colleagues, who work on IIT and are broadly sympathetic to it, set out what they take to be its achievements, two common misreadings, and three problems (§§5.1–5.3; the abstract counts five "points": the two misreadings and the three problems). The summary below takes the problems in four steps because §5.1 has two subsections. Achievements: IIT inspired the perturbational complexity index (PCI), which separates conscious from unconscious states in TMS–EEG studies (§3.1), and it links grid and chain architectures to the felt structure of space and time (§3.2). Misreadings: high Φ is not simply "more consciousness" (§4.1), and IIT's panpsychism is not specially problematic (§4.2).

The problems are the part this paper uses. First (§5.1.1), the IIT algorithm is not well defined whenever a graining of the system has memory: "The IIT algorithm is therefore not well-defined whenever there is at least one non-Markovian graining" (§5.1.1, p. 11). Second (§5.1.2), "The IIT algorithm has not been computed on any realistic model of a physical system"; it has been run only on toy systems of binary units with memoryless dynamics (§5.1.2, p. 12). It also cannot take continuous state variables, because its maximum-entropy prior is undefined for them (§5.1.2). Third (§5.2), empirical measures are proxies, not approximations: an approximation needs a bound on the discrepancy, and "one cannot approximately compute Φ from neuroimaging data" (§5.2, p. 13). Proxy measures "typically take into consideration functional segregation and/or integration" and so stand for at most two of IIT's six axioms (§5.2). Candidate empirical Φ measures "often" move "in different directions under the same change of parameters", so "validation with models is important" (§5.2, citing Mediano et al. 2019). Fourth (§5.3), IIT's discrete formalism does not fit field-based physics (§5.3). Box 2 recommends saying plainly that empirical work uses "proxy Φ measures".

The output of the algorithm also depends on three "grainings": how the system is divided into components, the time step, and how component states are defined (§2, p. 4).

## Relation to the argument
**Ruling D9 and premise P11.** The author rules that "we use IIT and phi to gauge the irreducibiltiy of any given triad" (sic). This preprint, written from inside the IIT community, sets three conditions that any use of Φ on a triad has to answer, and the paper is stronger for answering them in advance. (1) Behaviour in a human triad, coded into discrete states at some time step, is very likely non-Markovian under any natural coding; on §5.1.1 the IIT algorithm is then not defined. (2) Anything computed from observed behaviour would be a proxy in the §5.2 sense, not Φ, and should be called that. (3) The three grainings in §2 are exactly the choices the draft says it will fix in advance (coding, lag, partition). The paper treats them as determining the answer, which supports pre-registering them and cuts against reading any one value as "the" irreducibility of a triad.

What it supports: validating a measure on small binary Markov models, where the algorithm can be run, matches where Barrett et al. say it has been run (§5.1.2). Their own interest is consciousness; they say nothing about groups, organisations or triads, so the extension is the lab's, not theirs.

## Caution
A preprint, version 1, not yet peer reviewed. It is a position piece by IIT's sympathetic critics; its claims about what has and has not been computed are their survey, not a systematic review. It concerns IIT as a theory of consciousness. It cannot be cited for anything about social systems, and nothing in it endorses or rejects using Φ as a measure of a triad's irreducibility.
