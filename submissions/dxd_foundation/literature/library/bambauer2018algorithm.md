## What it argues
Gaming is "a change in the subject's behavior in order to affect the algorithm's estimate without causing any change to the key characteristic that the algorithm is attempting to measure" (p. 10). Four tactics: avoidance, altered conduct, altered input, obfuscation (pp. 12–13). Designers' countermoves: more complexity, randomness, frequent model changes, immutable proxies, more data sources, and "reduce the transparency of their model so that gamers do not have as much information" (p. 15).

Example 4 is the platform case. Firms game intermediaries' scores by astroturfing and by contract terms that forbid negative reviews; "the intermediaries have caught on" and "cloak their ranking models to reduce transparency and hinder gaming", add complexity, and police reputation-management firms, borrowing from Google's treatment of link farms (pp. 21–22). The authors note that "the commercial firm games an algorithm used by consumers rather than the other way around" (p. 21).

Part II weighs four values. Gaming serves autonomy (pp. 23–25) but usually raises error (p. 25), favours "the wealthy and educated" who have the information and time to game (p. 29), and carries "third-order inefficiencies—the costs of the system itself", including "the persistent worry and vigilance required to take as much advantage as possible from an algorithm" (p. 32). Part III: the EU Data Protection Directive's transparency measure "promotes control and enables gaming, too" (p. 36); the FCRA's disclosure of the four leading adverse factors "can clearly be subsequently used to game the system" (p. 44); Section 230 and the First Amendment protect intermediaries "from demands to disclose their inner workings, giving them the opacity to vigorously engage in these countergaming strategies" (p. 45). Part III.C, "Laws Eliminating the Need for Gaming", shows that compelled pooling (the ACA, the CARD Act) "renders gaming efforts by card holders unnecessary" (p. 47).

## Relation to the argument
**P8 / D3.** The clearest legal statement that disclosure and gaming are two sides of one coin: transparency rules "both enable gamers to manipulate the system and hamper the firm's ability to use countergaming measures" (p. 35). That supports the abstract's sentence, with a twist: disclosure does not spare the person from gaming because it is what makes gaming possible, and the platform answers with opacity.

**P10 and the "smoking gun".** Part III.C is the design move the author is looking for, in legal form: change the rule so that the strategic task disappears ("pooling ... has rendered moot both the ... interests in personal information to discriminate ... and ... interests in gaming", p. 46). It is the first candidate convention in P10, achieved by law rather than interface.

**Coordination cost (Q3).** The third-order inefficiency passage (p. 32) is the nearest statement to "gaming is a cost borne to reach the other party", but the authors frame it as social waste, not as a cost of coordination.

## Caution
A US-centred legal taxonomy with illustrative, not empirical, examples; the authors "make only modest policy recommendations" (p. 5). The platform material is anecdotal (news reports in footnotes 83–89). Do not cite it for the size of any effect.
