## What it argues

Sutherland and colleagues identify "gig literacies": the skills online freelancers develop to work with and around the platform that matches and evaluates them. They take a practice-centric view of literacy, "which examines work literacy as an ongoing process rather than a set of fixed outcomes or benchmarks" (p. 461). The findings are organised by literacy: building reputation, self-presentation, maintaining productivity, mitigating transaction risk and building relationships (pp. 463–469).

Learning the rating algorithm is central and is done partly by acting. Workers' Job Success Scores could fall for reasons beyond client ratings; "Some details on how ratings are calculated are buried in Upwork's documentation, and workers consulted more experienced workers on forums ... However, workers also learned to build ratings through trial and error" (p. 464). One freelancer found that his score fell after a good public rating and traced it to private client feedback that is not displayed; "By monitoring how their ratings changed in response to particular events, workers could figure out how to improve them" (p. 464). Workers broke projects into smaller jobs to accumulate ratings and gave refunds to avert bad ones (p. 464). The discussion generalises: workers develop these literacies "by engaging in situated practices or experimentation with the platform to make sense of its functions, or by exchanging ideas with other more experienced workers", and "learning the behaviour of largely opaque algorithms is a source of competitive advantage" (p. 470). The authors name the result "a platform literacy, which is the know-how required to leverage platform resources in order to minimize the precarity of independent work, while retaining as much autonomy as possible from those platform structures themselves" (p. 470).

## Relation to the argument

**The word-separation precedent and the literacy/algorithmacy split (§5.1).** This paper already uses "literacy" for the enacted, trial-and-error competence the draft calls algorithmacy. Its literacy is practice-based, situated and learned by experimenting with an opaque algorithm in a three-party marketplace. That narrows the draft's contrast at line 242, which sets algorithmacy against an "AI literacy" defined as conceptual knowledge. Part of the literacy literature does mean that; the gig-work literature does not. The draft's distinction can stand only if it is drawn against the conceptual strand by name and if the paper says how algorithmacy differs from "gig literacies" and "platform literacy".

**Ruling D7 and premise P9.** The paper supports the draft's claim that workers learn the mediator by acting and observing (P3's discovery of private feedback). It also shows that they read documentation and forums alongside acting, which qualifies "do not learn ... from company documentation" (line 266).

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** This paper is not cited in DRAFT_v2.md (grep "Sutherland", "Jarrahi", "gig literac": no hits). It cites Lee et al. (2015), which the draft cites at line 266. The lines below are where the draft makes claims this source bears on.
- Line 242 (algorithmacy distinct from "AI literacy", defined as conceptual knowledge): complicates. A practice-centric "gig literacy" / "platform literacy" covering trial-and-error learning of algorithms predates the draft (pp. 461, 470).
- Line 246 (algorithmacy lets an individual "infer hidden constraints, and protect their welfare across an algorithmic intermediary"): narrows the novelty. The paper's "platform literacy" (know-how to "minimize the precarity of independent work, while retaining as much autonomy as possible", p. 470) overlaps with the welfare clause. The inference of hidden rules is the literacy the paper describes on p. 464 and p. 470 ("learning the behaviour of largely opaque algorithms"), though it is not part of that definition.
- Line 266 (workers "do not learn dispatch and surge behavior from company documentation. They form working theories from what the system does in response to their own moves, and they trade those theories in online forums"): supports the second and third sentences (p. 464, p. 470); qualifies the first, since Upwork workers also dug through documentation (p. 464) as well as forums. The draft's sentence is about ride-hail drivers; this paper is about Upwork freelancers.
- Line 268 ("continuous behavioral experimentation"): supports (p. 464, p. 470).

## Caution

A qualitative study of 39 people on one platform, recruited through social media and personal sites; skilled freelancers with more autonomy than ride-hail or delivery workers, as the authors stress (p. 470). It describes literacies; it does not measure whether trial-and-error learning made workers' beliefs about the algorithm correct.
