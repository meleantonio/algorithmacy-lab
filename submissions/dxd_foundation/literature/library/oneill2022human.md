## What it argues
The review defines a human–autonomy team (HAT) as "interdependence in activity and outcomes involving one or more humans and one or more autonomous agents, wherein each human and autonomous agent is recognized as a unique team member occupying a distinct role on the team, and in which the members strive to achieve a common goal as a collective" (Delineating HATs). An agent counts as a teammate rather than a tool only with interdependence and perceived agency, which the authors tie to the levels of automation: levels 2–4 are "automation as a tool", levels 5–6 "partial autonomy" and 7–10 "high agent autonomy" (Table 1). The concept "is not new": Woods (1996) wrote that introducing intelligent agents "is a change in the team composition", and Malin et al. (1991) described "a joint human-intelligence system team" (HAT, as a concept, is not new).

Of the 76 studies, all were simulations, none field-based; 57 were military or emergency command-and-control tasks and 17 other; the CERTT-UAS-STE testbed supplied 18 articles (24%) and the MIX testbed 15 (20%) (Research Environments; Limitations). Team composition: 65 articles had a single agent and, of these, 45 paired the single agent with a single human; 22 had two or more humans; 7 had multiple agents; one had both (Team Composition). Autonomy: 26 articles at high and 31 at partial levels, 15 with several. Communication was text chat in 39, visual in 10, audio in 2, mixed in 18. Dependent variables: performance in 70 studies, workload 39, trust 24, situation awareness 23, coordination 15, shared mental models 6 (Table 2).

Themes (Table 3): higher agent autonomy "generally" improved outcomes, though in 11 studies higher autonomy "involved performing more activities or tasks"; transparency "has mixed effects", improving performance and trust but able to "lead to negative outcomes such as complacency, conflict, and workload", and "high transparency may create a level of complacency that leads to less vigilance in overseeing or questioning the autonomous agent's work"; reliability was uniformly positive; "human–human teams almost always outperformed HATs", partly because humans push information rather than wait to be asked; interdependence and training helped. The authors' own summary of the evidence base: generalizability "will be limited to action/execution tasks, short-term research environments with 'zero-history' teams, dyads, limited communication, and limited autonomy levels" (Base of scientific knowledge on HATs). They call for mediation analyses, longitudinal and field designs, and agents designed for roles "rather than just working under the direct control and teleoperation of the human team members" (General Discussion).

## Relation to the argument
For facet question 3 this is the field's own count of where the lineage admits several humans and machine agency. It cuts against a flat reading of the abstract's "assumes a dyad": 22 of 76 studies had more than one human, the agent must have "decision-making latitude" (the paper's phrase for what levels 2–4 lack) to count, and the lineage's own history names teams in 1991 and 1996. It supports a narrower form: 45 of 76 studies were one human and one agent, the authors describe the evidence as built on "dyads", and in every study the agent and the humans "strive to achieve a common goal". The mediator of the abstract, whose objective is its own and whose counterparts' objectives differ, is outside the definition of a HAT. So the lineage models multi-party teams but never an interested third party.

For the read-to-check sentence and facet question 4, the transparency theme is the relevant evidence: giving the human more of the agent's reasoning to read raised workload and in some studies complacency. That supports the abstract's doubt about reading as the way to check, inside the dyad, and the review's remedy is pushing behaviour and training, not a change of representation.

## Caution
Narrative, not quantitative; the search used "teamwork" as a required term and excluded physical robots and autonomous vehicles; two laboratories supplied 44% of the studies; all laboratory, mostly military simulations, mostly short-lived student teams; the authors say it "is also unclear whether humans necessarily perceived the autonomous agent(s) as a team member" (Limitations). Published 2020 online; nothing on language-model agents. Locators are section names because the XML carries no page numbers.
