## What it argues

Vallas and Schor sort the literature on platform work into four images, "entrepreneurial incubators, digital cages, accelerants of precarity, and chameleons" (abstract, 16.1), and propose a fifth: platforms as "permissive potentates that externalize responsibility and control over economic transactions while still exercising concentrated power" (16.1). The fifth image is triadic by construction. "In contrast to hierarchies (which centralize power), markets (which disperse it), or networks (which parcel it out to trusted collaborators), platforms exercise power over economic transactions by delegating control among the participants. They do so by establishing a digital infrastructure with which to govern the service triangle that links employers, workers, and customers (Leidner 1993). The effect allows the platform to play the role of tertius gaudens—'the third who rejoices'—as in Simmel's classic theory of the triad (in Wolff 1950)" (16.10).

What the platform keeps and what it cedes is listed: it "retains authority over important functions—the allocation of tasks, collection of data, pricing of services, and of course collection of revenues—but it cedes control over others, such as the specification of work methods, control over work schedules, and the labor of performance evaluation" (16.10). Quoting Kornberger et al. (2017, p. 79): "control is radically distributed, while power remains centralized" (16.10). Four features follow: digital intermediation as a business model; an open employment relation that produces "high heterogeneity among platform workers" (16.11); supervisory affordances in which "ratings systems have redistributed much of the labor of surveillance to customers" (16.12); and spatial dispersion (16.12).

The review is sceptical of the cage. Workers "can indeed develop forms of resistance": Rahman's freelancers "forming online alliances with clients to game the performance evaluation system" (16.7); Chen's Didi drivers, "40% … using digital strategies such as bots or owning multiple phones to subvert the algorithm" (16.7). "To go further and argue that algorithmic management of platform work expands company control over the work process or forestalls workers' capacity to contest their subordinate position is to overestimate or reify the power of digital technology" (16.7). It is also sceptical of the ratings instrument: "the general inflation of ratings raises questions about the efficacy of such reputational controls" (16.12). The research agenda asks whether workers "can … gain support from the consumers and customers they serve, altering the power in this triadic relationship" (16.15).

## Relation to the argument

**P11 and ruling D9.** The review supplies the author's triad with a named theoretical ancestor (Simmel's *tertius gaudens*) and a named structural fact (control delegated to the two counterparts, power kept by the third). "Irreducible" is supported in the weak sense that the platform's power runs through the other two; the review does not argue that the triad cannot be decomposed, and does not measure it.

**The abstract's "binds and optimizes."** "Binds" matches the retained functions (allocation, pricing, revenue). "Optimizes" is not the review's word; it treats the platform's objective as profit and externalisation (16.10) without a formal objective.

**Where it cuts against the author.** Two places. First, the alliance evidence (16.7): the counterparts can side with each other against the mediator, which means the triad is not fixed in the shape the abstract assumes (two parties each "gaming the mediator"); one party may game it *with* the other. Second, the heterogeneity point (16.11): supplemental earners "can refuse low-paying tasks" (16.8), so the pressure to game the mediator varies with dependence (Schor et al. 2019, cited 16.8).
