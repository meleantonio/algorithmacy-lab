## What it argues

Against the view that declining contributions reflect inequity-averse altruists disappointed by free-riders, the authors test a "confused learners" hypothesis: people start uncertain and "learn from experience how to improve their payoff" (ms p. 2). Payoff-based learning "requires a reliable correlation between behaviour and payoffs," and that correlation is weakened when benefits from groupmates "swamp the true cost" of one's own contribution (ms p. 4). They define influence as the share of a person's potential payoff range under her own control, (|1−MPCR|) / ((|1−MPCR|) + MPCR(N−1)) (eq. 1, ms p. 4), which rises as group size or the return falls.

Simulation: more influence gives a stronger negative correlation between contributions and payoffs (F(1,45) = 126, R²adj = 0.73; ms p. 5). Black box: with influence of 43% (N = 3, MPCR 0.4) contributions fell from 46% to 15% over 16 rounds; with influence of 11–12% (N = 12, or MPCR 0.8) they "finished at just over 50%, indicative of no learning on average" (ms p. 6). Meta-data: initial contribution 49% (95% CI 47.2–51.0), declining 2.4 points per round; the decline was faster with smaller N and smaller MPCR, and influence predicted the rate of decline (F(1,209.4) = 43.2, B = −0.44; ms p. 6). When studies showed groupmates' payoffs, the decline ran "as if" the correlation were −1, and influence mattered less (ms p. 7): "a difficult environment for trial-and-error learning does not matter when players can reliably learn by observing and comparing among others" (ms p. 7). In "public delight" games (MPCR > 1) contributions rose faster with more influence (ms p. 8). None of three families of prosocial models fit as well (ms pp. 8–9).

Closing: "Perhaps much of modern life is at least partially like a black box, where individuals cannot perfectly determine the effects of their actions upon society, nor how others are affecting them" (ms p. 10).

## Relation to the argument

**Q4 and the meaning of "ecological" in a triad.** The paper gives a quantity for what the author's phrase needs: the correlation between what a person does and what comes back, which the authors call influence. In a platform the mediator sits between action and return and pools many counterparts, which on this measure lowers influence. The evidence says learning from one's own outcomes then slows to nothing within the horizons tested (ms p. 6), and that showing others' outcomes restores it (ms p. 7). That second finding is a design lever with a behavioural test behind it, and it is not disclosure of the mechanism; it is disclosure of counterparts' outcomes.

**P9 / D7.** Supports the conditional form: people do understand through deciding and taking in results, when their own action moves the result enough to be seen through the noise of others.

**P8, disclosure.** The authors' own instrument for faster learning is comparative outcome information, not a description of the rule. This sits with the author's D3 distinction, with the caveat that outcome comparison is also "something to read."

**Exposure 1 (no convention tested in a triad).** Still true; these are N-person games with a fixed rule, not a mediator with an objective.

## Caution

Read in the author manuscript; page and line numbers are the manuscript's and the published article's figure and table numbers may differ. The meta-analysis uses aggregate study means, not individual data (ms p. 10), and stops at 2017 (ms p. 10). The black-box experiment used only the first box per session for the main result (ms p. 11). Group size and return are confounded with influence by construction; the authors address this with the simulated-correlation variable. The "black box" and "modern life" remarks (ms p. 10) are the authors' interpretation.
