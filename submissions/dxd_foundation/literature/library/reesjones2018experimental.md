## What it argues

Rees-Jones and Skowronek ask whether a strategy-proof mechanism, the flagship success of market design, in fact removes the need to strategise. The NRMP uses a version of deferred acceptance, under which truthful ranking is optimal for the applicant (p. 4). The authors put the people who had just used it through "an incentivized matching task" run with "the standard algorithm that was used by the NRMP" (p. 6). In each simulation 50 students applied to 5 programs of 10 places each; all students agreed on the ranking of programs; every student had some chance at every program, so any list other than "all five, in order of payment" was strictly suboptimal (p. 7). Students were pointed to the NRMP training link and told nothing more about the mechanism (pp. 6–7).

Main results:
- "23.3% (N=399)" of 1,714 submitted a suboptimal list (p. 12). Of those, 64.7% permuted the order but ranked all five (15.1% of the sample), 28.3% shortened the list (6.6%), and 7.0% did both (1.6%) (pp. 12–13).
- Cost: suboptimal lists earned $18.20 on average against $22.80 for optimal lists, 21.2% less (t = −5.43, p < .001) (p. 13). The authors warn that this gap mixes the effect of the list with assigned strategic position.
- Validation against the real match (p. 14): students who said they had misrepresented to the NRMP were 9.4 points more likely to do so here (22.6% against 33.0%, p = .013); misrepresenters had shorter real NRMP lists.
- Correlates (average marginal effects from a logit, p. 15–18): a one-SD rise in MCAT score, +5 points of truth-telling (SE 0.010); randomly assigned test score, +4 points; Raven's matrices score, +3 points. Overconfidence went the other way from the usual expectation, +8 points in the full model, which the authors, in a footnote, say could arise because overconfident students overestimate their strategic position (fn. **, p. 17). Priming the expected match had no effect (p = .743).
- Advice: advice from the NRMP website raised truthful reporting (AME 0.10 in the full model); advice from other students did not once other factors were controlled (AME −0.01, p = .565 in the full model; on its own it was +0.04, p = .043) (pp. 17–18). The authors read free-text advice (SI) and report "a substantial fraction of recommended strategies are misguided" (p. 19).
- Trust: 97.3% trusted the NRMP to run the algorithm honestly; 63.4% did not trust other students to report truthfully. Trusting residency programs to rank fairly lowered truthful reporting by 5 points (p. 18).

In the discussion they conclude that "the use of this mechanism will ultimately favor the groups who best understand it" (p. 19) and that women were eight points more likely to misrepresent (p. 20). They call for training and "credible, high-quality advice" (p. 19).

## Relation to the argument

**P8 (a mediator's rule can remove a strategic task, and presentation decides who benefits).** The source supports the second clause and qualifies the first. Deferred acceptance removes the strategic task in theory; in this sample about one student in four behaved as if the task were still there, after practice with the real mechanism and with a career at stake. The task disappears for the person only when the person believes it has gone. That fits the argument map's requirement that such rules also show the task is gone. It also cuts against any suggestion that removal alone is enough.

**P9 and ruling D3.** The advice result is evidence for D3's distinction. Information from the mechanism's own owner (the NRMP website) helped; information passed among the participants did not. Both are literacy-type interventions in D3's sense, so the paper does not test an algorithmacy-type one.

**The word-separation precedent.** The authors report who bears the cost of the remaining complexity: lower scorers, women, the distrustful. The scribes' move lowered the cost most for learners (P6); here the cost of a mechanism that is hard to read falls unevenly across groups. The comparison is an analogy.

Facet check: the facet (H, entry for reesjones2018experimental) read this arXiv text in part and its quotations and the AME 0.10 for the NRMP website agree with the full text. No contradiction found.

Limits for the argument: the participants are highly trained and the setting is a clearinghouse with no objective of its own, which is the weakest point flagged in P8.

## Caution

Cite the arXiv text as a working paper; the 2018 PNAS article is shorter and I did not read it, so page numbers and some wording differ. The task is a simulated match with money at stake, not the real match. Only 25 of 147 schools took part and about 52% of the students contacted completed and passed exclusions (p. 6). Several correlates are not randomised and the authors say so (p. 8). The finding concerns a mechanism that is strategy-proof for applicants; it says nothing about platform recommenders.
