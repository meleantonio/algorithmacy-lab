## What it argues

The paper asks which assignment mechanism is better when families misjudge their chances. New Haven used a Boston-style mechanism that rewards strategic ranking, against a strategy-proof deferred acceptance (DA) mechanism where truthful ranking is a dominant strategy. The survey finds that beliefs about admissions chances "differ from rational expectations values" (abstract): stated chances differ from rational-expectations values "by a mean (absolute) value of 37 percentage points" (p. 2). Only 10.8% of respondents correctly said the neighborhood priority group beats the sibling group, and 20.6% correctly described the cost of ranking a school lower, both worse than random guessing on a three-option question (the authors: "worse than under random guessing", p. 24), although 89% reported using some district information source (p. 24). 32% of applicants who applied ranked a school other than their stated favourite first ("revealed strategic"), and 48% of those did so mistakenly, with better odds at the favourite (pp. 14, 16). Subjective beliefs predict the ranking choice; rational-expectations chances, conditional on beliefs, do not (pp. 2, 21). The authors say the evidence is "strongly suggestive but not definitive" because it is non-experimental (p. 23).

The structural model puts subjective beliefs into the family's choice. Counterfactuals: a switch to truthful DA raises mean welfare by 3.9 fewer miles travelled per trip, 27% of households' mean welfare gain over the outside option, larger for low-SES households (p. 4). An analyst who assumes rational beliefs finds the opposite: DA lowers welfare by 1.8 miles; "an analyst who assumed families had accurate beliefs would have reached the opposite conclusion" (pp. 2, 4). A best-case information intervention that gives every household rational-expectations beliefs would raise welfare 4.5 fewer miles (31%) against baseline and only 0.55 miles (3%) against DA (p. 5). Gains from scaled-down information interventions "are limited until belief errors are completely eliminated" (p. 44, Figure 6).

## Relation to the argument

**P8.** The strongest quantitative support in this batch for the first clause: where people misjudge the mediator's rule, a rule that removes the strategic task beats one that rewards getting it right, and the gain is larger for low-SES households. It also supplies the caveat the argument map needs: the comparison flips under the analyst's assumption of accurate beliefs (the argument map files the worry that a removed task relocates under Arteaga et al., which this paper predates and I did not read).

**Ruling D3 and P9.** The disclosure flag in SOURCE.md says evidence shows awareness, not changed behaviour. Here 89% used district information and understood little (p. 24), and the authors report "Descriptive evidence that using district-provided informational resources does not reduce belief errors" (p. 5). That is correlational and rests on self-report after placement, so it supports "weak and inconsistent", not "doesn't affect". The authors also state that a perfect information fix would bring the strategic mechanism level with DA, which leaves the literacy route open in principle.

**Against the author.** DA loses the expression of preference intensity, which the authors name as the benefit of the strategic mechanism (p. 1). Removing the task has a cost the paper prices. The district is the designer and has no interest of its own, so P10 gets no support.

## Caution

A working paper; the facet's 2018 date is the first circulation, the text on file is the December 2019 revision. Results rest on a structural model, a post-placement survey (the authors test for recall and ex post updating and find little, pp. 11, 20), and one low-income district. The intro gives the gap between with- and without-survey estimates as 5.7 miles or 39% of baseline welfare (p. 4); Section 6 gives 40% (p. 42). Welfare is in miles-travelled equivalents. Not an experiment on description or disclosure.
