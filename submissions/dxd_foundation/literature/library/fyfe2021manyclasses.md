## What it argues

The study was built to test whether a laboratory-backed instructional practice generalises across real classes. The practice chosen was feedback timing, where the literature points both ways: Kulik and Kulik's 1988 meta-analysis for immediacy in classrooms, and later work ("delaying feedback produces better learning and retention," Butler & Woodward 2018, quoted ms p. 4) for delay via spacing.

Result: "Across 38 classes, the overall estimate for the effect of feedback timing was 0.002 (95% HDI −0.05 to 0.05), indicating that there was no effect of immediate versus delayed feedback on student learning that generalizes across classes" (abstract, ms p. 2). The incentive manipulation made no difference (difference 0.00, HDI −0.10 to 0.10; ms p. 10). Between-class heterogeneity was small (SD of class means 0.06; ms p. 11). "There were no credibly non-zero effects for 40 pre-registered moderators" (ms p. 2). The authors note trends, not credible effects, toward delayed feedback where exams used retrieval items (pooled coefficient 95% HDI −0.113 to −0.002, ms p. 17) and where dosage was higher (more quizzes, longer delays), while cautioning that these regions were under-sampled (ms pp. 14–16, 18).

On the prior evidence: Kulik and Kulik "reported small to moderate advantages for immediate feedback in 10 out of 11 studies conducted in classroom settings that often lacked experimental designs" (ms pp. 17–18). The conclusion is methodological as much as substantive: "repeated A/B testing of a global effect in isolated settings will likely yield conflicting findings with limited external validity" (ms pp. 19–20).

## Relation to the argument

**Q3, feedback timing evidence that cuts both ways.** The largest field test the facet found returns a tight null for immediacy against a few days' delay on a learning outcome. It neither supports the author's "immediacy" nor the delayed-feedback counter-claim; it says that in ordinary practice the timing variable did not matter. For the abstract this means "immediacy" cannot be carried by the educational literature; what remains is the human-factors claim about acting in the moment (Shneiderman, Hutchins et al.), which is about performance during action, not later learning.

**P9.** Kahneman and Klein's "rapid" belongs to the acquisition of skill in high-validity environments; this study is about retention of content after a quiz. The two are different claims and this card is a reason to keep them apart in the draft.

**Method.** The ManyClasses design, many heterogeneous sites with one manipulated contrast, is a model for the test the argument map says is missing (exposure 1): a convention varied across many platform settings with a behavioural outcome.

## Caution

Read in the accepted preprint; the published article may differ in pagination and minor wording. The delay was short (1–5 days) and dosage low (17 of 38 classes had one or two quizzes per condition); the authors say the design "is ill-suited to determine whether immediate or delayed feedback timing could be beneficial under special circumstances" (ms p. 19). Students could revisit feedback or share it, which dilutes the contrast (ms p. 20). Volunteer instructors, college students, Canvas only. The outcome is a course exam, not behaviour in a market.
