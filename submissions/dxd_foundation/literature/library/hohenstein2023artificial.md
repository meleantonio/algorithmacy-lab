## What it argues

Smart replies change how people talk and how they are seen. In Study 1 the authors randomise each member of a pair to have smart replies or not, and use the partner's randomly assigned availability as an instrument for the partner's actual use. Availability "was a strong encouragement to use them" and smart replies were 14.3% of sent messages; availability meant 10.2% more messages per minute (intent-to-treat t(198) = 2.173, P = 0.0309) (p. 2). Perception and reality diverge. The more a participant thought the partner used smart replies, the less cooperative (t(92) = −9.89) and less affiliative (t(92) = −6.90) they rated the partner, "even after controlling for their partner's actual smart reply use"; the authors add this "does not show causally" what happens (p. 3). Yet the partner's actual use raised the self's rating of cooperation (IV b = 15.66, P = 0.018) and affiliation (b = 21.79, P = 0.007) (p. 3). Perceived and actual use correlate weakly (r = 0.22, p. 3).

Study 2 isolates sentiment. Negative smart replies produced more negative conversations than positive ones (d = .352) and than Google's replies (d = .323); Google's replies did not raise sentiment significantly above no replies (P = 0.58) (pp. 3–4). The Discussion draws the line: effects occur "when users … use them" and not on exposure alone, since "we did not find any main effects of being exposed to smart replies" (p. 4). Closing claim: "the prevailing anti-social connotations of AI undermine these potential benefits if used overtly" (abstract). The authors flag unmeasured long-run effects, including possible homogenisation of language (p. 5).

## Relation to the argument

**Triadic evidence (facet H).** One of the few randomized designs where a third party (the AI) sits inside a two-person exchange and each person's outcome depends on what the other does with it. The outcomes move with what the mediator writes and with what the other party suspects, not with any skill at reading the tool. That supports the reading in facet H's "what moved the work" paragraph, with a qualifier: the tool was shown to everyone in the availability arms, and exposure alone changed nothing, so what moved outcomes was *use* of the mediator's output.

**Ruling D3 and P9.** The partner could not tell which messages were suggestions, so this is a non-disclosure design; the penalty the paper reports attaches to suspicion, not to disclosure. It does not test disclosure. It fits the claim that a visible AI role can carry a social cost, which is a different point from "disclosure doesn't change what people do".

**Facet check:** facet H says "a mediator that writes for one of them" and "a mediator that edits one party's message". The tool offers tap-to-send suggestions the user chooses; it does not write or edit unprompted. The facet's "closer and more cooperative" is the abstract's wording and holds for the partner-use IV result only. The abstract does not say that Google's replies did not raise sentiment against no replies (Study 2), which the body does.

## Caution

MTurk workers discussing one policy topic for about six minutes; the partner is anonymous, with no history. Study 1 perception results are correlational (authors' own words). IV estimates rest on an exclusion restriction the authors argue is plausible by design (p. 6). Cannot be cited for relationships over time or for generative models beyond the Google reply model used.

## Also used by facet T (2026-10-15)

Facet T asks whether HCI treats a human–human exchange through a mediating system as a triad. This experiment is the nearest case, and its design answers the question. The smart-reply model is offered to one or both partners and acts for whoever taps it; it has no objective of its own, and the outcome that moved was the partner's *suspicion* of use (t(92) = −9.89 for cooperation), not any property of the mediator. Under the field's founding definition (`hancock2020ai`, p. 4) the AI "operates on behalf of a communicator," a principal–agent relation, and platform ranking or matching is excluded from AI-MC. For the abstract's sentence the lesson is that the AI-MC literature, including this paper, is evidence about a dyad with a delegate, not about a triad whose third member "binds and optimizes." Facet T records it as the best-controlled design to borrow from (random assignment of the mediator's availability to one side, instrumented use) if the author's triad is ever put in a lab.
