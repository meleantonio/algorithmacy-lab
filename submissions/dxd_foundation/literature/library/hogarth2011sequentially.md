## What it argues

People encode frequencies they experience one at a time with little effort (Hasher & Zacks, cited WP p. 6), so the authors turn probabilistic reasoning problems into sequences of simulated outcomes the person generates by clicking. Their claim against Gigerenzer is that natural frequencies in his experiments were "summarized"; "participants in their experiments never actually experienced data sequentially" (WP p. 7).

Exp. 1 (Bayesian updating, birthday, conjunction, Linda, hospital, regression to the mean, Monty Hall). "Across all problems and groups, the percentage of correct answers after experience exceeds that of the analytic responses, and typically by a large margin" (WP p. 14). Bayesian task, Sophisticated A: 5 of 29 (17%) correct analytically, 28 of 29 (97%) after experience, 23 of 29 (79%) on the paid final answer (WP pp. 15–16). Mean samples drawn per problem: 66 and 65 for the two sophisticated groups, 49 for the naive (WP p. 14). Final answers mostly sat between the experience and analytic answers; some participants "did revert toward answers made without experience" (WP p. 18).

Exp. 2 (allocate 40 credits across two investments described by a regression). The analytic group underestimated the probability of ending below 40 by 16 points on average (SD 9); the experience groups erred by 0 (SD 11) and 5 (SD 9); t = 7.1, p < 0.001. On the probability of beating no-investment the analytic group erred by +24 (SD 10) against +1 (SD 16) and +3 (SD 8); t = 7.7 (WP pp. 23–24). "There was little or no difference in accuracy of probabilistic inferences between the groups of Sophisticated and Naïve participants who experienced simulated outcomes" (WP p. 24).

The discussion sets boundary conditions: how much experience is needed and whether active sampling matters (WP p. 27); trust in the mechanism, "a more appropriate metaphor is that of a 'grey box'" (WP p. 29); and a "clash of intuitions" between the analytic reaction and the experienced one (WP p. 30). The authors place the work under "choice architecture" and trace the principle to Brunswik (1952) (WP pp. 26–27), and relate it to Kahneman and Klein (2009) on when to trust intuition (WP p. 31).

## Relation to the argument

**P9 / D7 ("we understand THROUGH deciding and quickly ingesting the results").** This is the strongest direct evidence in the facet for the author's picture: statistically naive adults reached the accuracy of trained students after a few minutes of generating outcomes and watching them (WP pp. 14–16, 24). The feedback here is immediate and unequivocal, and the environment is kind by construction.

**What the result does not reach.** The person acts on a simulation whose generating process is fixed and known to the experimenter ("The program simulates correctly the current situation," WP p. 13). No counterpart, no mediator with an objective, no cost to sampling. The naive group sampled less (WP p. 14), and the authors ask whether people trust a mechanism they cannot inspect (WP p. 29). Transposed to a platform, the "grey box" question becomes who built the simulation and whether its model of the counterpart is honest, which returns the argument to P12 (custody of the convention).

**P10, the second convention.** The paper is a worked case of the convention the argument map marks `[inf]`: outcomes returned fast and plainly enough to learn from. It shows the effect for probabilistic inference, not for coordination with another person, and it shows the gain can be partly surrendered at the final answer (97% to 79%, WP pp. 15–16).

## Caution

Read in the 2010 working-paper version; the published article has a different title and may differ in numbers, wording and pagination, so every locator here is to the working paper and should be rechecked against *JEP: General* 140(3) before print. Small samples (20 and 18 naive adults, recruited through the authors' contacts; WP pp. 11, 22); Exp. 2 is between-subject only; the naive groups were not paid for accuracy in Exp. 1. The problems in Exp. 1 "were specifically chosen for their history of inappropriate responses" (WP p. 31), so the size of the gain is not general.
