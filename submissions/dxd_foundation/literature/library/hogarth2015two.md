## What it argues

The article restates Hogarth's (2001) distinction. Wicked learning environments are "situations in which feedback in the form of outcomes of actions or observations is poor, misleading, or even missing"; in kind ones "feedback links outcomes directly to the appropriate actions or judgments and is both accurate and plentiful" (p. 379). The new move is to split inference into two settings: L, where a person learns, and T, where the person predicts or chooses, and to ask how the elements of information in the two match (p. 380). Six cases follow (Fig. 1, p. 381): A (L = T) and B (L ≈ T, random error only) are kind; C (elements of L filtered out), D (T a subset of L), E (L and T intersect through systematic factors, "self-fulfilling prophecies or so-called treatment effects") and F (nothing in common) are wicked (p. 380).

Two statements matter here. "A wicked learning environment can emerge as a result of actions taken by the person making the inferences (as in self-fulfilling prophecies, Case E) as well as the characteristics of the environment" (p. 380). And on change over time: "We have not explicitly considered dynamic or nonstationary environments. At one level, such environments are wicked (a likely Case E). However, if the nature of the dynamic change can be inferred from the first setting, these environments can be kind" (p. 382).

The prescriptive line is to make environments kind on purpose: "our framework suggests deliberately creating kind environments. Indeed, this reasoning motivated our work on simulated experience" (p. 382), where people experiencing sequential outcomes gave accurate probabilistic judgments (Hogarth & Soyer 2011). Table 1 (p. 383) lists survivorship bias, censorship bias, selection bias and the "hot stove" effect as cases of mismatch. Closing on Erev and Roth (2014), the authors say that instead of assuming agents can calculate maximising solutions "one should provide experiences that lead to appropriate responses—that is, in kind environments. Although the way to achieve this remains uncertain, posing the problem in these terms is a major step forward" (p. 383).

## Relation to the argument

**The abstract's "ecological feedback immediacy (… Kahneman & Klein, 2009)".** Kahneman and Klein borrowed "wicked environments" from Hogarth; this is the fuller statement of what makes feedback usable. The condition is a match between where one learns and where one acts, with feedback that is "accurate and plentiful" (p. 379). Speed is not in the definition. The article supports the "ecological" half of the author's phrase and says nothing for the "immediacy" half.

**P9 and exposure 3 of the argument map.** The author's Part 1 says an adaptive intermediary disrupts the feedback that learning by acting needs. This article gives that claim a classification: a mediator that reacts to the person's own behaviour produces Case E (treatment effects), and a nonstationary mediator is "a likely Case E" unless the person can infer how it changes (p. 382). The paper does not mention platforms; the application is the project's inference.

**P10, the second convention.** "Deliberately creating kind environments" (p. 382) is the nearest statement in this literature to the author's second convention, and the authors' own instance is simulated experience of outcomes, not faster return of real outcomes. Their closing sentence concedes that "the way to achieve this remains uncertain" (p. 383).

## Caution

A seven-page conceptual piece with no new data; its empirical support is cited from elsewhere (Hogarth & Soyer 2011, Table 1 sources). "Kindness or wickedness can vary in degree," and the authors say their "intention here is limited to classification" (p. 380). It does not treat interfaces, timing of feedback, or multi-party settings; the Case E reading of a mediator is the project's. The repository cover sheet's "25(5)" is an error.
