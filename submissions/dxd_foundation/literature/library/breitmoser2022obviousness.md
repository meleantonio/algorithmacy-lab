# Breitmoser, Y., & Schweighofer-Kodritsch, S. (2022). Obviousness around the clock. *Experimental Economics, 25*(2), 483–513. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10683-021-09720-z

## What it argues
The authors replicate Li's (2017) second-price (2P) against ascending-clock (AC) comparison "using the same random numbers, interface, and instructions (aside from translation)" (p. 5) and add three formats that split the move from 2P to AC into steps: 2PAC-B (sealed bids, then a blind ascending clock replays the outcome), 2PAC (the same with drop-out counts shown), and AC-B (a live clock with drop-out counts hidden). Only AC and AC-B are OSP; 2P, 2PAC and 2PAC-B are strategy-proof but not OSP (Proposition 1, p. 8). The step that theory says should make dominance obvious is 2PAC-B → AC-B, because that is where live bidding on the clock is added (p. 3).

Results. "Our 2P and AC replicate the respective behavior in Li's study almost exactly" (p. 3). After about three rounds, in mean absolute deviation of non-winning bids from values, "Clock-presentation (2P → 2PAC-B) accounts for around 50% in reducing deviations from truthful bidding, dynamic bidding (2PAC-B → AC-B) promotes this further by 12%, which is statistically insignificant, and the remaining share of almost 40% is due to live drop-out information (AC-B → AC)" (pp. 3–4; Figure 1 labels the first two steps 49% and 12% and gives 25% for passive drop-out information, p. 3; the live drop-out share is the text's "almost 40%", and the 39% used elsewhere in the facet is that figure rounded from the three shares summing to 100). "Theoretical obviousness therefore fails our stronger test" (p. 4). Overbidding by more than €1 develops "almost exclusively in 2P", reaching about 40% of bids, against under 20% in every other format (p. 9, Result 2). In the first three rounds the OSP formats do reduce under-bidding, but in the unfamiliar X-auctions "we find no such initial differences, suggesting that those in our standard auctions are in fact driven by familiarity" (p. 4). A structural model finds bidding "dynamic in all formats featuring a price clock, including 2PAC and 2PAC-B", with no treatment differences in contingent reasoning (β = 0 everywhere) and dominance recognised only for underbidding (Result 3, p. 16).

Conclusion: "OSP should not be relied upon as a stand-alone knock-out criterion"; "obviousness is really a matter of what one gets to see (such as an ascending clock as part of the results presentation), not how one formally gets to decide" (p. 18). They suggest static mechanisms "might generally be improved by exploiting analogies to their dynamic OSP counterparts in how they are presented", and that "running two practice runs may be considered a substitute for implementing an OSP mechanism in our case" (fn. 17, p. 18).

## Relation to the argument
This is the most important counter-evidence in facet Y. The abstract's "strategic offloading (Li, 2017)" leans on Li's experiment as proof that an OSP rule relieves people of strategic reasoning. Breitmoser and Schweighofer-Kodritsch show, with Li's own materials, that most of the gain comes from showing the person an ascending clock after the fact and from seeing rivals drop out, not from the OSP property. For the author this cuts two ways. Against: the "offloading" cannot be attributed to the incentive structure of the rule; the person is helped by a representation of the outcome, which is closer to the abstract's "representational effect" and "ecological feedback immediacy" than to a rule change. For: the finding that presentation does most of the work, and that drop-out information (feedback from others) does the rest, supports the abstract's later clauses and P9 more than P10. It also narrows ruling D3: what helped here was neither disclosure of the rule nor advice but a change in what the person sees while deciding. The 36% against 7% figure from Li's serial-dictatorship treatment (`li2017obviously`) was not replicated here; the authors "excluded the third part of his experiment (random serial dictatorship mechanisms)" (p. 5).

## Caution
Discussion-paper version; the published article may report different numbers or tests. Student subjects, one lab, private values only; the authors say their "main lessons are general in nature" (fn. 4, p. 2) but tested only auctions. The decomposition percentages are averages after round three and depend on the "standardized" deviation measure (Figure 1 note, p. 3). The structural model's claims (β = 0, dynamic processing) rest on a logistic-choice specification the appendices justify. Nothing here concerns matching, platforms or mediators with objectives.
