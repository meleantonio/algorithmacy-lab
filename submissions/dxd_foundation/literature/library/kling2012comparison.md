## What it argues
Public policy pairs choice with free comparative information and assumes that availability is enough. The authors call the gap between availability and use "comparison friction" (§I). They test it with a letter. The intervention group received a one-page cover letter showing (1) the person's current plan and its predicted annual cost for their own drugs, (2) the lowest-cost plan and its cost, (3) the potential savings and (4) the end date of open enrollment, plus a printout from Medicare's Plan Finder. The comparison group received a letter pointing to the Medicare website; both groups received a booklet on using it (§VI).

Results (§VI.C): 28% of the intervention group switched plans between 2006 and 2007, against 17% of the comparison group, a difference of 11.5 percentage points (9.8 after regression adjustment). The predicted annual cost fell by about $100 per letter recipient, roughly 5% of the comparison group's predicted cost (Abstract, §I). Of the intervention group, 9% moved to the lowest-cost plan against 2% of the comparison group; the other switches went to other plans (20% against 15%, not significantly different) (§VI.D). In 2008 switching was 23% in the comparison group and 20% in the intervention group, not significantly different (§VI.C).

Baseline facts show how little people had used the free tools: only 18% reviewed personalised plan comparisons; over 80% were satisfied with their plan; only 37% knew that only some plans have a deductible (§V). A nationally representative figure for ever using personalised cost information is also 18% (§VII). The authors rule out the reading that switching costs absorb all the gain: it would require the letter to have caused only 10% to switch, each gaining about $1,030, which "seems implausible" when the switch takes a phone call (§VII). They list the letter's components: "a recommended default option (the lowest-cost plan), a clear statement of that option's benefits (potential savings), and a deadline" (§VI). They add that it "neither contained difficult to acquire information nor reduced the effort required to change plans" (§VI).

## Relation to the argument
Premise P8 rests on this paper for "28% against 17%" and the facet reads it as a computed comparison that did what a standard-format disclosure (Seira et al. 2017) did not. The full text supports the numbers (n = 406) and complicates the reading. First, the letter was a bundle: a personalised computation, a default, a statement of benefit and a deadline, delivered through a partnership with a trusted hospital and after an in-person baseline interview, and the authors say further work "could unbundle these effects" (§VIII). So the paper does not isolate the computation as the active part. Second, the gain went to those who did the switching; the intervention changed the mix of who acts, not the person's understanding. Third, the sample is Wisconsin patients with more college graduates and more dissatisfaction than a national sample (§I), so the 28% is not a national rate.

On D3: the paper shows that information that was free and available did nothing until it was delivered with the calculation done. That supports the author's point that disclosure alone is weak, in a way Seira et al. also show. It does not show that the lever was algorithmacy-side, since the person still had to decide and call. For P10 and D8: the authors note that few third parties provided personalised comparison and that rules limited their role, and they flag the risk of plans capturing the market for advice (§VIII), a point that bears on who should hold the comparison.

Facet check: n = 406 and 28% against 17% confirmed. The facet's gloss ("computed comparison") omits the default and deadline that the authors list.
