## What it argues

The paper defines a field. AI-mediated communication (AI-MC) is "interpersonal communication in which an intelligent agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to accomplish communication goals" (abstract, p. 1). Classic CMC kept agency with the sender: "Agency remains with the communicator: message production and impression management are broadly understood to manifest the communicator's goals" (p. 1). AI-MC "upend[s] assumptions around agency and mediation" (p. 1). Table 1 (p. 3) gives five dimensions: magnitude, media type, optimization goal, autonomy and role orientation. Autonomy "is similar to the principal–agent relationship (Sappington, 1991), in which the human communicator is the principal who delegates some authority and autonomy to the AI agent" (p. 4). Role orientation is sender or receiver; "Google Translate allows both sender and receiver to converse, using AI as mediator" (p. 4). "Commonly, the involvement of AI is not disclosed" (p. 2).

The boundaries matter for this facet. The authors exclude assistants that "do not represent other individuals, such as Apple's Siri or Amazon's Alexa" (p. 4), and they exclude platform algorithms: AI-MC "could be more broadly conceptualized to include all algorithms that mediate human communication, like the Facebook 'Newsfeed' and other content ranking, recommendation and classification algorithms … This type of conceptualization is too large to serve as a useful research framework and we therefore exclude these kinds of algorithmic tools that indirectly support human communication" (p. 4). On disclosure: "disclosure is complicated" (p. 8); a bot-disclosure law "seems to rely on the assumption that a disclaimer will better equip receivers to evaluate a message's intent and veracity," and the authors note "the empirical uncertainty of this assumption (Jakesch et al., 2019; Waddell, 2018)" alongside legal objections (p. 8). The agenda also records an early result: "when task-oriented conversations were not successful, participants assigned significantly less responsibility to their partners when AI mediation (smart reply) was used (Hohenstein & Jung, 2019)" (p. 5).

## Relation to the argument

**Question 2 of the facet (is there HCI work that treats a human–human interaction through a mediating system as a triad?).** This is the field's founding definition, and it answers no in a precise way. The AI in AI-MC is an *agent* of one communicator, modelled as principal and agent (p. 4); its optimization goal is the sender's (Table 1, p. 3). A platform mediator with its own objective is explicitly outside the frame (p. 4). So AI-MC studies (including `hohenstein2023artificial`) are evidence about a dyad with a delegate on one side, not about the abstract's triad. That is a finding the paper should state rather than paper over: the nearest HCI literature defines the mediator so that the third party has no interest of its own.

**P8 and ruling D3 (disclosure).** The paper's own position is that whether and how AI involvement should be disclosed is open and context-bound (p. 8), which is consistent with the author's claim that disclosure is not the lever, but it offers no behavioural evidence.

## Caution

A definitional essay with no study. The advance-access pagination differs from the journal's; convert before citing. The authors say the dimension set will "evolve" (p. 4). It should not be cited as evidence that mediators are neutral, only that the AI-MC framework models them as agents of one side.
