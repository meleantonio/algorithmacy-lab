## What it argues

Kirsh asks why thinking is so often interactive. The received answer is cost: "Cognitive processes flow to wherever it is cheaper to perform them" (p. 442). He accepts it as "essentially, a correct account" but "only one of the reasons" (p. 442), and adds a third cost, coordinating inner and outer processes (anchoring, p. 443, Fig. 3). The essay's main part lists seven further benefits (abstract, p. 441): a shareable object of thought (§3), rearrangement (§4), persistence and independence (§5), reformulation and explicitness (§6), natural encoding (§7), multiple representations (§8) and construction (§9).

Passages that matter here. On memory against perception: "it cannot be assumed, without argument that the costs are always lower in perceptually retrieving information than 'internally' retrieving information" (p. 446). On rearrangement: "Physical movement has replaced mental computation" (p. 447), with Tetris as the case where "physical rotation is, in fact, a bit faster than mental rotation" (p. 447). On explicitness: "the numerals '√2209' and '47' both refer to the number 47, but the numeral '47' is a more explicit encoding of 47", and "Much external activity can be interpreted as converting expressions into more explicit formulations" (p. 449). On cost and dependency: "Tools reshape the cost structure of task performance, and people adapt by becoming dependent on those tools" (p. 450). On construction: "The physical world does not lie" (p. 451). In a footnote he gives Vygotsky's view that "what we learned to do first on the outside we came to do on the inside" (p. 451, n. 3).

The last section argues that some processes are "Type 2" in Marr's sense. Kirsh first quotes von Neumann, for whom "a real object might not constitute the simplest description of itself", then Marr, for whom such a process is "the simultaneous action of a considerable number of processes, whose interaction is its own simplest description" (both quoted p. 453). His examples are protein folding, the n-body problem, "the solution to certain market equilibrium problems, situations where the outcome depends on the voting of n participants" (p. 453). For these, "it is necessary to run the process, and it is best to run the process repeatedly. No tractable equation will work as well" (p. 453); when the system is not to hand, one runs a physical analogue such as an orrery (p. 453, Fig. 11). Footnote 8 adds that software agents can "qualitatively change what humans can do" (p. 453).

## Relation to the argument

**P5 and the abstract's representational effect.** Section 6 gives the cleanest cognitive-science description of what a space between words does: it is a reformulation that makes a boundary explicit, so that recognition replaces inference. Kirsh does not mention text; the match is by function.

**D7 and P9 (understanding through deciding).** The Type-2 passage is the strongest warrant in this facet for the author's claim that a person understands a many-agent outcome by acting and taking in the result: for outcomes that depend on n participants, Kirsh says, one must run the process. Two limits hold the claim to a narrow form. Kirsh's thinker runs a model or a repeatable physical process; a person on a platform runs the real system once, with other agents and no reset. And `kayongo2022visualization` shows that when every agent acts on the same display the result moves. So the source supports "run it to know it", not "run it and the result will stay still".

**"Internalize text."** Footnote 3 is the only passage in this facet that touches the abstract's claim that a representational change let readers internalise; it is Vygotsky, offered as speculation.

**Costs.** The dependency sentence (p. 450) belongs in the paper's costs paragraph beside `grinschgl2021consequences`.

## Caution

An essay without data; the origami and recipe observations are "pilot studies" with no numbers. The market and voting examples occupy one sentence and are not analysed as strategic interaction. The Tetris figures are second-hand from Kirsh and Maglio. Do not cite it for reading or for learning outcomes.
