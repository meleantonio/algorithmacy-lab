## What it argues

The authors argue that platform-work research has looked at platform–worker relations and neglected the client. "The current debate on platform work has not yet sufficiently considered how platforms, based on algorithmic management, shape the interrelationships between workers and clients" (p. 2). Their proposal is a "service relation approach" (abstract, p. 1) built on Leidner's (1996) "three-way interest alliances," "anchored in a triangular service relation—platform, client, worker—configured by intersubjective, institutional and spatio-temporal dimensions" (p. 2). Work on these platforms "mandatorily implies the engagement of three parties, platforms, workers and clients (Florisson and Mandl, 2018)" (p. 3). "Considering only the interrelationships between platforms and workers prevents an apprehension of the complex interplay of interests underlying the configuration of the triangular service relation" (pp. 4–5).

Within that triangle the client is not fixed as the worker's opponent. Workers develop "strategies that allow reframing the client as not simply an antagonist but also as a potential ally of workers within the interrelationships they establish with the platforms" (p. 2); they "can also establish connections with clients, for example, when they achieve client trust and are able to agree to allocate tasks directly without the need for platform intermediation (Jarrahi et al., 2020)" (p. 3). The review also repeats the "anticipatory compliance" finding (Bucher et al. 2021) that workers "contribute to the co-construction of the algorithms' power" (pp. 3–4), and ends by calling for research on "the network of the voices of platforms, workers and clients" and on "the differences and interconnections between algorithmic and human management" (abstract, p. 1).

## Relation to the argument

**P11 and ruling D9.** A direct, if thin, statement that the platform relation is a triangle and that two-party framings miss its configuration. It supports the abstract's "counterparts … coordinate through an algorithmic mediator," and it names the alliance structure (Leidner's three-way interest alliances) that a triad makes possible, which is Simmel's point (`simmel1950sociology`, p. 145).

**Where it cuts against the author.** The clearest evidence it carries is of *disintermediation*: workers and clients who trust each other "allocate tasks directly without the need for platform intermediation" (p. 3). If counterparts can leave the mediator once they have met through it, the triad is irreducible only while the platform holds the match, which is a time-bounded claim, not a structural one.

## Caution

A seven-page mini-review with no original data and no stated search method; it cannot be cited for how common any pattern is. Its triangle is borrowed from service-work sociology (Leidner, Lopez), so the claims are about service relations, not about the algorithm's objective.
