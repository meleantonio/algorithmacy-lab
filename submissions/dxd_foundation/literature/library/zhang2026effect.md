## What it argues

Tibetan separates syllables with a dot (the tsek) but not words. The authors replaced the tsek at word boundaries with half-width or full-width spaces and asked whether larger spaces help more, and whether less proficient readers gain more (Introduction; Method 2.1).

Global measures (Tables 2–3): full-width spaces shortened average fixations and lengthened saccades for both groups. Total sentence reading time did not differ across the three conditions for either group (high proficiency 3,662, 3,685 and 3,639 ms; low proficiency 6,490, 6,314 and 6,441 ms; Table 2), and the authors state that "manually inserting interword spaces of different sizes did not significantly shorten or prolong the total time participants required to read the sentences" (Discussion 4.1). They attribute the null to a trade-off between unfamiliar layout and clearer boundaries, citing Bai et al. (2008), and to the removal of the tsek, which "disrupted readers' long-established reading habits" (Discussion 4.1).

Local measures on two-syllable target words (Tables 4–5): full-width spacing cut gaze duration, regressive time, total fixation time and fixation count. The cut was larger for low-proficiency readers: total fixation time fell by 140.57 ms for them against 86.25 ms for high-proficiency readers, and regression time by 123.98 against 49.24 ms (Results 3.2.2). The conclusion: "full-width spacing yields a stronger facilitative effect on lexical recognition for low-proficiency readers" (Conclusion). The authors contrast this with Chinese, where full-character spaces were reported to disrupt reading (Liang 2010, cited in Discussion 4.1), and put the difference down to Tibetan's stacked letters and visual crowding.

## Relation to the argument

This bears on P4, which already cites Wang (2024) for "a gain in Tibetan". It **narrows that clause**: the gain is in word-level processing and eye guidance, not in how long a sentence takes to read. In that respect Tibetan behaves like Chinese and Thai in the draft's list. It also **complicates the learner clause**. Within native readers, the weaker readers gained more from spaces, which runs against the implication that only practised dependence makes a convention pay.

For P5, the moderation by proficiency fits the representational argument: an external boundary helps most where the reader's internal segmentation is weakest.

## Caution

University students only; "low proficiency" means lower entrance-exam scores among native readers, not learners. The spaced conditions removed the tsek at word boundaries, so spacing is confounded with the loss of a familiar mark. Spaced sentences were physically longer, which the authors acknowledge inflates saccade amplitudes (Discussion 4.1). Several references in the paper are given in abbreviated form (e.g., "Kohsom, 1997", "Winskel and Radach, 2009"), so its literature review should not be relied on for citation detail.
