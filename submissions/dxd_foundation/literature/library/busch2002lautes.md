## What it argues

Busch defends the older consensus (Norden 1898, Balogh 1927, Hendrickson 1929) against Knox (1968), Gavrilov (1997) and Burnyeat (1997): reading aloud was the ancient "normal case", "unabhängig von der Textsorte" ("regardless of the kind of text", p. 7). He grants the revisionists' minimal point at once. That ancient readers had both modes "ist ohne Einschränkung zuzustimmen", and the claim that the ancients *could not* read silently "ist ohnehin ein merkwürdiges Mißverständnis" ("is a strange misunderstanding in any case", p. 5). His quarrel is over what was normal and for whom.

Four lines of argument carry the case:

1. **Word use (pp. 8–14).** Unmarked *legere* often means audible reading (Cicero, *Fam.* 9.20.3), while silent reading is marked as a special case (*tacitus legere*, Hor. *Sat.* 2.5.68; Suet. *Aug.* 39; *Nero* 15.1). The stock phrase *tacite legere* first occurs in Augustine (*Conf.* 6.3; fn. 26). The adverbs oppose "silent" to "especially loud", with no term for normal audibility, so *legere* already meant reading with the voice (p. 12).
2. **Script and schooling (pp. 14–21).** Grammarians defined the letter as the smallest part of articulate voice (Donatus, p. 15), and Quintilian describes reading as voice and eyes working at once (*Inst.* 1.1.34, p. 18). On *scriptio continua*: it was originally Greek; Latin divided words at first, but "Ab dem 2. Jahrhundert n. Chr. setzte sich jedoch auch für das Lateinische die Scriptio continua durch, von der man erst im Mittelalter wieder abging" (p. 19). Following Raible, reading aloud gives the unspaced text a second, auditory channel and so is "eine beträchtliche Erleichterung des Textverständnisses" ("a considerable aid to understanding", p. 20).
3. **Skilled and unskilled readers (pp. 21–28).** Most readers had elementary schooling only, so reading was for them "eine ziemlich schwerfällige Angelegenheit und oft eher ein Entziffern" ("a rather laborious business, often more deciphering", p. 25). Readers like Ambrose, practised enough to read silently despite *scriptio continua*, "waren sicherlich eine Ausnahme" ("were surely an exception", p. 27).
4. **Indirect witnesses (pp. 28–40).** Epitaphs that ask the passer-by to say farewell to the dead "only work" if read aloud (pp. 30–32). Ovid reads a friend's speech alone "lingua … properante per horas" (*Pont.* 3.5.7–10, p. 28). Cydippe, tricked by the inscribed apple, now reads "sine murmure" on purpose (*Her.* 21.1–2, p. 33). Augustus's order that reprimanded knights read their tablets *taciti* implies that aloud was the expected default (p. 37). Burnyeat's chief witness, Ptolemy's *On the Criterion* 5.1–2, describes silence only when readers dwell hard on a passage, so it marks silence as the special case (pp. 37–40).

He closes by endorsing Balogh's summary that ancient readers read aloud "in der Regel" and silent reading "war zwar nicht unerhört, doch immer eine Ausnahme" (p. 41), and rejects Gavrilov's claim that reading "is fundamentally the same in modern and in ancient culture" (p. 45).

## Relation to the argument

Busch bears on P1 and on the paper's handling of the silent-reading dispute (facet B). He sits between the two camps the draft names. He agrees with Knox, Gavrilov and Burnyeat that silent reading was possible and practised. He disagrees that it was ordinary, and he links reading aloud to *scriptio continua* the way Saenger does (fn. 107 calls Saenger's account of script and reading "überzeugend").

For the human-factors reading in §3.2, Busch's mechanism, sound held in auditory memory while the reader finds the word boundaries (p. 20), is close to the draft's line 111 ("hold ambiguous strings of sound while testing competing segmentations"). It is still a philologist's inference from Raible, not a measurement.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Busch is not cited in DRAFT_v2. It bears on these lines:
- Line 79 (*scriptio continua* "for more than a millennium"). **Narrows for Latin.** Busch dates Latin *scriptio continua* from the second century AD (p. 19), which agrees with Dickey (2017).
- Line 81 (Saenger argues reading was laborious and done by pronouncing or subvocalising; "Classicists dispute how heavy that burden was for trained readers (Heilmann, 2020)"). **Supports the draft's account of Saenger and adds a classicist on his side**: reading for most readers was "Entziffern" (p. 25), and reading aloud aided comprehension of *scriptio continua* (p. 20). The draft's framing implies classicists disagree with Saenger; Busch shows the field is split.
- Line 85 ("classical accounts show that skilled readers could and did read silently long before word spacing appeared (Knox, 1968; Gavrilov, 1997; Burnyeat, 1997; Heilmann, 2020)"). **Supports as worded, narrows any wider reading.** Busch concedes both "could" and "did": he accepts "without reservation" Gavrilov's finding that loud and silent reading both existed (p. 5). What he disputes is frequency. Readers practised enough to read silently were "surely an exception" (p. 27), and he rejects Burnyeat's reading of Ptolemy as proof that silent reading was normal (pp. 37–40). The sentence is defensible as written if "skilled" means a small elite. It should not be read as "silent reading was common".
- Line 111 (the paper's interpretation of boundary detection by sound). **Consistent in mechanism** (p. 20), with the same caveat the draft states: no study has measured it.

## Caution

A philological argument from word use and selected passages; it measures nothing. Busch's "Normalfall" is a claim about what speakers took for granted, and the passages he chooses (epitaphs, love elegy, imperial anecdote) are as open to rereading as those of his opponents. The article does not engage Johnson (2000), which appeared two years earlier but is not cited, and it predates Johnson (2010), McCutcheon (2015) and Heilmann (2021). So it does not answer the later revisionist case. Translations of German passages are mine.
