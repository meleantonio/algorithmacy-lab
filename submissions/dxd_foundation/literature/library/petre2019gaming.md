## What it argues
Platforms "routinely denigrate" visibility tactics "as gaming the system" (abstract); the authors find "the line between what platforms deem illegitimate algorithmic manipulation and legitimate strategy is nebulous and largely reflective of their material interests" (abstract). They "make no attempt to distinguish between algorithmic management and manipulation" (Introduction). Three metaphors do the moral work: organic versus artificial (Google's "Unnatural links to your site"), crime and deviance ("Podghazi", "Instagangs"), and games and cheating ("corking the bat") (Findings). The sharpest finding is a reversal: a Facebook best-practices page told publishers to "try using text prompts and calls-to-action in your posts to encourage engagement", which Facebook "subsequently labeled as 'engagement bait' and demoted later that same year" (The Work of Authenticity Metaphors). Platforms' advice to "create unique, relevant content that can naturally gain popularity" funnels strategic effort to paid promotion (same section). They call this "platform paternalism" and note the distributional edge: Rap Genius, venture-funded, recovered; freelancers who lost Instagress "struggled to find alternative affordable ways" (Conclusion).

## Relation to the argument
**P8 / D3 — qualifies the sentence's vocabulary.** "Game" is the platform's moral label for what, from the producer's side, is reaching an audience. The abstract uses "game" descriptively; the author should say so, or the sentence inherits the platforms' framing.

**Q1 — disclosure moves.** The one documented disclosure (Facebook's best-practices page) later became a demotion rule. Disclosure of what counts did not spare publishers; it dated.

**Q3.** The producers' tactics (SEO, reciprocal linking, pods) are efforts to be seen by audiences through the mediator, which is the coordination-cost reading, though the authors frame it as labour conditions and power.
