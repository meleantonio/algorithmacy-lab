## What it argues

The authors ask whether counterfactual explanations ("If John had drunk 3 units instead of 5 units, he would have been under the limit") help users understand an AI system better than causal explanations ("John was over the limit because he drank 5 units") or a bare description ("John was over the limit"), and whether categorical features are understood as easily as continuous ones (abstract, p. 1). Participants learned to predict whether a person was over or under the legal limit from five features: units (4 unique values), weight (36), duration (60), gender (2), stomach fullness (2) (pp. 6–11). In the test phase they saw no explanation and no feedback (p. 8).

Results (pp. 10–13). Explanation had a main effect on accuracy, F(2,124) = 5.63, p = .005, partial eta-squared .083. The counterfactual group (M = .636, SD = .08) beat the control (M = .590, SD = .08), p = .003, d = .22. The causal group (M = .614) differed from neither (p = .245 against counterfactual, p = .186 against control). Explanation did not interact with phase. Accuracy rose from training to test for the categorical features, gender t(126) = 5.626, d = .50, and stomach fullness t(126) = 4.430, d = .39, but not for units, weight or duration (p > .17), a pattern independent of explanation type (p. 11). Test accuracy ranked gender (.796) > stomach fullness (.675) > units (.615) > weight (.557) > duration (.549), the reverse of the number of unique values; the authors read this as diversity of values, "rather than some abstract ontological status of the feature" (p. 11). Subjective measures dissociate: counterfactual satisfaction M = 27.83 against causal 22.79, p = .002, d = .76; trust 26.15 against 20.21, p < .001, d = .88; the control group sat between and did not differ significantly from either (pp. 12–13).

The authors warn that an explanation that people like but that does not raise accuracy could make them "feel better about the AI system, without gaining any insight into why it made a prediction," like the illusion of explanatory depth (p. 14). They add that the accuracy rise for categorical features appears with experience and "we cannot attribute this effect to the provision of explanations" (p. 14).

## Relation to the argument

For facet H/I, this is a clean experimental case on the premise behind P9: aids that give people something to read show weaker, less consistent effects on what they do than on how they feel. Here the aid (a counterfactual) raised accuracy by about 4.6 percentage points over a description (d = .22), did not beat the causal wording, and raised satisfaction and trust far more than accuracy. It is a single-session test with a toy domain and no stakes. It supports the paper's caution against treating disclosure or explanation as the lever (ruling D3) only as one small result. It also gives a design-side lesson for the word-separation analogy: tasks differ in what the person must infer, and simple two-valued features were learned from experience while many-valued continuous ones were not, with or without explanation.

Facet check: the facet quotes the published abstract (three studies, N = 127, 136, 211) and says counterfactuals gave higher accuracy than controls but no more than causal explanations, with higher satisfaction and trust. The preprint's Study 1 agrees on each point. The facet's "bearing" line says counterfactual feedback "improves prediction"; the preprint puts the effect at d = .22 and the counterfactual–causal difference at p = .245, so the improvement is small and rests on one pairwise contrast.

## Caution

This is the first-study preprint. Cite the published article (three studies) for any claim beyond N = 127, and check its numbers against this card; they may differ. One toy domain, crowd workers paid £2.61, one session of 80 items, binary categorical features only (the authors say so, p. 15). Accuracy is measured on predicting the system, not on any decision with consequences. The categorical advantage is confounded with number of values and arose with practice, not from explanation. No expert-novice comparison.
