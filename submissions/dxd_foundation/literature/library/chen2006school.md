# Chen, Y., & Sönmez, T. (2006). School choice: An experimental study. *Journal of Economic Theory, 127*(1), 202–231. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jet.2004.10.006

## What it argues
The first laboratory comparison of the Boston mechanism (BOS) with the two strategy-proof alternatives, Gale–Shapley (GS) and top trading cycles (TTC). The paper documents that manipulation under BOS was openly advised in the field: Minneapolis's placement centre told parents to make "the first choice a true favorite and the other two 'realistic,' that is, strategic choices", and the Seattle and St. Petersburg press gave similar advice (pp. 204–205). Hypotheses: under BOS "a significant proportion of students will misrepresent their preferences"; under GS and TTC, being strategy-proof, "participants will reveal their preferences truthfully" (pp. 208–210), though the authors note their own earlier housing experiment found "about a third of the participants misrepresent their preferences under a variant of the TTC mechanism" (p. 210).

Result 1 (p. 215) and Table 4 (p. 216): proportions of truthful preference revelation were BOS 0.139, GS 0.722, TTC 0.500 in the designed environment and BOS 0.278, GS 0.556, TTC 0.431 in the random environment; BOS is significantly below both strategy-proof mechanisms, and TTC is significantly below GS in the designed environment (p = .003) and weakly in the random one (p = .068). Result 2: under BOS 70.8% received their *reported* top choice but only 28.5% their *true* top choice (p. 216). The authors then write: "even though both GS and TTC are strategy-proof, we have some misrepresentation of preferences. Since roughly 80% of the participants under Boston, 53% under TTC and 36% under Gale–Shapley misrepresent their preferences, it is important to analyze who manipulates" (p. 217). The commonest pattern is the "district school bias", ranking the safe school higher than in true preferences (Table 4 columns).

## Relation to the argument
The founding lab result behind the "people misreport even under strategy-proof rules" strand. It supplies the 36% (GS) and 53% (TTC) misrepresentation figures that later papers round to "about 30%" and that `hassidim2017mechanism` quotes as 36 percent. For the abstract's sentence: a strategy-proof rule cut manipulation from about 80% to 36–53% in one-shot play with no advice, which is a large but partial removal of the strategic task, and the residue took the shape of a habit carried over from the Boston game (rank the safe school high). That is the pattern the author's "game the mediator" clause describes, persisting after the rule no longer rewards it. The field quotations (pp. 204–205) are also evidence that under a manipulable rule the strategic work was pushed onto parents by the system's own advisers. Supports P10 in its narrow form; nothing on platforms or interested mediators.

## Caution
Partial read; the design section and efficiency results were not read, and the 2011 corrigendum concerns the efficiency comparison in Table 7, not the truth-telling rates cited here. One-shot play with no feedback and no advice; participants had no information about others' preferences, which later work (Pais and Pintér 2008) showed lowers truth-telling further when added. "Truthful" under GS and TTC counts only the ranking down to the district school (p. 215), so the rates are not directly comparable with full-list definitions elsewhere.
