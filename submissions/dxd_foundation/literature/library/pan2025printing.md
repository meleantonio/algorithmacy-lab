## What it argues

The study keeps Chinese text unspaced and marks word boundaries by colour instead, so the layout does not change. It asks whether older readers, whose acuity has declined, gain more from such cues than young readers (Introduction).

Results (Table 1): older adults' reading speed was 375 characters per minute in mono-color, 380 with word-coloring and 342 with nonword-coloring; young adults' was 516, 513 and 490. Nonword coloring hurt both groups, more so the older adults (Results, simple effects for gaze duration and total reading time). Word coloring helped only the older adults: "our results demonstrated benefits from the word segment condition over the mono-color baseline only for the older adults", with higher reading speed, fewer regressions and shorter total reading time, while "For the young adults, the differences between word segment and mono-color conditions were not significant (all p values > 0.05)" (Results). The reported p for the older adults' reading-speed gain is printed as "p = 0.20" beside t = 2.329, which looks like a typo for 0.020.

The authors are frank about size: "The observed benefit of a reading speed of five characters/min for the older adults was not gigantic" (Discussion). They locate the benefit in less rereading, "rather than initial saccade generation or lexical access" (Discussion). The abstract states that "both groups benefited overall", which the simple effects qualify. Their review notes that inserting spaces into Chinese "leads to rather mixed outcomes", while colour is "a less interfering visual cue" (Discussion), and that Japanese spacing benefits appear only for all-kana text (Introduction).

## Relation to the argument

This bears on P4 (mixed effects of added boundaries in unspaced scripts). It **supports P4 for skilled young adults** (no reliable gain from correct boundary cues) and **complicates the account of who gains**. The readers who gained were older adults with more years of reading unspaced Chinese, not fewer. The authors frame older readers' need in terms of reduced visual acuity (Introduction; opening of the Discussion). The specific mechanism they propose is less rereading, possibly linked to older readers' riskier reading strategy (Discussion). Note too that the older group had better vocabulary scores than the young (Method). For the paper, a boundary cue's value depends on the reader's perceptual state as well as on habit, and a cue can be delivered without spacing.

It also bears on P5: a boundary mark that changes nothing in the layout (colour) helped where spaces have given mixed results, which strengthens the representational point that what matters is the visible grouping, not white space as such.

## Caution

The cue is colour, not spacing, so the study tests boundary marking in general, not the historical convention. The materials were edited to remove segmentation ambiguity, which favours the cue. Older participants had well-preserved vocabulary and memory (Method), so the result may not hold for frailer readers. The abstract's "both groups benefited" overstates the young adults' result; cite the simple effects. The likely p-value typo should not be quoted.
