## What it argues

Simmel's claim is that the step from two to three members changes the form of a group, and that no later step does so again. "The addition of a third person completely changes" a dyad, "but … the further expansion to four or more by no means correspondingly modifies the group any further" (p. 138). "The triad is a structure completely different from the dyad but not, on the other hand, specifically distinguished from groups of four or more members" (p. 141). Three mechanisms carry the difference. In a dyad "there can be no majority which could outvote the individual. This majority, however, is made possible by the mere addition of a third member" (p. 137). Among three, "each one operates as an intermediary between the other two, exhibiting the twofold function of such an organ, which is to unite and to separate" (p. 135). And the triad opens three "typical group formations," which "are impossible if there are only two elements" and which, beyond three, "only expand in quantity but do not change their formal type" (p. 145).

The three formations fill chapter 4. (1) *The non-partisan and the mediator* (pp. 145–154). A mediating third "deprives conflicting claims of their affective qualities because it neutrally formulates and presents these claims to the two parties involved" (p. 147); each party must now argue in terms that can "win over even the mediator" (p. 147). Simmel separates the mediator, who "only guides the process of coming to terms," from the arbitrator, who "ends up by taking sides" (p. 148); when parties "choose an arbitrator, they relinquish this final decision" (p. 151). (2) *The tertius gaudens* (pp. 154–162; the translator's note 18, p. 154, glosses it "the third who enjoys"). Here the third uses "his relatively superior position for purely egoistic interests" (p. 154). Two variants: "either two parties are hostile toward one another and therefore compete for the favor of a third element; or they compete for the favor of the third element and therefore are hostile toward one another" (p. 155). "On the largest scale, the tertius gaudens is represented by the buying public in an economy with free competition" (p. 156). The advantage "derives from the fact that he has an equal, equally independent, and for this very reason decisive, relation to two others" (p. 159), and it "disappears quite generally the moment the two others become a unit" (p. 160). His labour example is exact: when unions fixed a standard wage for each job, the employer kept "the choice among the different labor groups" but could "no longer make any profitable use of it" (p. 161). (3) *Divide et impera* (pp. 162–169): the third "intentionally produces the conflict in order to gain a dominating position" (p. 162); employers "categorically refuse to negotiate with intermediary persons who do not belong to their own employees" (p. 164); at the limit the third "can make himself invisible between them, so to speak, so that the clash of the two is not against him but against one another" (p. 167).

The master-and-servants passage (p. 141) states the mechanism from the superior's side: "It is easier to keep two rather than one at a desired distance; in their jealousy and competition the master has a tool for keeping them down," a "transformation of a numerical into a qualitative difference."

## Relation to the argument

**P11 and ruling D9 (irreducible triad).** This is the oldest and clearest statement that a triad is not two dyads: the third's presence makes a majority, mediation and exploitation possible, and these forms "are impossible if there are only two elements" (p. 145). It supports "irreducible" in a *formal* sense: the properties belong to the configuration, not to any pair. It does not supply a measure, and Simmel's irreducibility is qualitative, not the quantitative Φ the author names (SOURCE.md D9; see `engel2018integrated`).

**The abstract's "mediator that binds and optimizes."** Simmel's vocabulary splits the author's single word. A mediator who "binds" is, in his terms, an *arbitrator* (the parties "relinquish this final decision," p. 151); one that "optimizes" for itself is a *tertius gaudens*. Vallas and Schor (2020, p. 282 of the published version) apply exactly that label to platforms (`vallas2020what`). The author's phrase therefore names a third that is arbitrator and interested party at once, which Simmel treats as distinct figures. That is a point to state, not a contradiction.

**Counter-reading.** Simmel's buying public is the paradigm *tertius gaudens* (p. 156): there the third is the dispersed consumer and the two competitors are suppliers. On a platform the roles are reassigned, and the party that "enjoys" is the one in the middle. His remedy, "objectification" of the disputed matter (p. 161), is close to the facet-P idea of a rule in the mediator that removes a strategic task.
