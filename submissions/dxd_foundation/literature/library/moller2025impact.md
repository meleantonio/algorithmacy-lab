# Møller, A. G., Romero, D. M., Jurgens, D., & Aiello, L. M. (2026). The impact of generative AI on social media: An experimental study. *Scientific Reports, 16*, 9376.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1038/s41598-026-40110-8 · **Read depth:** full_text (the unedited "Accepted Article in Press" manuscript, received 27 Oct 2025, accepted 10 Feb 2026, 26 PDF pages; this is not the version of record; the Supplementary Information it points to is not in the PDF and I did not read it; I cite PDF pages, which include two cover pages) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article (accepted manuscript, unedited) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** randomised controlled experiment on a custom Reddit-style discussion platform; 680 US participants recruited on Prolific in a US-representative sample (3–21 Jan 2025), put into groups of five, each group assigned to a control or one of four GPT-4o tools; about 130–140 participants per condition; three 10-minute discussions each (cats against dogs, oats, universal basic income) · **Parties modeled:** other (a peer group of five, each with an optional platform-supplied AI aid) · **Facets:** H · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
The four tools vary in initiative and task: Chat (an open sidebar assistant, reactive), Conversation Starter (proactive openers), Feedback (reactive comments on drafts) and Suggestions (three reply drafts agreeing, neutral or disagreeing; Methods, p. 18). Producer-side results (Fig. 2, p. 6; Table 1, p. 8):
- Chat and Suggestions raised the survey item "AI suggestions can make it more likely for me to participate in online discussions" (the text calls this willingness to engage): pre–post change 0.67 and 0.56 against 0.27 for control (Chat p = 0.004, d = 0.37; Suggestions p = 0.032, d = 0.28; N = 123 and 117).
- All four tools lengthened comments. Chat gave 28.6 words against 18.8 for control (p < 0.001, d = 0.43); Suggestions 27.8 (d = 0.52).
- Conversation Starter raised participation equality (normalised Shannon entropy 0.92 against 0.89; p = 0.028; d = 0.35).

Consumer-side, the authors write that "none of the AI interventions improve user perceptions compared to the control condition" (p. 7). Chat and Conversation Starter comments were rated less informative (Conversation Starter 3.79 against 4.08 on a 1–5 scale, p = 0.014; Chat 3.85, p = 0.060). Replies received were rated lower under Conversation Starter (3.39 against 3.63, p < 0.001) and, in the text, "all but" Suggestions. All treatments raised "Dislike" reactions (Chat 7% against 3% of reactions, p < 0.001). Participants called AI-assisted text "robotic" and "generic". The share of participants believed by others to use AI tools rose from 13.8% (control) to 37.7%–43.6% across treatments (p. 13).

Usage: Chat was used at least once by 94.4% of treated participants (n = 118; 960 prompts), Feedback by 74.8% (n = 98), Conversation Starter by 71.7% (n = 91), Suggestions by 64.3% (n = 83) (pp. 8–9). Participants chose the agreeing suggestion 48.6% of the time (582 of 1,197), more often in the oats and politics threads than in the cats thread (p. 12).

The authors infer a "duality": "although AI interventions broaden participation and increase the volume of content, they also risk creating 'semantic garbage'... and degrading the quality of subsequent human conversation once AI is introduced to a thread" (p. 7). They draw four design principles: optional use with disclosure of directly copied text, personalisation, contextual awareness, and familiar interfaces (pp. 13–15).

## Relation to the argument
**P9 and the triad.** Four tools that each hand one party something to use or read produced more content and, on the other parties' side, lower rated quality and more suspicion. The effect arises between the parties, not in the user's own reading, which supports facet H's note that a mediator editing one party's text changes how the others see them. No tool improved both sides: "no single AI tool enhances both producer and consumer experiences" (p. 4). That is the kind of evidence P9 asks for on aids that sit on one party's side.

**D3 (disclosure).** The authors' first principle is disclosure: "content directly copied from generative AI tools should be clearly labeled" (p. 14), though revising a draft with AI "may not require explicit labeling". They cite Gallegos et al. (2025), whose title reports that labelling AI-generated messages "does not reduce their persuasive effects" (reference [36]), without discussing the tension. Their own data show people already guess who used AI (13.8% to about 40%), with no labels in the experiment. The experiment never tested disclosure.

**Cuts the other way.** Feedback, the tool that asks the writer to refine their own text, saw "an increase in perceived value alignment" from 33.9% to 42.5% (p. 14). The authors read this as reflective personalisation helping. It is a single secondary result.

Facet check: facet H quotes the abstract, including "negative spill-over effect on conversations". The main text ties it to the lower-rated replies (Fig. 2d, Table 1) and to the inference that quality falls "even among users not using the AI themselves" (p. 13). I found no separate test of spill-over in the main text, and the supplement is unread, so treat the spill-over as the authors' inference from the reply ratings, not as a separately estimated effect.

## Caution
An unedited accepted manuscript; I did not see the supplement with prompts, regressions and demographic tests. Ten-minute, single-session, text-only discussions of three seeded topics; participants were told which tool they had but not whether other people's comments were AI-generated (p. 18). Within-group dependence is handled by bootstrap, and no comparison across tools is corrected for multiplicity that I could find. The paper's perceived-quality outcomes are ratings by other participants, not measured decision quality. It is a group of peers with a platform-supplied aid and no platform interest at stake, so it does not model an owner whose interest differs from the users'.
