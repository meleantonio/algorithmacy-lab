## What it argues

The authors ask whether marking sub-word units (syllables or phonemes) in the text itself speeds word recognition for skilled readers (§3). They test five cues (colour, connection, outline, underline, spacing between segments inside a word), each at both grain sizes, against unaltered text, with correct ("meaningful") and incorrect ("meaningless") segmentations (§§4, 6.2).

**Experiment 1** (§6; 48 participants analysed of 60 who finished; 500 trials each, 10 repetitions per condition, within-subjects). All ten hypotheses that a meaningful cue would beat plain text "were strongly rejected": "Phonologically meaningful visual segmentation impeded rather than enhanced Word identification" (§6.9, PDF p. 5). Word responses ran 49–66 ms faster than pseudoword responses, which they take as a check that the procedure can detect effects of that size (§6.9). Meaningless cues cost 25.33–83.89 ms (syllable underline least, syllable outline most). Meaningful against meaningless: a difference between −2.36 and 8.11 ms, probability 0.859 that meaningful cues were slower (RQ2). Cues differed from each other by 14.40–40.16 ms among meaningful ones and up to 56 ms overall. The authors sum up: "All interventions, meaningful or meaningless, impede word identification by between 25ms and 84ms" (§6.10, PDF p. 6). Only syllable outline beat its meaningless control, and the authors call that pattern "novel and surprising" (§9.1).

**Experiment 2** (§7) was underpowered; they do not report it. **Experiment 3** (§8; 95 analysed of 96; between-subjects; syllable underline against syllable outline; 120 training trials with a video of instructions and a secondary task that rewarded attending to, or ignoring, the segments). After training to attend, underline cost 22.69 ms and outline 54.41 ms against plain text; pre-training costs were 19.34 and 63.44 ms. Attend and ignore groups did not differ conclusively; a post-hoc power check says the design would have caught differences of 24.75 ms (underline) and 29.26 ms (outline). Plain-text performance improved 6.29 ms from pre-test to post-test (§8.9). Conclusion: "even with instruction and training, phonological segmentation impedes word identification" (§8.10, PDF p. 8).

**On inter-word spacing.** The introduction states: "According to Saenger [58], the addition of space between words in written text was a pivotal graphical innovation that led to faster, silent reading" (§1, PDF p. 1). The discussion qualifies it: "the introduction of inter-word spaces may have initially impeded readers who were already skilled with scriptura continua" (§9.1, PDF p. 9). The authors then list mixed results for Japanese [59] and Thai [69], and evidence that spaces added to Chinese "is disruptive to skilled readers [41], but helpful for children [68] and L2 learners [62]". They offer this as plausible for English sub-word cues, not as a finding.

## Relation to the argument

**Premises P3 and P4, and rulings D1, D2.** The source adds no test of word spacing. Its §9.1 paragraph restates the evidence behind P4 (fluent readers of unspaced scripts gain nothing from spaces; learners and children gain) and supplies the hypothesis that the same cost-for-the-practised, benefit-for-the-learner pattern applies to a new cue, which is the pattern the author's history needs a precedent for. Its experiments show a cost when practised readers meet a cue they have not learned, which cuts against any claim that a re-representation helps the reader it is imposed on. It is not evidence on the Roman or medieval case.

**Facet check:** the facet read only the abstract, the introduction and the start of the background, and says the introduction "states Saenger's account as settled" and that the authors may not discuss his critics. Fuller reading: §9.1 hedges the same account and notes the opposing evidence, though the paper never names Saenger's critics (it cites Saenger once). Second, the facet quotes the abstract's range "between 32.7ms—color technique—and 70.7ms—connection technique". Those two figures appear only in the abstract; I found neither in the body, whose ranges are 25–84 ms (Exp. 1), about 17–63 ms (Exp. 3) and "at least 17ms" in the conclusion (§10). The facet's other points hold: the paper tests sub-word cues, not word spacing, and "192 participants" is a count of collected datasets (48 + 48 + 96) that includes Exp. 2, whose results are withheld.

## Caution

A preprint, not the version of record. The task is lexical decision on isolated words, which the authors say "might not always be predictive of reading performance" (§9.3); the sample probably leans toward skilled readers and was not screened for reading ability; training lasted 120 trials and the authors "cannot rule out" that weeks of training would help (§9.3). The cues are sub-word, not inter-word, and the sample is modern English readers, so the paper cannot be cited for the effect of word spacing on ancient or medieval readers.
