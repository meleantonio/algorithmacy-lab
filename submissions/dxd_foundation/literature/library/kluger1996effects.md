## What it argues

From the abstract only. Kluger and DeNisi state that "Since the beginning of the century, feedback interventions (FIs) produced negative—but largely ignored—effects on performance." Their meta-analysis "(607 effect sizes; 23,663 observations) suggests that FIs improved performance on average (d = .41) but that over 1/3 of the FIs decreased performance." This "cannot be explained by sampling error, feedback sign, or existing theories" (abstract).

They propose feedback intervention theory (FIT), whose central assumption is that "FIs change the locus of attention among 3 general and hierarchically organized levels of control: task learning, task motivation, and meta-tasks (including self-related) processes." Moderator analyses suggest "FI effectiveness decreases as attention moves up the hierarchy closer to the self and away from the task," and these findings are "further moderated by task characteristics that are still poorly understood" (abstract).

## Relation to the argument

**Premise P9 and ruling D7 (understanding through deciding and quick results).** The result is a standing qualification of Convention B: feedback on one's own performance hurt performance in over a third of the effect sizes. The mechanism the authors offer is attention, not speed. Feedback hurts when it draws attention toward the self and away from the task. That suggests a design condition the paper could use: a convention that returns results should keep attention on the task (the consequence of a move) and away from judgements of the person. Whether the meta-analysis tested feedback *frequency* or *timing* as a moderator could not be checked from the abstract.

**What it does not show.** Nothing in the abstract concerns volatile or noisy environments, algorithms, or a third party. The setting is a person receiving information about their own task performance.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 366: "Similarly, Kluger and DeNisi (1996, 1998) showed in a meta-analysis that more than one-third of feedback interventions reduce performance. Providing uncurated, rapid feedback on a volatile algorithm hands the user a stream of noise." The first sentence matches the abstract ("over 1/3 of the FIs decreased performance"). Two refinements: the abstract counts 607 effect sizes, and "over 1/3 of the FIs" is presumably a share of those effect sizes rather than of feedback interventions as a population (the body, which would settle this, was not seen); and only the 1996 article is the meta-analysis, the 1998 piece reports it. The second sentence is the draft's own inference. It is **not** supported by what I could see: the abstract attributes the harm to attention moving toward the self, and says nothing about speed, curation or volatility. The draft should not let the citation carry that sentence.
