## What it argues
The binarized scoring rule (BSR) is incentive compatible for any decision maker who wants to maximise the chance of a prize, and participants are asked to report a known objective prior (an X-in-10 chance), so any deviation is a false report. In the Information treatment, which showed the exact state-contingent winning chances for each provisional report, false reports averaged 41.5 percent with no time trend, and 85 percent of participants misreported at least once (p. 6); false reports were 52.8 percent for non-centred priors against 24.6 percent at 0.5 (p. 7), and pulled toward the centre. Table 2 (p. 12): false-report rates were Information 0.415, RCL (a calculator that reduces the compound lottery) 0.325, No-Information 0.206, Feedback 0.217 in periods 1–2 rising to 0.341 in periods 9–10, Description 0.245. "The proportion of false reports when participants have no quantitative information on the incentives is half that in the Information treatment and 60 percent lower than in the RCL treatment (p<0.001 and p=0.031)" (p. 13). In Feedback, which started from the No-Information screen and revealed the earned winning chance after each period, false reports rose significantly (p = 0.003) and "reaches the Information-treatment level within four periods" (p. 15). A verbal, non-quantitative Description preserved truth-telling (24.5 percent, not different from No-Information, p = 0.474) (p. 17). Risk attitudes did not predict false reports except in RCL (fn. 11, fn. 22). Exit comments show deliberate hedging: "I kept my initial answers at 50% because you get a 75% chance of getting the $8 anyways" (fn. 12, p. 9). The authors state the dilemma: "we want participants to be fully apprised of the offered incentives... On the other, the point of the mechanism is to measure beliefs with minimal noise, and information on the quantitative incentives distorts the reported beliefs" (p. 16), and they note that "advocating for what amounts to a black box from the point of participants is jarring to the general philosophy of incentivized decision making" (p. 16).

## Relation to the argument
This is the strongest single-person result in the facet that *more* disclosure of a truthful mechanism's workings produced *more* gaming. People who could see the payoff table used it to hedge against the mechanism rather than to report the truth; those told only that honesty maximised their chances reported most truthfully. For ruling D3 the paper is direct support in a narrow setting: disclosure of the rule changed behaviour, and for the worse. For the abstract's "strategic offloading" clause it is a warning: a mechanism can be incentive compatible for all risk preferences and still invite strategising once its mechanics are legible, so legibility of the rule and relief from strategy pull apart. It also bears on the abstract's "feedback immediacy" clause with a counter-case: per-period outcome feedback here taught people to game (p. 15), because the feedback was about the payoff rule, not about the decision's object. The setting has no counterpart and no mediator with an objective, and the task is a one-shot report with a flat payoff maximum (p. 21), which the authors say limits the cost of mistakes.

## Caution
Read as the 2020 working paper under its earlier title; the AER article may differ in framing, numbers and section order, and the card's locators are to the working paper. A belief-elicitation task, not a matching or market mechanism. Student subjects and small stakes. The result concerns quantitative incentive information in a particular interface; the authors' "Description" treatment shows that a verbal account of the rule need not do harm.
