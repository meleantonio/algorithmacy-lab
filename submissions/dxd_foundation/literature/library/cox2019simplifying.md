## What it argues
Google announced that it would "transition publisher inventory to a unified first price auction for Google Ad Manager", to be "complete by the end of this year". The stated reason is complexity: "a single ad can pass through a mix of auctions, with different rules, before a winning bid price is selected... This complexity has made it difficult for advertisers and agencies to properly value programmatic inventory and it has driven our publishers and app developers to implement increasingly complicated ad monetization strategies, reducing transparency across the industry." Under the new rule "An advertising buyer's bid will not be shared with another buyer before the auction or be able to set the price for another buyer. The buyer that wins the auction pays the price they bid." The post concedes the change moves strategic work rather than removing it: "the change from second to first price will require both buyers and sellers to make changes in their programmatic strategies... publishers and app developers will need to rethink how they use price floors and technology partners will need to adjust how they bid." The change excluded Google Search, AdSense for Search and YouTube.

## Relation to the argument
Question 4's third case, and the one where the mediator says in its own words why it changed the rule. Two points. First, the move was *away* from a second-price rule (the strategy-proof family) toward first price, the format `akbarpour2020credible` shows is the only credible static one; Google's own wording ("will not be shared with another buyer... or be able to set the price for another buyer") is a credibility promise, not a simplicity-for-bidders promise. Second, the post states that users must now "rethink" and "adjust" their strategies; the strategic task was redistributed, not offloaded. For the abstract this is a counter-case to any suggestion that platforms converge on rules that free users from strategy: this one moved to a rule that requires bid shading, because the layered second-price system had become ungovernable and untrusted. It supports the triad framing (the mediator optimises for its own position in a complex market) and cautions against reading "simplifying" in a mediator's mouth as relief for the parties.
