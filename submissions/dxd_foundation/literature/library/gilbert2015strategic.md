# Gilbert, S. J. (2015). Strategic offloading of delayed intentions into the external environment. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 68*(5), 971–992. https://doi.org/10.1080/17470218.2014.972963

**Identifier:** doi:10.1080/17470218.2014.972963 · **Read depth:** full_text (the open-access publisher PDF, CC BY 3.0, "Published online: 18 Nov 2014", 22 pages in the online-first pagination 1–22, from UCL Discovery; journal pagination 971–992 not shown on the copy read) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** four online experiments on Amazon Mechanical Turk, N = 1,196 in total (Experiment 1a, 100; 1b, 100; 2a, 439 analysed of 675; 2b, 557 analysed of 826); a drag-the-circles task with optional reminders; a naturalistic follow-up task over one week · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (a person and an external reminder) · **Facets:** Y · **Read:** 2026-10-15

## What it argues
The paper introduces "intention offloading": "acting on one's environment to create perceptual triggers for delayed intentions" (p. 16). "Yet strategic offloading of intentions to the external environment is not typically permitted in laboratory tasks" (abstract, p. 1). In the task, participants could drag target circles toward their intended location at the start of a trial, so that "the locations of the target circles themselves represent the intention, providing a perceptual trigger" (p. 3). Experiment 1a: externalising was more frequent with three targets than one, F(1, 98) = 43, p < .001, η² = .31, and with an interruption, F(1, 98) = 5.2, p = .025; the distribution was bimodal, "individual participants tended to either always or never externalize" (p. 5). Experiment 1b removed the option: interruption then lowered accuracy, F(1, 98) = 10.4, p = .002, whereas with offloading allowed in 1a it had not, so "participants can eliminate the costs of interruption by offloading intentions" (p. 7). Experiments 2a and 2b: accuracy was higher in the offloading than the non-offloading groups (2a, F(1, 437) = 4.8, p = .029; 2b, F(1, 555) = 71.7, p < 10⁻¹⁵) (p. 11); the task uniquely predicted claiming three $0.25 bonuses on named days up to a week later (standardised β = .13, t(988) = 3.8, p = .00017), "albeit reflecting a weak effect size (η² = .022)" (pp. 12, 14); 47% claimed no bonus despite intending to claim all three (p. 10). Older participants externalised more (t(616) = 3.0, p = .003), with caveats (pp. 16–17). The discussion frames the results as metacognition: "metacognitive awareness of the likelihood of forgetting... prompted compensatory offloading" (p. 5), and places the work within distributed cognition (Clark; Hutchins; Kirsh 1995, 1996) (p. 2). Intention offloading "might be seen as an extreme form of implementation intention, where... the agent physically interacts with the world to create a perceptual cue" (p. 17).

## Relation to the argument
This card exists to settle a terminological point. "Strategic offloading" is an established term in cognitive psychology, used in this title and in later work (Engeler and Gilbert 2020; Murphy 2023), where it means a person's own metacognitively guided choice to put a memory demand into the environment. Li (2017) does not use the phrase, and his concept concerns a designer's choice of mechanism form, not a person's choice to offload. The abstract's "strategic offloading (Li, 2017)" therefore borrows a printed term with a different meaning and attaches it to a source that does not use it. The author should either rename the move (Li's own words are "simplicity" and avoiding "contingent reasoning") or cite the offloading literature for the term and Li for the mechanism. On substance the paper is friendly to the abstract: it shows that letting people move the representation of an intention into the environment raises performance and that people deploy it adaptively, which is the Kirsh–Maglio epistemic-action family the abstract also cites (Gilbert cites Kirsh 1995, 1996, pp. 2, 20). Risko and Gilbert (2016), already carded (`risko2016cognitive`), is the review that grew from this line.

## Caution
Online samples with short retention intervals (5–26 s); the author says it is "debatable" whether the task is prospective memory or working memory (p. 15) and that the naturalistic measure is "exceedingly noisy" (p. 14). The copy read is the online-first pagination; the journal's 971–992 page numbers were not available. Nothing here concerns mechanisms, markets or strategic interaction; the term "strategic" means adaptive to task demands.

## Also used by facet V (2026-10-15)

Facet V read the Europe PMC full text (PMC4448673) independently and takes three things the card above does not stress. (1) A terminological collision: the author's abstract writes "strategic offloading (Li, 2017)", but in the offloading literature the phrase is this paper's, and it names the agent's own metacognitively guided choice to externalise an intention; Li's paper does not use the phrase and his move is made by the mechanism designer, not the agent. The draft should cite both senses or rename the Li move. (2) Experiment 1b against 1a: interruption lowered accuracy only when offloading was impossible, F(1, 98) = 10.4, η² = .096, so offloading "can eliminate the costs of interruption" (Exp. 1b, Discussion). (3) The naturalistic link is weak by the author's own account, η² = .022, and 47% of participants who intended to claim all three bonuses claimed none. All of it is person–artefact; no counterpart, no mediator.
