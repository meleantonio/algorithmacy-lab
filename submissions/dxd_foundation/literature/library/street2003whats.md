## What it argues
Street sets out to update NLS, the tradition that treats literacy "as a social practice" (p. 77), by reviewing recent critiques and what they mean for practice.

The core is a contrast between two models of literacy (p. 77). In the "autonomous" model, "literacy in itself--autonomously--will have effects on other social and cognitive practices." Introducing literacy to "poor, 'illiterate' people" is then expected to improve their skills, prospects and citizenship "regardless of the social and economic conditions that accounted for their 'illiteracy' in the first place". Street says this model "disguises the cultural and ideological assumptions that underpin it". The "ideological" model "posits instead that literacy is a social practice, not simply a technical and neutral skill" (p. 77). He adds that "It is not valid to suggest that 'literacy' can be 'given' neutrally and then its 'social' effects only experienced afterwards" (p. 78). NLS therefore avoids "literacy" as a unit of study and uses "literacy events" and "literacy practices" instead (pp. 78–79).

Pp. 79–82 review four lines of critique. Brandt and Clinton (2002) warn against "the limits of the local": "Literacy practices are not typically invented by their practitioners" (p. 79, quoting them), and consolidating technologies from outside can unsettle local uses. Street agrees about the caution but refuses to label distant literacies "autonomous" because they are "also always ideological", and says local and global meet to make "a new hybrid" (pp. 79–80). Collins and Blot ask for general claims beyond piled-up local cases (pp. 80–81). Bartlett and Holland add Bourdieu's habitus and "figured worlds" (pp. 81–82). Pahl and Hornberger extend the field to multimodality and biliteracy (p. 82).

On practice (pp. 82–85), Street cites Hull and Schultz on the link between school and home literacies, a Nepal programme that builds on what groups already do, and a view of learning as "continuing processes, not a one-off shift from 'illiteracy' to 'literacy', from dark to light" (p. 85). On policy (pp. 85–87), he warns that governments in the UK and US now demand "scientific" evidence of the randomized, systematic-review kind, which ethnographic work cannot supply (Slavin, the National Reading Panel, the What Works Clearinghouse). He reports critics (Hamersley, Larson, Berliner, Erickson and Gutierrez) who link the trend to a resurgent positivism. He ends by asking the field to "analyze and contest what counts as 'literacy'" and to ask about the "limits of the local" (p. 87).

## Caution
A review article from 2003 on literacy in education and development, written for readers of comparative education. It is not evidence about reading history or interface design and makes no claim about either. It describes other scholars' critiques more than it argues a single thesis. It cannot be cited as saying that no layout convention affects reading.
