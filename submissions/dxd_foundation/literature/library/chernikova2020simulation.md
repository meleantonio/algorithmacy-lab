## What it argues

Chernikova and colleagues ask whether practising complex professional skills in a simulation raises learning, and whether the support wrapped around a simulation (examples, prompts, reflection phases) adds anything. They count a study only if it measured learning objectively (tests, expert ratings, behaviour counts) and compared simulation with a waiting control, with a differently taught control, or with a pre-test baseline (pp. 507–508).

The overall effect is large: Hedges' g = 0.85 (SE 0.08, 95% CI [0.69, 1.02], k = 145, N = 10,532). It is similar against waiting controls (g = 1.02, k = 16), differently instructed controls (0.82, k = 53) and pre-test baselines (0.88, k = 76) (p. 513). Heterogeneity is very high, I² = 95.86%, and the coded moderators explain only 4% of it (pp. 513, 526). Medicine supplies 126 of the 145 studies; teacher education 7, other fields 12 (p. 512).

By skill, communication (g = 0.44) and teamwork (0.50) gain least; technical performance (1.06) most (Table 1, p. 514). By form, document-based simulations are weakest (0.31) and mixed forms strongest (1.56); live models score 2.27 on only 3 studies (pp. 513, 518). Higher technology support goes with larger effects: simulators (medical) 1.07 and virtual reality 0.85 against screen-based 0.68 and simulations without technology 0.74 (p. 518). High-authenticity simulations beat low (0.86 against 0.58), though even low ones are large.

On support, the abstract says learners with low prior knowledge "learned better when supported by examples" and those with high prior knowledge gained more from reflection (p. 499). The body is more guarded. Examples present or absent differ by 0.88 against 0.81, not significantly (p. 519); prompts present are significantly lower than absent (0.65 against 0.92); reflection makes no difference (0.78 against 0.80). The prior-knowledge interactions are patterned but often non-significant: reflection in familiar against unfamiliar contexts is 0.74 against 0.49, p = .13 (p. 521). The authors call the added value of scaffolding "surprisingly small" (p. 524).

Two sentences matter for the engagement question. The authors read the size of the effect partly as feedback: "One of the issues in higher education is the lack of feedback in the context of complex authentic activities. Simulations typically address exactly this issue" (p. 522). They add: "One contributing factor may be that in many simulations learners can find out the correct strategy themselves, by trial and error if needed" (p. 524). Feedback "was not explicitly coded" (p. 512).

## Relation to the argument

D7 (understanding through deciding). This is the strongest measured support in facet I for learning by acting, and it should be cited with its conditions. The simulations are approximations of practice with reduced complexity; the learner can try, fail safely and see results; and the regularities are fixed and designed. The authors credit the "approximation-of-practice approach" (p. 523). That differs from a triad whose mediator adapts to both sides and offers no practice space; the flag in D7 about Part 1's point on adaptive intermediaries stands.

Feedback confound. The paper cannot separate acting from the feedback that acting brings; it never coded feedback. A reader of D7 should not cite g = 0.85 as the effect of deciding alone.

P9. Support aids (prompts, hints) did no better than none, and prompts did worse on average. That is mild support for the claim that giving the learner something to read adds little once practice is available.

Facet check: facet I took its bearing from the abstract. The scaffolding-by-prior-knowledge claim is weaker in the body, as shown above. The facet's pairing with Lavoie et al. (transfer) is not made here: Chernikova does not cite Lavoie or Norman, Dore & Grierson, and its outcome tests do not separate transfer to practice.

## Caution

Mostly medical education, with 126 of 145 studies; the authors say the findings do not "straightforwardly generalize" to other domains (p. 525). The paper does not report whether outcomes were measured in practice or inside the simulated task, so it says nothing on transfer. Heterogeneity is extreme, so moderator results are suggestive. Nothing here concerns an interested third party or an adaptive system.
