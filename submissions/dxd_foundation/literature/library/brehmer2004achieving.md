# Brehmer, B., & Nählinder, S. (2004). Achieving what cannot be done: Coping with the time constants in a dynamic decision task. *Proceedings of the 22nd International Conference of the System Dynamics Society.*

**Identifier:** https://proceedings.systemdynamics.org/2004/SDS_2004/PAPERS/236BREHM.pdf · **Read depth:** full_text (conference paper PDF, 12 pages as saved, no printed page numbers; locators are sections) · **Source-tier:** proceedings · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** two laboratory experiments with the NEWFIRE forest-fire microworld; Experiment I N = 10 undergraduates (within-subjects, 28 scenarios), Experiment II N = 40 undergraduates (two between-subject conditions, 20 scenarios each) · **Parties modeled:** person–system (one fire chief controlling simulated units) · **Facets:** I · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues

Brehmer and Nählinder ask how people cope with the time constants of a dynamic task: units take time to reach a fire, and the fire spreads meanwhile. Earlier NEWFIRE work showed that people "quickly learn[] to respond rapidly and massively to a fire," while dead time and delayed reports caused "considerable problems" (Introduction). The authors ask whether the rapid response is calibrated or only a blanket rule.

Experiment I (N = 10; 8 close fires needing 1 unit and 8 distant fires needing 4, plus 12 fillers) let participants move units before ignition. Participants issued 8.35 commands for distant fires against 6.36 for close fires (F 1/143 = 16.23), which the authors read as 4.2 units sent to distant fires and 3.2 to close fires, where 4 and 1 sufficed. They lost 1 forest cell to close fires but 5.7 to distant fires, against an expected 4. Only 2 of 280 trials (0.7%) lost the base. The 10 participants issued on average 15.03 commands before the fire started, enough to move almost every unit.

Experiment II (N = 40) barred pre-ignition moves and added a cost display. Participants no longer separated the two fire types: units sent in the first period rose from 1.6 in block 1 to 2.5 in block 4, "what should be expected if the participants had sent 1 FFU to the one-FFU fires and 4 FFUs to the four FFU-fires," yet no difference between fire types appeared. The base was lost in 84 of 800 scenarios (11%). "Good" participants sent more units early than "bad" ones, not better-discriminated numbers (Results, Individual analyses). The authors conclude that participants "employ a simple qualitative heuristic rule, and avoid coping with the quantitative details" (Discussion, Exp. II), and that prepositioning units "makes it unnecessary to learn the time constants." They add that people may "fail in such tasks, not because they do not understand the dynamics, but because they cannot handle the quantitative details," and call for "separate indicators" of the two.

## Relation to the argument

Ruling D7 says people "understand THROUGH deciding and quickly ingesting the results." This paper complicates that for tasks with feedback delay. Participants acted many times, improved overall (significant block effects in both experiments), and still did not learn the specific time constant; performance came from a coarse rule that made the delay irrelevant. Acting produced competence without the understanding the quantitative structure would give. Part 1's point that an adaptive mediator disrupts the rapid, unequivocal feedback learning needs gains indirect support: here the feedback is slow and the learning is correspondingly shallow. The finding that prepositioning (a change to the task's set-up before the decision) beat learning also sits near the paper's design-over-training line.

## Caution

A short conference paper by two authors with student samples and a microworld; there is no population of practitioners. The two experiments differ in more than the prepositioning rule (update rate 20 s against 10 s, a cost display, a different scenario mix, longer sessions), which the authors discuss only for update rate. The text gives the Experiment II final block as 38 cells lost in Results and 34 in Discussion; cite neither without checking. The reported F values (for example F 1/19 for a 40-person comparison) look inconsistent with the stated design, and the text carries typographical slips. Participants may have been satisfied with a costly strategy; the authors say they cannot tell inability from satisficing.
