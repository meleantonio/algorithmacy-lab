**Identifier:** doi:10.14763/2025.2.2007 · **Read depth:** full_text (the journal's PDF, 33 pp. with references; read whole; locators are the PDF's section numbers and printed page numbers) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article (open access, CC BY 3.0 DE) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** conceptual analysis of two cases, the myFICO paid credit-score service and the announced-then-abandoned Tinder Concierge, with public sources; no data · **Parties modeled:** person–system (user and platform), with other users as competitors in dating · **Facets:** U · **Read:** 2026-10-15

## What it argues
Platforms "often use this gaming concern to justify their reluctance to provide algorithmic transparency" (abstract, p. 1). The authors describe "gaming-the-system-as-a-service (GaaS)", in which "transparency promises are wrapped into an assisted gaming service and sold as a premium feature" (p. 1). myFICO sells credit reports at $19.95 for one bureau or $59.85 for three, showing the score's five components and weights (payment history 35%, amounts owed 30%, length of history 15%, new credit 10%, credit mix 10%) and a simulator of 24 actions (§3.1, pp. 11–14; prices and weights p. 12, simulator p. 14). Tinder Concierge was announced in March 2020 at $20–50 a month as access to "our team of experts who will help you craft the perfect profile" (§3.2, p. 16). They distinguish technical from "embodied" transparency, understanding gained "through direct interaction with a system" (p. 3), and quote DSA Article 27 on recommender-system parameters (§2.1, p. 6).

Three objections: GaaS "introduces an incentive for maintaining (or even introducing new) levels of opaqueness as a necessary precondition for offering GaaS" (§4.1, pp. 20–21); it is pay-to-win, so unequal (§4.2); and it commodifies transparency, which "may become a luxury affordable only to the wealthy" (§4.3, p. 26). Draws on `cofone2019strategic` for the proxy layers (§2.3, pp. 8–9).

## Relation to the argument
**P8 / D3 and the abstract's sentence.** The cases show platforms selling the very thing mandated disclosure is meant to give for free: help in gaming the mediator. That supports the sentence from an unexpected side. If gaming help is a product, disclosure has not removed the need to game; it has priced it.

**Q3 — coordination cost.** Tinder users pay to reach other users through the ranking; the authors frame the fee as a cost of access to "dates, love, intimacy" (p. 22). That is the abstract's coordination cost made explicit, in a dating rather than a labour market.

**Limits.** Two cases, one of them never launched; no measurement.

## Caution
A first exploration by the authors' own description; prices and features come from press and company pages at the time of writing. Nothing here measures whether paid transparency improves outcomes for buyers.
