## What it argues

The study asks whether advanced second-language (L2) readers of Chinese resolve temporary segmentation ambiguities the way native (L1) readers do, and whether colour-marked word boundaries help them (Introduction 1.3–1.4).

L2 readers read "about twice as slowly": sentence reading time 7,823 against 3,126 ms, 27.93 against 12.37 fixations, forward saccades of 0.55 against 3.47 characters, a pattern the authors call "a cautious character-by-character reading strategy" (Results 3.1). Comprehension was high in both groups (92% L1, 86% L2; Results).

Colour cues did little at sentence level. L1 readers were numerically faster with mono-color text (3,000 ms) than with either colour scheme (3,255 and 3,122 ms); L2 readers were numerically faster with colour (7,764 and 7,691 ms against 8,012; Table 4). The authors conclude that "the mean differences were small and unlikely to reflect meaningful improvements in reading efficiency" (Discussion 4.1). At the target word, colour that cued the wrong (embedded-word) analysis slowed L1 readers, while L2 readers showed "limited sensitivity to color cues, with minimal facilitation or disruption" (Conclusions). Their broader reading of the colour literature: "the facilitative effects of text color may be more limited than previously claimed. Rather than broadly enhancing word segmentation, text color may exert its strongest influence when it disrupts segmentation" (Discussion 4.3). The introduction states that skilled L1 Chinese readers read at "approximately 250 words per minute, comparable to reading speeds for spaced alphabetic scripts like English (Liversedge et al., 2016)" (Introduction).

## Relation to the argument

This bears on P4, and in particular on its learner clause, which the ARGUMENT_MAP marks as resting on abstracts (`[abs]`). For high-proficiency L2 readers, boundary cues gave no meaningful gain, which **supports** the draft's claim that adult L2 readers do not reliably gain. The study also **narrows** the inference from "unspaced script" to "harder to read": skilled L1 readers of an unspaced script read about as fast as readers of spaced English, on the authors' cited figure. Boundary marking, on this evidence, can disrupt more readily than it helps. For P5 this is a caution: a re-representation that mismarks units does harm, and one that marks them correctly may add little for a reader who already segments well.

## Caution

Colour, not spacing, is the cue; the study asks about segmentation ambiguity, not about spacing as a convention. The L2 group was heterogeneous in first language, though more than half (34 of 60) were Vietnamese (Table 1), and highly proficient, so the null says nothing about beginners. The 250 words-per-minute figure is the authors' citation of Liversedge et al. (2016), which was not checked here. Sentence-level means in the text (e.g., 4,857 vs 4,926 ms) average over groups and should not be confused with the group means in Table 4.
