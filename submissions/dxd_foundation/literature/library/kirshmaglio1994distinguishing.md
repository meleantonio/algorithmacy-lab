**Identifier:** doi:10.1207/s15516709cog1804_1 · **Read depth:** full_text (UCSD author copy, OCR text, 36 pages; journal pagination printed in the running heads and used below; pp. 513–549 read in full including the reference list; figures and the Figure 7–8 axes are garbled by OCR and were read only through their captions and text) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** observational keystroke records of Tetris players at intermediate, advanced and expert levels (number of players not stated in the text I read), a computational model (RoboTetris), and a pilot mental-rotation experiment with 3 players · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (one player, one game; no counterpart) · **Facets:** E, I · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues

Kirsh and Maglio split actions in two. Pragmatic actions bring an agent "closer to his or her physical goal"; epistemic actions are "physical actions that make mental computation easier, faster, or more reliable", and the agent performs them "to change his or her own computational state" (epistemic definition p. 513, "own computational state" p. 514, pragmatic definition p. 515). A third category is excluded by scope: they study "ordinary actions—not sensor actions" (p. 515). An epistemic action reduces the space, time or error-rate of a mental computation (p. 514).

They build a classical four-phase model of Tetris cognition (bitmap, chunking, placement search, motor plan; pp. 518–522) and show it fails on the data. If players planned before acting, rotations per falling piece would average half the possible rotations (1.5 for a four-orientation piece); the observed averages are higher for every piece type (Figure 6, p. 523), and rotation "begins in earnest by 400 to 600 ms" (Figure 7 caption, p. 524), at times before 100 ms (p. 525). Letting the phases overlap does not rescue the model, because a false start costs between 75 ms and 750 ms (shortest interkeystroke interval about 75 ms, average about 250 ms; p. 526).

They then give five epistemic uses of rotation (p. 527). Early rotation exposes the hidden part of a piece 150 to 300 ms sooner (p. 527), and players rotate more when the partial image is ambiguous in both shape and column, though "no player reported noticing that zoids begin falling in different columns" (p. 529). Physical rotation takes "as few as 100 ms" against "800 to 1200 ms" for mental rotation, an estimate "based on pilot data" from three players (one intermediate, one advanced, one expert), eight presentations per pair (p. 530–531; allowing 200 ms for pressing the key does not close the gap, p. 532). Rotation can also shrink the attentional decision tree to depth two (Figures 12–13, p. 537) and ease matching (p. 538). One epistemic translation exists: in about 1% of drops, players move a piece to the wall and back before dropping it, and the routine follows higher drops; mean drop height after the routine is 19.04, 19.33 and 20.05 squares against 13.18, 13.69 and 15.65 for ordinary drops, for intermediate, advanced and expert players (Table 1, p. 541; each pair differs at α = .05).

In the Discussion they extend the point: a novice chess player "usually finds it helpful to physically move a chess piece when thinking about possible consequences" (p. 545), and "Experts keep their costs lower, in part, by performing more epistemic actions" (p. 547).

## Relation to the argument

The card bears mainly on D7 (understanding through deciding and quickly ingesting results) and on the vocabulary in facets E and I.

For D7, it supports a narrow form. Acting to see what happens is cheaper than computing it in the head, and skilled players do more of it, as the authors write on p. 547. The authors name the condition that makes it work: "the local effects of an action are totally determinate. There are no hidden states, exogenous influences, or other agents to change the result of hitting the rotate key" (p. 543). A platform that adapts to the person, or a counterpart who answers differently each time, lacks all three. The source therefore cuts against carrying D7 over to coordination through an algorithmic third party, and it agrees with Part 1's point that an adaptive intermediary breaks the rapid, unequivocal feedback that learning by acting needs. The pilot comparison (100 ms against 800–1200 ms) is a latency result that depends on a near-instant reply.

For the word-separation precedent, the agent changes its own display. The scribe changes someone else's page before the reader acts, so the term does not name the scribe's move (facet E states this; it is the project's inference, not Kirsh and Maglio's).

Facet check: facet E's reading (epistemic action is something the agent does to its own task) matches pp. 513-514. Facet I's three quotations are exact. Its page ranges are slightly wide (the chess passage is p. 545, not 543–546; "Experts keep their costs lower" is p. 547). Facet I says the player "already knows" the rules; the paper supports this through p. 543 but also notes that players do not know they use the column cue (p. 529). Facet I does not give the sample size behind the 800–1200 ms figure: three players, a pilot, "we estimate" (p. 530).

## In conversation with

`hutchins1995cognition` is not cited; the paper cites Hutchins (1990), "The technology of team navigation" (p. 514), as an example of external supports in navigation, which is a different work. `norman1993things` is likewise not the cited book: Kirsh and Maglio cite Norman (1988), *The psychology of everyday things*, for interface research on internal and external structure (p. 514). The bib's other Kirsh entries (`kirsh1995intelligent`, `kirsh1996adapting`) are his later papers; this text does not cite them.

Leads (own list, not in the bib): Shepard and Metzler (1971) mental rotation; Tarr and Pinker (1989), orientation dependence in shape recognition (p. 533); Stigler (1961), "The economics of information", as the cost-benefit model (p. 547); Reason (1990), *Human error* (cited as a skill-acquisition account the data do not fit, p. 518); Ambros-Ingerson and Steel (1987), interleaved planning (p. 524).

## Caution

Do not cite it for Tetris sample size (not stated in the text I read) or as evidence that acting reveals the structure of an unknown or changing system; the authors tie the effect to a fully determinate game. The rotation-time comparison rests on a pilot of three players, and the authors admit epistemic and pragmatic motives often cannot be separated (p. 527). Several uses of rotation (decision trees, retrieval, matching) are proposals, labelled as conjecture.
