## What it argues

A learning trap is "the formation of a stable false belief even with extensive experience" (p. 1553). Rich and Gureckis argue that such traps come from the interaction of choosing and learning: when feedback arrives only for the options one acts on, early generalisation stops the exploration that would correct it. The paper reviews three kinds. In the hot stove effect (Denrell & March), a stochastic but good prospect looks bad early and is avoided: "Avoiding the option appears to be the safest strategy, but it is exactly the strategy that prevents learning the true structure of the environment" (p. 1554). Underweighting of rare events and similarity-based avoidance are the other two (pp. 1554–1555).

The new trap is attentional. When two features jointly decide whether a prospect is good, a learner who attends early to one feature avoids every prospect carrying it and so never receives the feedback that would reveal the second (Figure 1, p. 1556). In simulation, over a quarter of agents with contingent feedback and selective attention fell into the trap, while agents given random feedback on half the trials did not; the trap "occurred due not to an overall poverty of information but to a specific pattern of behavior that prevented information from being gained" (p. 1558).

People fell into it more than the model did. In Exp. 1 (beehive task), the test-phase one-dimensional rule score was .83 with contingent feedback against .75 with full feedback, and the two-dimensional (correct) score .72 against .82; contingent participants thought 37.6% of hives dangerous against 28.2% for full-feedback participants, when the truth was 25% (pp. 1560–1561). Exp. 2 (job applications, 128 learning trials) replicated this: 1D score .86 against .75, 2D score .78 against .87, believed unsuitable 36.5% against 24.4% (p. 1565). Of the contingent participants who said they had "learned completely", 57.6% (19 of 33) were in the trap (p. 1565). Three interventions (individuating prospects, occluding features, outcome noise on 10% of trials in the first five blocks) did not help. Occlusion "appeared to have little" effect; individuation and noise lowered 1D scores only by also lowering 2D scores, "a hollow victory" (p. 1567), and "none of the interventions allowed people to obtain the accurate understanding of the environment possible through receiving full feedback following each choice" (p. 1567). The authors' closing point on active learning: "If the learner can choose to avoid stimuli that are expected to be unpleasant, active learning may lead to worse learning outcomes than passive exposure" (p. 1568).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D7 and premise P9 (understanding through deciding).** This is the clearest experimental evidence I found that learning by acting can settle on a false model even when the environment is deterministic and feedback is immediate and unequivocal. The task met both of Kahneman and Klein's conditions as the draft states them, and people still learned the wrong rule because their own choices cut off the feedback. The draft's §5.3 places the failure of learning-by-acting in noise, delay and hidden rules supplied by the mediator; Rich and Gureckis place a large share of it in the learner's own choice-contingent sampling. That cuts against the draft's framing, not against its conclusion that the mediator should change the feedback.

**Premise P10, second candidate (Convention B).** The study also supports one specific form of the convention. The cure that worked was "full information": being told what an avoided option would have yielded. That is close to a pre-commit sandbox that shows how the mediator would have treated moves the person did not make. Speed was not the variable; coverage of untried options was.

- Line 368 (pre-commit sandboxes): supports by analogy. Full foregone-outcome feedback was the only condition that gave accurate learning (pp. 1561, 1565, 1567). This is a single-agent lab task, not a mediated triad.

## Caution

Online samples, artificial categories, small bonuses and short sessions; one trap type (attentional) tested in depth. Choice-contingent feedback in the lab is a model of, not evidence about, platform workers. The paper does not test a mediator that changes its rules, delays feedback or has its own objective. The interventions tested were designed to slow generalisation, not to increase exploration, so the null results do not cover exploration-raising designs.
