## What it argues

Consenting users were randomly kept on the default ranking ("Algorithmic Feed") or moved to reverse-chronological ordering ("Chronological Feed"). The authors note that chronological order "technically constitutes an algorithm", but it "is not predictive" and applies "the same rule … to all users at all times" (PDF pp. 1, 2). About 80% of the material users saw was affected by the manipulation (PDF p. 3).

The exposure effects were large. Facebook users in the Algorithmic Feed spent 73% more time per day than the average US monthly active user, and those in the Chronological Feed 37% more (p < 0.005); on Instagram the figures were 107% and 84% (PDF p. 3). Facebook likes per exposure fell from 6.7% to 3.1% (PDF p. 3). The Chronological Feed cut the share of friend content by 24 percentage points on Facebook. It raised untrustworthy-source content from 2.6% to 4.4% and cut uncivil content from 3.2% to 1.8% (PDF p. 4). Time moved elsewhere: Instagram users spent 36% more mobile time on TikTok and 20% more on YouTube (PDF p. 3).

The attitude effects were null. Across both platforms users in the Chronological Feed did not report lower affective or issue polarization ("p ≈ 1 in all cases, adjusted for multiple comparisons"), and knowledge did not differ (p > 0.63 for all estimates) (PDF p. 4). The authors "could rule out effect sizes smaller than those found in previous research" (PDF p. 4) and say the findings "rule out even modest effects" (PDF p. 6). On-platform political engagement fell (Facebook −0.117 SD, p < 0.005; PDF p. 4). Limits they state: a roughly three-month window; one election; no "general equilibrium" effects, because "our design thus cannot speak" to what happens if every user switched (PDF p. 6).

## Relation to the argument

**Facet H, "steering by exit."** The ranker was swapped for a rule the user can read, and what changed was behaviour on the platform, not attitudes. For the argument this is evidence for a narrow claim: in a dyad of user and ranker, a rule the user can predict changes exposure and engagement sharply and changes downstream beliefs little. It does not isolate what the paper's thesis needs, a change in the user's task, since users did not choose the switch or do any new work.

**P7 and the folk-theory literature.** The authors open from folk theories of opaque algorithms ("Because the algorithms used by social media companies are largely opaque to users, there are numerous conceptions or 'folk theories'", PDF p. 1) and cite Eslami et al. (their ref. 7). The experiment shows that the folk theories about polarization are not borne out by this swap. It says nothing about whether users' theories changed.

**Against the author.** The result cuts against any claim that the interface rule of a platform drives what people believe. It is a null on attitudes, from a collaboration with the platform's owner (PDF p. 3), who "did not have the right to prepublication approval" (PDF p. 6). It speaks weakly to P10.

**Facet check:** facet H quotes the abstract and the sample sizes accurately, and calls the study "dyadic" and "the person drops the ranker". The second phrase misdescribes assignment: users were randomised into the chronological feed, not given a choice.

## Caution

A null result on attitudes from a three-month, one-election, US-only sample of consenting, more active than average users (the authors compare them with US monthly users, PDF p. 3). It cannot be cited for the effect of a feed rule on an unselected user, on a non-US setting, or on long-run exposure. The 80% manipulation share comes from "independent estimates" cited, not measured here. SM unread.
