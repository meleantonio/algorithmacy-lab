## What it argues

Airbnb's engineers describe how they moved search ranking from gradient-boosted trees to neural networks. The frame is the two-sided market: "The home sharing platform at Airbnb is a two sided marketplace for hosts to rent out their spaces … to be booked by prospective guests" (p. 1). The ranking model "is responsible for ordering the listings available according to the guest's likelihood of booking" (p. 1); it is one "particular model in this ecosystem" of ranking models, which includes "models that predict the likelihood of a host accepting the guest's request for booking" (p. 1). A new model is trained to rank "the booked listings in the logs at as high a rank as possible" and is shipped only if an A/B test shows "a statistically significant increase in conversions" (p. 1). The objective, in short, is bookings.

Several passages show what the platform itself can and cannot see. "We find no objective truth in the logs, only tradeoffs highly conditional upon the budget and tastes of the guest which remain mostly hidden" (p. 3). Listing-id embeddings overfit because "Even the most popular listing can be booked at most 365 times in an entire year" (p. 4). A multi-task model that also predicted long page views "increased long views by a large margin. But bookings remained neutral" (p. 4). The final DNN has "an input layer with a total of 195 features" and two hidden layers (p. 3); inputs include the price "of listings that have the Smart Pricing feature enabled, supplied by a specialized model" (p. 3). On interpretability: "Estimating feature importance and model interpretability in general is an area where we took a step back with the move to NNs" (p. 7); score decomposition was "a conceptual error," ablation was lost in noise, permutation gave "somewhat nonsensical results" (pp. 7–8). A top-versus-bottom analysis shows "The distribution of prices for top ranked listings are skewed towards lower values, indicating the sensitivity of the model to price" (p. 8). The retrospective asks "are we accurately representing all our users?" (p. 8).

## Relation to the argument

**Question 4 (Airbnb search ranking as "binds and optimizes").** The optimizing is documented from inside: the ranking maximises the platform's conversion metric, not either party's stated preference, and it is tuned on the platform's own logs. The binding is softer than Uber's: the host still sets the price (with a platform model offering one), but visibility is the platform's to give, and a listing outside the top ranks is not seen.

**P8 and ruling D3 (disclosure).** This is the strongest evidence in the library that disclosure cannot deliver what the author's critics would want: the platform's own engineers could not produce a stable account of which features drive the ranking (pp. 7–8). There is no legible text to disclose. A host who wants to rank higher must act and observe, which is the author's P9 in practice.

**Where it cuts against the author.** The paper is the platform's voice and reports no host or guest behaviour; "gaming" appears nowhere. The price sensitivity (p. 8) implies the ranking rewards lower prices, which is a case where the mediator's objective and the guest's interest run together.

## Caution

An insider engineering report without a methods section in the social-science sense: no sample sizes, no effect sizes for the online gains (shown only as relative plots), no description of what guests or hosts see. Read as a preprint. The authors' aim is to help other engineering teams, not to describe the market.
