## What it argues

Stark and Pais ask what organisational form a platform is and conclude it is none of market, hierarchy or network: "whereas for markets the verb is contract, the verb for hierarchy is command, and for networks it is collaborate. By contrast, platforms co-opt" (p. 48). Management on a platform then has a specific shape: "This relationship is typically three-sided: on one side, the platform owner; on the second side, the provider …; and on the third side, the user-customer" (p. 53). What "is not obvious (in much of the research on algorithmic management) is that this triangular geometry (Vallas & Schor, 2020, p. 282) and the power asymmetries along its various axes are key to understanding algorithmic management in the platform economy" (p. 54).

The mechanism is enrolment without delegation. "Platform owners co-opt the behavior of providers and users, enrolling them in the practices of algorithmic management without managerial authority having been delegated to them" (abstract, p. 47). "Yet neither is it the case that providers and users are controlling each other. … These feedback loops are not circular. Instead, they are twisted by the triadic relations of owner-provider-user" (p. 54). Three contrasts with Taylorism organise the middle of the paper: "rules but these are not bureaucratic," "rankings but not ranks," "monitoring but it is not disciplinary" (p. 49). On rules, frequent changes in terms and protocols are "a major source of uncertainty" (p. 55) and "the production of uncertainty" is itself "a source of non-bureaucratic control" (p. 55). On rankings, "below a certain position in the ranking you might just as well be invisible" (p. 57), and n. 26 (p. 57) records that the EU P2B consultation found "arbitrary and discriminatory changes to search algorithms made by online platforms with little or no warning or explanation." On visibility, "platform owners relinquish supervisory control … in favor of enrolling the behavior of the other two parties to the exchange" (p. 60); the metrics "are accessible to the second and third parties themselves (at least partly and often with considerable opacity)" (p. 60). Table 1 (p. 61) sets visibility "Triangular" and accountability "Deflected," and the text concludes: "no one is accountable because there is no accountability in algorithmic accounting" (p. 61).

Two further claims matter here. Footnote 13 (p. 54): "Scientific management is taking place in some parts of the platform's operations, especially where there is no triangular relationship. For example, Amazon's enormous warehouses." And the closing section pairs Curchod et al.'s "coalition of the invisibles" (buyers and platform owner, against the visible sellers, p. 64) with Rahman and Thelen's societal coalition of "firm owners, investors and consumers" (p. 64).

## Relation to the argument

**P11 and ruling D9.** The strongest sociological statement in the library that the platform relation is three-sided and that its control works *through* the third side. The "twisted" loop (p. 54) is a claim of non-decomposability in prose: the provider's rating comes from the user, is translated by the owner, and returns to govern the provider. It supports the abstract's "forming an irreducible algorithmic triad" in the sense that none of the three dyads carries the control by itself. It offers no measure and no test.

**The abstract's "binds and optimizes."** Supported in a narrower form. The owner sets terms unilaterally (p. 55; n. 15, p. 54, on conditions that are not "terms of a contract") and ranks (p. 57); that is "binds." The paper does not model an objective function; "optimizes" is read in from the economics cards (`rochet2006two`, `hagiu2015multi`).

**Disclosure (P8, D3).** The P2B note (n. 26) and the opacity of metrics (p. 60) fit the author's claim that disclosure does not reach the person's task; the paper argues the problem is instability as much as secrecy (p. 55).

**Where it cuts against the author.** Footnote 13 is a boundary: where the platform employs directly (warehouses), the relation is a dyad with a Taylorist instrument. The triad is a property of the marketplace form, not of "algorithms" as such. The paper also places the owner on the consumer's side (p. 64), which complicates any reading in which the two counterparts face the mediator symmetrically.

## Caution

An essay with no data of its own; every empirical sentence is secondary. The year is unsettled (volume 2020, published 29 January 2021). It argues against reading platform power as a panopticon, so it should not be cited as support for "digital cage" claims. The authors' interest is class and politics; the triad is an analytic device, not a measured object.
