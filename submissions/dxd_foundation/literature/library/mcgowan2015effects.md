# McGowan, V. A., White, S. J., & Paterson, K. B. (2015). The effects of interword spacing on the eye movements of young and older readers. *Journal of Cognitive Psychology, 27*(5), 609–621.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1080/20445911.2014.988157; open copy in the University of Leicester repository (figshare item 10127054, file "McGowan-JCP-FINAL-SUBMITTED.pdf"; hdl 2381/36304) · **Read depth:** full_text (authors' final submitted manuscript, 30 pages: abstract, introduction, method, results and discussion, general discussion, references, Tables 1–4, Figure 1; manuscript page numbers; published online 12 December 2014) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article (author manuscript) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** eye-tracking experiment, 15 young (18–25, mean 19.9) and 15 older adults (67–80, mean 72.5), native English speakers; 120 sentences with a high- or lower-frequency target noun; three displays: normal interword spacing, condensed to half its size, expanded to 1.5 times; Courier New, about 3.3 characters per degree; EyeLink 1000; ANOVA by participants and items · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (reader and typography) · **Facets:** R2 · **Read:** 2026-10-14

## What it argues

After the same group's finding that older readers suffer more when spaces are removed (`mcgowan2014aging`), this study asks whether older readers are also more sensitive to the small changes in space size that fonts and justification produce (pp. 5–6).

The effects were "small, but reliable" (p. 17). Sentence reading times (Table 1, p. 25) were 2,327 ms normal, 2,306 condensed and 2,387 expanded for young adults, and 2,857, 2,866 and 2,928 ms for older adults. Expanded spacing gave "a small but significant increase in reading times" with more but shorter fixations and less word skipping; condensed spacing gave longer fixations and more skipping (pp. 11–13). Forward saccades changed by about as much as the spaces did (−0.5 characters condensed, +0.4 expanded, against changes of −0.5 and +0.5): "readers modify the size of their saccades to accommodate small changes in interword spacing size" (p. 12). Landing positions on target words did not move (about 2.9 characters in, p. 16).

No age-by-spacing interaction reached significance on any measure (p. 12, p. 15), and spacing did not change the word frequency effect (p. 15). The authors conclude that "as long as some space information is present, the eye movement behaviour of older adults is resilient to small changes in interword spacing size" (p. 19), and that the effects are "pre-lexical", probably through crowding of the outer letters (p. 16, pp. 18–19). More space was not better: wider spaces pushed the next word into poorer parafoveal vision (p. 17).

## Relation to the argument

This bears on P5 and on the draft's oculomotor account of spacing in §3.2. It **supports** the claim that spaces guide saccades: readers rescaled their saccades to the spaces and kept landing at the preferred position. It also **narrows** the account in a way the draft does not yet say. The benefit of spacing is a threshold, not a quantity. Once a boundary is visible, enlarging it adds nothing and can cost time, because the reader's parafoveal preview gets worse. For the paper's design lesson, a convention can be over-applied: more of the cue is not more help.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** McGowan et al. (2015) are not cited in DRAFT_v2. They bear on these lines:
- Line 113 ("By carving white channels between character clusters, spacing gives the oculomotor system targets for the next saccade, and removing the spaces changes where the eyes land"). **Supports the targeting claim** (saccades rescaled to spacing, p. 12; landing positions held, p. 16) **and narrows it**: wider channels did not help and slowed reading slightly (2,387 vs 2,327 ms young; Table 1).

## Caution

Fifteen readers per group; English sentences in a monospaced font. The file read is the authors' submitted manuscript; the journal version may differ, and page numbers are the manuscript's. The manipulations are small, so the paper speaks to how much space helps once spaces exist, not to the presence or absence of spacing that the draft's historical case concerns.
