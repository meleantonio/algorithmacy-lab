# Loos, M., & Luzak, J. (2016). Wanted: A bigger stick. On unfair terms in consumer contracts with online service providers. *Journal of Consumer Policy, 39*(1), 63–90. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10603-015-9303-7

## What it argues

Loos and Luzak test the terms of four large online services against EU unfair-terms law and find six kinds of clause that are "unlikely to pass the Directive's unfairness test" (abstract, p. 63). Two kinds bear on custody: clauses that let the provider change the terms unilaterally, and clauses that let it change the service itself.

The legal yardstick already exists. Annex I of the 1993 Directive lists as potentially unfair a term "enabling the seller or supplier to alter the terms of the contract unilaterally without a valid reason which is specified in the contract" (para. 1(j), p. 67), and one "enabling the seller or supplier to alter unilaterally without a valid reason any characteristics of the product or service to be provided" (para. 1(k), p. 72). From CJEU price-change case law (Invitel, RWE, Kásler) the authors draw two conditions for a fair change clause: the contract states when and how terms may change, and the consumer may terminate after notice (p. 68). They argue courts should apply the same test to non-price terms and to service changes (pp. 69, 72).

The four providers fail. Table 1 (p. 70) quotes the change clauses; in each, continued use counts as acceptance. Google gives 14 days' notice but exempts legal and new-function changes; Dropbox and Twitter notify only changes they themselves judge material (pp. 70–71). The authors conclude that "all standard contract terms of online service providers investigated by us fail to meet the conditions set by the CJEU" (pp. 71–72), and that the service-change clauses of Facebook, Twitter and Google "are unfair" (p. 74). Unilateral termination clauses, liability caps, California forum clauses and choice-of-law clauses fail too (pp. 74–86).

The title states the remedy. Because "it seems unlikely that individual consumers will in fact bring a claim against an online service provider for what many consumers still perceive to be 'free' services", they assign the task to consumer authorities and organisations, which can sue in their own courts under Brussels I (p. 88).

## Relation to the argument

**Custody test (§7.1–7.3).** The paper is a close legal reading of exactly the clauses that make platforms "High (unilateral)" on the draft's custody test, and it supports that rating for terms of service in 2015. It also shows something the draft's table hides. EU law has, since 1993, listed unilateral change of terms and of service characteristics without a valid reason among the terms that "may be unfair" (Annex I, an indicative list; the authors' wording, p. 67), subject to exceptions in para. 2(b) (p. 67); the CJEU conditions of notice and a right to exit come from case law on price changes (p. 68). [S2 correction: the card said "presumptively unfair", which overstates the Annex's legal force] So "first-generation" regulation in the EU did not rely only on disclosure and procedure: a substantive constraint on unilateral change existed, and the problem the authors identify is enforcement. That narrows the draft's line between disclosure-era rules and "structural intervention".

**Ruling on statutes (line 441).** The paper supports the draft's claim that the owner keeps its power unless an outside body acts, but locates the gap in enforcement of an old rule, not in the absence of law.

## Caution

Doctrinal analysis, not adjudication: the authors predict how courts would rule and say a court "may decide otherwise" on some clauses (pp. 84, 86). Four providers, terms as of August 2015; all four have rewritten their terms since. The service-change analysis extends CJEU price-change case law by analogy (pp. 69, 72); the CJEU had not then ruled on non-price changes. EU consumer contracts only; business users and workers on platforms fall outside the Directive's notion of consumer (pp. 66–67).
