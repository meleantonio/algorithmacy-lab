## What it argues

Rahman argues that algorithmic evaluation on the platform builds an "invisible cage," in which "platforms deliberately hide the norms and expectations for how people should behave" (abstract, p. v). The case is the Work Success Score (WSS), introduced without notice (§7.1, pp. 86–87). The platform published inputs and a high-level formula, "(successful contract outcomes - negative contract outcomes) / total outcomes" (p. 89), but withheld the calculation. Its stated reason, as Rahman quotes it: "We don't reveal the exact calculation for your score. Doing so would make it easier for some users to artificially boost their scores" (p. 89). Rahman glosses this as hiding the calculation "to ensure freelancers could not explicitly game the measure" (p. 89).

The chapter documents what freelancers faced: opaque expectations (pp. 88–91), unpredictable impact, including a drop "from 100% to 82%" after closing an idle contract (p. 93), no notice of when the score updated (p. 94), "no recourse" (p. 95), and private client feedback that counted against freelancers without being shown to them (pp. 96–100). Freelancers' resulting behaviour he calls "superstitious reactivity," because they "were never able to verify if and how their actions contributed to their success" (abstract, p. v).

In theory (§8.1), the platform "hid its algorithm to prevent gaming of the system and maintain a stratified body of freelancers whose ratings were controlled solely by the platform" (p. 117). He lists the cage's components: boilerplate terms, no proactive communication, partially opaque inputs, "completely opaque processing," unpredictable impact, no recourse, and changing conditions (p. 117). He disclaims insight into motive ("I do not have further insight into StarWorks' motives," p. 118) and suggests the platform needed only enough freelancers to serve clients, who supplied revenue (p. 118).

His remedies (§8.3): "too much transparency contributes to gaming behavior while too much opaqueness contributes to superstitions and resentment" (p. 136), so he proposes "intermittent, limited disclosure", e.g. monthly or quarterly reasons for score changes (p. 136). He doubts regulation alone (GDPR, credit scoring, pp. 137–138) and proposes worker-run third-party tools on the Turkopticon model (pp. 138–139).

## Relation to the argument

**P9 and P10 (feedback that is rapid and unequivocal).** This is the strongest field evidence in the pool that the mediator destroys the conditions for learning by acting: unannounced score updates, unknown timing (p. 94) and hidden private inputs (pp. 96–100) leave the score neither rapid nor unequivocal. Freelancers "trying various methods to take apart and understand the algorithm" (p. 117) are acting through the system in Kirsh and Maglio's sense and failing to learn from it, which supports the paper's diagnosis.

**Ruling D3 and P8: where Rahman cuts against the paper.** His own fix is a disclosure, "intermittent, limited disclosure" of the major reasons for score changes (p. 136). That is a literacy-side remedy by the paper's own taxonomy. He also accepts part of the platform's rationale: full transparency invites gaming (p. 136). The paper can cite him for the diagnosis, but should say that his proposed remedy is the kind it argues against, or recast his "reasons for change" as a form of feedback immediacy.

**P12 and ruling D8.** The asymmetry between clients (revenue) and freelancers (p. 118), the suspension of his freelancer but not client account (p. 118), and the one-sided terms (ch. 6, not read) support P12's claim that the interface owner is an interested party who changes conventions alone. His call for independent worker tools (pp. 138–139) is a candidate holder of the convention, which bears on D8.

## Caution

A dissertation, not the refereed version, read in part. One platform, a pseudonym, interviews recruited through paid one-hour jobs ($30, p. 48); clients recruited through freelancers. The account of the platform's motives rests on its public statements and Rahman's inference. Chapters 3, 5, 6 and §7.2 were not read, so any claim the draft might later draw from boilerplate creep, algorithmic agnotology or the specific superstitions needs those chapters opened.
