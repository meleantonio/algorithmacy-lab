## What it argues

Masuda and colleagues ask whether telling bidders that a mechanism is strategy-proof makes them bid sincerely. They argue that "the Vickrey auction does not exhibit the expected performance by merely explaining the auction rule to participants" and propose to "announce the strategy-proofness" instead (p. 903). After the instructions and a 17-question quiz, subjects in the advice treatments received a paper, also read aloud: "You can maximize your earnings by bidding your valuations as they are, regardless of what others bid," preceded by "Please consider carefully whether this advice is true or false" (p. 908). The same sentence was given in the pay-your-bid auction, where it is false, so that the pay-your-bid change estimates the experimenter-demand effect (p. 909; restated p. 916).

Results. Sincere bids were 20.6% without advice and 46.9% with it in the Vickrey auction, a rise of 26.3 points (95% CI 24.0 to 28.7), against 5.8% to 8.3% (2.5 points) in pay-your-bid (Table 2, p. 914; Result 2, p. 915). The net advice effect is 23.8 points (95% CI 21.2 to 26.5; Result 3, p. 916). Overbidding fell from 63.4% to 45.3% of bids (p. 912). Subjects with a perfect quiz score responded 15.0 points more than the rest (Result 4, p. 917). Efficiency rose from 0.967 to 0.977 (p = 0.020), mainly in periods 1–13 (0.956 to 0.970, p = 0.039); the net effect on efficiency against pay-your-bid was 0.009 at p = 0.051 (Table 6, p. 920; Results 5–6). Bidders' payoffs rose by 14.6 yen per auction (14.553, significant at 5% in Table 7, though Result 7 calls it a marginal increase); seller revenue did not change (Table 7, p. 921). In the Ausubel auction, which is not strategy-proof, the same kind of advice raised sincere bidding by 8.4 points (Result 9, p. 926).

The authors place advice against the structural route: strengthening strategy-proofness, as Li's (2017) obvious strategy-proofness does, "severely restricts the admissible class of mechanisms", whereas "our approach of providing advice can be applied to any strategy-proof mechanism" and "drastically improves its truth-telling performance" (p. 905). They concede two limits: the advice was kept simple, "This simplicity makes the effect of advice limited," and in a symmetric environment both auctions were nearly efficient, so "the effects of advice on efficiency are limited" (pp. 935, 938).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D3 and premise P8.** This is the clearest case in the library against an unqualified reading of D3 ("disclosure of the algorithm doesnt affect our ability to navigate it"). A one-sentence statement of a property of the rule more than doubled sincere bidding, and the net effect survives the demand-effect control. The authors themselves frame it as an alternative to structural redesign (p. 905). The draft already absorbs this by splitting Target 3 (a stated property) from Target 4 (full text), which is the honest move. It should not drift back to "disclosure does nothing."

**Premise P10, Convention A.** Masuda supports the claim that a person needs to be told, or shown, that the strategic task is gone: even though over 60% of Vickrey subjects scored perfectly on the 17-question rules quiz (p. 911), 63.4% of bids without advice were overbids (p. 912). That is evidence for the "show that it is gone" half of Convention A. It is not evidence for an interface: the advice was a printed sentence read aloud by an experimenter.

**What it cuts.** The efficiency result is small because the design left little room for it (p. 938). "Behavior changes more than outcomes" is a fair summary of this study; as a general law it would overreach.

## Caution

Japanese students in a lab, paid in yen, 25 periods; one simple sentence of advice delivered on paper by the experimenter, not a screen or a platform. Symmetric valuations left efficiency near ceiling. The advice in the Vickrey treatment is true, so subjects could check it; the result does not show what an untrusted mediator's statement would do. The Correction (2022) was not opened; per Crossref and the bib note it concerns the supplement, not the article text.
