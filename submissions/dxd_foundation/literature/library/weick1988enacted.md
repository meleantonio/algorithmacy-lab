## What it argues

Weick states the claim in the abstract: "Sensemaking in crisis conditions is made more difficult because action that is instrumental to understanding the crisis often intensifies the crisis" (p. 305). The introduction names the dilemma: "people think by acting," and "There is a delicate tradeoff between dangerous action which produces understanding and safe inaction which produces confusion" (p. 305). He opens with Bateson ("An explorer can never know what he is exploring until it has been explored") and a line from Union Carbide's manual, "The situation will determine the appropriate action." Weick answers that "actions determine the situation" and that "preconceptions" more often decide what looks appropriate (p. 306).

Enactment means that "when people act, they bring events and structures into existence and set them in motion" (p. 306). It has two steps: bracketing part of experience on the basis of preconceptions, then acting inside the bracket, which "tends to confirm preconceptions" (p. 307). The residue is an "enacted environment," summarized privately as a "cause map" of if-then assertions (p. 307). "Action precedes cognition and focuses cognition" (p. 307).

Three variables then shape sensemaking and the crisis itself. **Commitment:** when action is "irrevocable, public and volitional," the justification built afterwards hardens; "the explanation tends to persist and become transformed into an assumption that is taken for granted" (p. 310). At Bhopal the commitment to secrecy was "one of the last assumptions workers considered" (p. 310). **Capacity:** people "see those events they feel they have the capacity to do something about" (p. 311); specialists miss the big picture; centralised authority contracts and cuts the action that builds meaning; Bhopal's operating staff per shift fell from 12 to 6 (p. 312), so "the number and quality of actors available to do that acting and interpretation become crucial variables" (p. 312). **Expectations:** top management's view that the plant was unimportant produced the cost-cutting and indifference that made it dangerous (pp. 313–314).

Weick's closing section says action can help: it simplifies a complex task, which matters because stress hurts complex-task performance more (p. 315), and it can slow tightly coupled processes. He proposes an "ideology" of enactment, which keeps "an awareness of the necessity to balance dangerous action with safe inaction in the interest of diagnosis" (p. 315). The article reports no data of its own.

## Relation to the argument

Ruling D7 says people "understand THROUGH deciding and quickly ingesting the results" and "pilots dont spend time reading the situation, they act." Weick gives the nearest published wording of the first half: cognition "lies in the path of the action" (p. 307). He does not support it without a condition. Three points cut against the author's version. (1) Acting trades off against "safe inaction"; the article does not say that more acting yields more understanding. (2) The acts that build understanding also change the thing understood, so the person's own acts become part of the problem (p. 309). Applied to a platform, the person's acts feed an adaptive intermediary, which Part 1 line 66 already flags as disrupting clean feedback. (3) The explanation that follows an act tends to harden into a blind spot (p. 310). The paper can cite Weick for the claim that action is an epistemic tool in ambiguous situations, and must keep his warning beside it. The setting is crisis in industrial plants with loosely coupled elements, not routine use of an app, and nothing here concerns pilots.

## Caution

A 1988 conceptual article, argued from secondary accounts of one disaster, with no measurement. "Enactment" is defined broadly enough that most acts qualify, so the claim is hard to test. Do not cite it for the effect of acting on understanding in routine tasks, for pilots, or for algorithmic platforms. Weick concedes that "the relative importance of enactment is exaggerated and borders on hyperbole," and defends that for its usefulness in finding places to intervene (p. 316).
