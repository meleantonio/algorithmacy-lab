## What it argues
A strategy is "obviously dominant" if, "for any deviation, at any information set where both strategies first diverge, the best outcome under the deviation is no better than the worst outcome under the dominant strategy" (Abstract). A mechanism is obviously strategy-proof (OSP) if it has an equilibrium in obviously dominant strategies. The definition depends on the extensive form, so two games with the same normal form can differ: the ascending clock auction is OSP and the second-price sealed-bid auction is strategy-proof (SP) but not OSP (§1).

Li gives two readings, each with a theorem. The behavioural one: a strategy is obviously dominant if and only if an agent who cannot reason contingently, state by state, about hypothetical scenarios can recognise it as weakly dominant (§1). The classical one: OSP mechanisms are those a planner can run with only partial commitment power (§1). He adds that "policymakers could announce that a mechanism is strategy-proof, but that may not be enough" if agents do not understand it, and cites Google's advertising for its second-price auction, which seemed to imply strategy-proofness when the mechanism was not (§1, with Edelman et al. 2007).

Experiment (§5). Three pairs of mechanisms implement the same choice rule, one SP-not-OSP and one OSP: second-price against ascending clock auction (plus a variant pair with an added feature), and a strategy-proof random serial dictatorship (SP-RSD, subjects submit ranked lists of four prizes) against an obviously strategy-proof one (OSP-RSD, subjects take turns picking). Results:
- Auctions: a randomly chosen auction was 31 percentage points more likely to close within $2.00 of the equilibrium price under the clock auction than under second-price, and 28 points more likely in the variant pair (§5.3, p. 28).
- Serial dictatorship: in the ten-round games, "36% of games do not end in the dominant strategy outcome" under SP-RSD and 7% under OSP-RSD (§5.3, p. 29). In rounds 6–10 the error rate was 28.9% against 6.7% (Table 4).
- One-shot, higher stakes (404 subjects, 101 groups): 40% against 8% (p = .0001, Fisher's exact test); first-round error rates in the repeated games were 44% and 17% (§5.4, p. 30).
- In SP-RSD 29% of submitted lists contain errors, and the commonest error swaps the top two prizes (p. 29).
Li calls this "only preliminary evidence" that obvious dominance classifies mechanisms by cognitive complexity (§5.5).

## Relation to the argument
This is the base for P8: a rule built so the agent need not reason through contingencies removes a strategic task, and how the rule is presented (sequential, with the options visible) decides how many benefit. The 36% against 7% figure is in the working paper, §5.3, and matches the premise. Three limits matter for the author's paper. First, the two arms are equivalent in theory, so what differs is the interface and the sequence, which fits the author's talk of changing the interface (D10), but the person does not learn anything; the task changes. Second, the mechanism has no objective of its own and no stake against the participant: the planner is a neutral designer. Li himself says OSP is "only one" dimension of simplicity and leaves framing effects and the search for the dominant strategy open, adding that "in a well-engineered mechanism, the answer may be that the designer draws the agent's attention to it" (§6, p. 31). Third, OSP is not available for every aim; he reports that for some school priorities no mechanism OSP-implements deferred acceptance (citing Ashlagi and Gonczarowski), so the approach has limits where the mediator must satisfy stability.

Facet check: the facet's "Li 2017 reports a lab experiment" and the 36% / 7% figure are right. My reading puts the figure in §5.3 rather than §5.2, and the 7% compares only the two serial-dictatorship games; the auction pairs use a different measure.
