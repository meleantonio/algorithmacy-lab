## What it argues
Hungary assigns about 100,000 applicants a year through a "strategically simple" DA variant in which ranking inconsistently with one's preferences is dominated; most programs are offered both with and without a tuition waiver, listed consecutively in the same drop-down, at zero marginal application cost (pp. 2, 6). "Out of 528,172 ROLs in the eligible sample, 58,178 (11 percent) contain a dominated choice" (p. 8); among relevant ROLs (those with a self-funded contract) the share is 27 percent (p. 9); among high-school seniors 5.8 percent (16.6 percent of relevant lists). "Between 12.3 and 18.7 percent of these revealed dominated choices were costly", with an average loss "approximately 6,600 dollars" per costly case, so 970 to 1,412 dollars per dominated choice and 129–188 dollars across the population (p. 2). Dominated choices are more common for higher-SES applicants, in larger settlements, for lower GPA (one SD of 11th-grade GPA, −4.2 points or 38 percent), for women (+3 points), and for more selective programs (one SD of cut-off, +3.4 points), the last surviving ROL fixed effects (pp. 11–12). The authors read this as "applicants make more dominated choices when they are less costly" (p. 3), which "rul[es] out cognitive limitations as a sole determinant" (p. 4). Undoing all dominated choices would change 2.2–3.1 percent of assignments and reduce the number admitted, because funded seats are over-demanded (p. 10).

The discussion (pp. 13–14) weighs explanations. Pessimistic beliefs can explain the pattern only if beliefs are badly wrong. Lack of awareness of the optimal strategy is "consistent with the higher rates of dominated choices among two groups of applicants who presumably have less access to advising". Mistrust is unlikely because cut-offs are published and "applicants can verify that their assignment is indeed the option they ranked highest among those whose cutoff they surpassed" (p. 13). Expectations-based loss aversion (Dreyfuss et al. 2022; Meisner and von Wangenheim 2019) "can explain the patterns we document" (p. 13).

## Relation to the argument
The largest field sample in the facet (N = 528,172) and the one with the largest stakes. It confirms that a strategy-proof rule leaves a measurable share of people gaming it, here 11 percent overall and 27 percent of those who could show it, in a system with published cut-offs that let anyone check the outcome. Two details matter for the abstract. First, the cut-off publication is a feedback convention of exactly the kind the abstract's last clause calls for ("ecological feedback immediacy"), and it did not prevent dominated choices; it arrives after the decision, once a year, so it is feedback without immediacy, which is consistent with P9's conditions rather than against them. Second, the finding that dominated choices rise where they are cheaper (selective programs) means some of this is calculated near-indifference, not failure to understand; that complicates any claim that a design change would "free" people from a burden they are, in part, choosing to carry lightly. Supports P10 only in the narrow form; cuts against reading field misreporting as pure cognitive overload.

## Caution
Author manuscript, November 2022; the JPE Micro text may differ. The mechanism is strategically simple but not strategy-proof in full (fn. 1), so "dominated" refers to the ordering of contracts, not to list length. The data report at most seven contracts per list (fn. 11). Correlations are descriptive. The "dominated" label depends on the assumption that applicants prefer funding and care only about their own assignment (p. 6), which the authors discuss at length.
