## What it argues
The authors detect misreporting in the field by exploiting a natural ranking: several programs admit students with or without a no-strings scholarship, so a list that ranks the unfunded contract above the funded one ("obvious flipping") or omits the funded one ("obvious dropping") is "a weakly dominated strategy" under the standard assumptions (p. 12). Scholarships ranged from 8,000 NIS ($2,070) to 90,000 NIS ($23,323) a year for MA programs (p. 11). Applicants "were advised on multiple occasions to submit their true preferences" in every email and letter, in the FAQ, and through a video lecture on DA sent to those who asked (pp. 11–12); a pop-up warned anyone who listed only one of a program's two contracts (p. 12).

Results. "Of the 672 ROLs in our sample, 130 (19.3%) obviously misrepresented the applicant's true preferences, with almost equal shares of obvious flipping and obvious dropping" (p. 16; Table II: 64 droppings, 70 flippings). Costly cases were few because scholarships were scarce: between 3 and 10 of the 130 (lower and upper bound), with average losses "of more than $6,000" and "more than $5,000" respectively (p. 18). In the 2015 survey, 20.2% reported some misrepresentation of program order; of the 54 respondents who had submitted an obvious misrepresentation, only 29 (53.7%) admitted it, so "survey-based estimates of preference misrepresentation" are biased downward (p. 17). In the 2014 survey, 18% answered yes to whether there was "a strategic advantage in ranking programs to which you think you have a better chance of being admitted (even though it was made clear that there was no such advantage)", and "An additional 21% reported that they thought that the answer was negative, but they could not explain why" (p. 18). Correlates: unranked applicants "were more than twice as likely to submit an obvious misrepresentation" (p. 21); misrepresentation falls with the MITAM score (Table IV); misrepresenters hold university degrees less often (65.4% against 76.1%, p = 0.016, p. 19).

Discussion: "even in near-optimal conditions for truth-telling—a high-stakes decision by highly educated participants who have received plenty of information, advice, and time—one cannot treat reports to a strategy-proof mechanism as necessarily representing an applicant's true ranking" (p. 23). Three explanations: misunderstanding or mistrust, leading to the heuristic that "the mechanism rewards a higher ranking with an increased probability of matching"; dominated strategies that are near-optimal under pessimistic beliefs; and preferences that depend on more than the assignment (pp. 24–25). The recommendation is to target likely misreporters with "decision support" such as the Genetic Counseling match's CONFIRM alerts, "direct explanation efforts", transparency and data access (p. 25). They also note that "describing the mechanism in terms of admission cut-offs" may reduce misunderstanding (p. 4, comparing Hungary).

## Relation to the argument
This is field evidence that a strategy-proof rule does not by itself take the strategic task off the person. About one in five of those who could reveal it ranked against their own interest after repeated advice and a lecture; 18% still believed there was a strategic advantage to gaming. For the abstract's sentence it means: a rule can remove the need to strategise in theory while the person keeps strategising in practice, and the ones who keep at it are the weaker applicants. That supports P10 only in the narrow form "a rule plus something that makes the rule's safety visible", and it speaks against the stronger claim that offloading is achieved once the rule is in place. For D3 it is double-edged: here advice, FAQ and a lecture (literacy-side interventions) left 19.3% misreporting, which fits the ruling; but the authors' remedy list is more disclosure and decision support, not a change of representation. The authors' remark on cut-off descriptions (p. 4) is a lead toward the representational line. The clearinghouse has no objective of its own, so the triad is not modelled.

## Caution
Author manuscript; journal pagination differs. Obvious misrepresentation is a lower bound and a special case; the authors stress it is "the tip of the iceberg" (p. 23) but also that it rests on the assumption that applicants prefer funding. Correlations are descriptive, not causal (p. 21). The survey ran after results were published, which may colour reports (fn. 23). Two cohorts of one national match in one profession.
