## What it argues

The authors use Uber's surge algorithm to trace a demand curve and estimate consumer surplus. The mechanism they exploit is the mediator's own rule: "Uber calculates each surge price to an arbitrary number of decimal places, but consumers are presented with discrete price increments (e.g., the lowest surge price is 1.2x …)" (p. 3); "A surge generator value of 1.249 leads to a surge price of 1.2x whereas a value of 1.251 triggers a 1.3x surge" (p. 9). Uber "does not employ a surge of 1.1x" (n. 22, p. 9), and "Surge prices are always greater than or equal to 1.0" (n. 18, p. 7). The rider "observe[s] the price offered … and whether she accepts or rejects that offer" (p. 3); on the other side, "driver-partners are sequentially given the opportunity to accept that order until one does so" (p. 6).

Results: about 21% of UberX sessions see surge above 1.0x (p. 7); purchase rates fall from 62% at base price to 39% under high surge (Table 1, p. 36, reported in the text at p. 9); at the 1.2x/1.3x threshold the purchase rate drops about 3% while price rises 8.3%, "an implied price elasticity of roughly −0.36" (p. 10); across fifty estimates "The median point estimate is −0.51; the mean point estimate is −0.57" (p. 14), pooled −0.55 (se .02) (p. 14); expected wait time falls .129 minutes at the threshold (p. 11). Consumer surplus: "$2.88 billion (SE=$122 million)" for the four cities in 2015, "$6.76 billion" extrapolated to the US, "approximately 1.57 times as large as consumer expenditures on rides taken at base pricing" (p. 5). The authors describe surge as a system that "helps increase supply at times of high demand, and allocate rides to riders who value them most highly (Hall et al. 2015)" (p. 7), and note that "driver-partners seeking more revenue adjust their labor supply in response to predictable demand shocks," so surge "is less tightly linked to intuitions about demand than might be naively expected" (p. 8). Business rules cap price increases (".5 in the first increase, .6 in the second," p. 20).

## Relation to the argument

**Question 4 (Uber surge and matching as "binds and optimizes").** Direct evidence of the binding: the rider gets a take-it-or-leave-it multiplier, rounded by the platform, never below base; the driver gets a sequential offer. The optimizing is stated by the platform's own authors as market clearing and allocation to the highest-value riders (p. 7). The triad is visible in the data structure: the rider's accept/decline, the driver's supply response and the platform's generator sit in one record.

**P9 (learning by deciding and taking in results).** The sessions are a natural setting for the author's claim: riders decide against a quoted price, with an immediate result. The paper does not study learning; it uses the decisions to recover demand.

**Where it cuts against the author.** Nothing here suggests riders "game the mediator"; demand is inelastic (most elasticities between −0.4 and −0.6) and riders mostly pay. The strategic party, if any, is the driver, whose labour supply tracks predictable surge (p. 8). The paper also shows the mediator overriding its own algorithm (business rules in 11.5% of sessions), which is a reminder that "the algorithm" is partly a human policy.

## Caution

Two authors (Cohen, Hall) were at Uber; "The data were chosen in conjunction with Uber to be large enough to be representative, while not revealing information that may have more business sensitivity" (n. 8, p. 4). Short-run elasticities from transient price changes (n. 11, p. 5). Not peer-reviewed. Four cities, first half of 2015.
