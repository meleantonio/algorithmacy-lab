## What it argues
Dasgupta and Hill ask how designers can help children build "critical algorithmic literacies", their word for "intellectual tools that allow young people to understand and critique the algorithmic systems that affect their lives" (Introduction, p. 1). Because "many of these powerful algorithms are invisible, only a small number of young people develop these literacies" (p. 1). From their Scratch work they draw four principles:

1. Connect children to data. A simple cloud-variable block let children see that a project could store usernames indefinitely, and a view-count project led community members to notice that views and viewers differ, so "data is not objective, but requires interpretation" (Principle 1, pp. 4–6).
2. Make "sandboxes for dangerous ideas". Children who can write a single `if` that bars newcomers learn how discrimination works; the designers contained the risk with warnings, staged release, and moderator involvement (Principle 2, pp. 6–8).
3. Use community-centred approaches. Children challenged algorithms by naming a conflict with Scratch's values (inclusion, creativity over popularity); access to Cloud Variables was limited to users active for some time (Principle 3, pp. 8–10).
4. Support "thick authenticity" (Shaffer and Resnick): data and problems from the learner's own world (Principle 4, p. 10).

They define "literacy" through diSessa's three pillars: material (signs, representations, "conventions of writing"), mental, and social, and, quoting diSessa (2001, p. 11), say a literacy emerges from "complex social forces of innovation, adoption, and interdependence" (Background, p. 3). They add Agre's "critical technical practice" and Freire's literacy methods (pp. 3–4). They state the limit plainly: "Our work is limited in that it has so far focused on individual learners and case studies" and they have "yet to conduct any systematic study of outcomes" (Discussion, p. 11). Most episodes of children questioning algorithms "emerged from the process of active creation with toolkits" (p. 11).

## Relation to the argument
The paper is a literacy-frame source, and it belongs where the argument sets literacy against algorithmacy (rulings D3 and D4: disclosure and transparency are literacy-based interventions). Two points matter.

First, Dasgupta and Hill's literacy is not disclosure. Their children do not read a statement about an algorithm; they build one and query data. That makes the literacy tradition less open to the objection that it reduces to "give the person something to read" (premise P9), and nearer to the author's ruling D7 (understanding through acting and taking in results). But it works on a person's relation to an algorithm that the child can inspect and write, in a community that polices the result. The mediator in the author's triad is neither written by the learner nor open to the learner's rewriting, which is the contrast the engagement-layer ruling names. It cannot be cited as showing that literacy training works for a platform's third-party coordination; the children's algorithms are their own.

Second, diSessa's three-pillar definition (material, mental, social) is a usable statement of the "complex of conventions" the author accepts in D6. The material pillar names representation and writing conventions, which is where word separation sits, and the social pillar names the community that adopts it. This gives the paper a literacy-theory way to say that spacing was one convention among several. It does not mention spacing.
