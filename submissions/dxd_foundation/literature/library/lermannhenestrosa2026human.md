## What it argues
The paper takes the EU AI Act's human-oversight requirement (Article 14, recitals 27 and 48, as the authors cite them in §1) and argues that it "rests on strong assumptions regarding human cognitive capacities, the epistemic access to complex AI systems and effective control over them" (§1). Three weaknesses (§2): "(i) the systemic fallibility of human judgment, (ii) practical limitations of how human oversight is implemented and currently envisioned, and (iii) conceptual ambiguities of meaningful oversight." The authors add: "human oversight achieves only one key objective of assigning responsibility to persons rather technical systems" (§2).

§3 gives three illustrations. §3.1 ("Anthropocentric Overconfidence") lists biases from the literature: automation bias, confirmation bias, people over-relying on humans even where an AI would do better, and an inability "to correctly decide when to diverge from algorithmic recommendations," with the claim that experts show the same limits (citing a mammography study). §3.2 ("Ethical Silver Bullet") argues that oversight shifts liability: it "reassigns responsibility to persons rather than technical systems," so that supervisors "might be used as foci of liability." §3.3 ("Symbolic and Epistemically Weak") points to closed models and proprietary architectures that deny supervisors expert access, and says that general-purpose AI, "products deployed at scale," cannot be made safe by user education alone.

§4 gives three remedies: incentive-based ethical stress-testing by independent researchers and civil-society testers, with protected reporting channels and rewards; "normative clarity," including "hard normative stops, preventing certain model behaviors regardless of contextual judgments" and mandatory use declarations; and accounting for limits of human judgment, with "non-oversight zones" and "meta-oversight structures." The closing view: "oversight is most effective when its limits are clearly outlined and when it is supported by non-human safeguards rather than used to compensate for their absence" (§4.3). The paper reports no data of its own.

## Relation to the argument
It supports premise P12 (people monitor badly) with a legal-governance version, and it moves the remedy from the person to structure, as the paper does. The stop-rules that "cannot be overridden by human oversight" resemble Nadella's external controls (P11) and the paper's rules in the mediator (P8). It also speaks to who holds the rule: the authors worry that supervisors become liability sinks and that providers escape responsibility, which sits with P10. Unlike the paper, it lets the regulator and provider hold the convention (rulings D8, D9 are not touched), and it has no notion of a user-side convention.

Facet check: facet Z (lines 284, 368) summarises it from the abstract as an institutional remedy, "no history of reading; no triad; none addresses Nadella." The full text agrees. It also says that general-purpose AI users are not served by "AI literacy" alone (§3.3), which is close to ruling D3, but it makes the point for regulatory reasons.
