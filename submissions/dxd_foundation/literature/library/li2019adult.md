## What it argues

The study tests whether older readers are more sensitive than young readers to everyday variation in letter and word spacing. Mean sentence reading times (Table 1, ms), extra-condensed to extra-expanded:

| | −20% | −10% | Normal | +10% | +20% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Young | 2,228 | 2,148 | 2,149 | 2,245 | 2,257 |
| Older | 3,400 | 3,061 | 2,884 | 2,875 | 2,977 |

The abstract's summary: "Condensing but not expanding text spacing disrupted reading more for the older adults." The age difference in reading times was largest for condensed and extra-condensed text (Results, Sentence-Level Measures). For young adults both directions of change lengthened sentence times somewhat; for older adults the cost came mainly from condensing (Discussion). Expanded spacing helped neither group: "there was little indication that increased spacing benefited reading for either age group" (Discussion). Forward saccades lengthened and word skipping fell as spacing grew, equally for both groups, which the authors take as evidence that "saccadic control is driven by the physical characteristics of words" and is preserved in older age (Discussion).

Spacing did not change the word frequency effect for either group, although the frequency effect was larger for older adults overall, in total reading time rather than gaze duration (abstract; Discussion). The authors contrast this with unspaced text, which does enlarge it, and conclude that "lexical identification can proceed as normal so long as visual cues to word boundaries are available" (Discussion). They attribute older readers' extra cost to crowding from condensed letter spacing, not to lexical processing (abstract; Discussion).

## Relation to the argument

This bears on P3 and P5 in the same way as `mcgowan2015effects`, and it **narrows** the draft's oculomotor account. Spacing has a working range. Below it, crowding slows older readers most; above it, the extra width costs fixations and helps nobody. The draft's "white channels" image is right about the mechanism but silent on dose. For the paper's analogy, the lesson is that a re-representation can be miscalibrated for one group of users (here, older eyes) while staying adequate for another.

It also **supports** the draft's lexical claim from a new direction: as long as some boundary cue is present, word identification proceeds normally; only its absence (unspaced text) disrupts it.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Li et al. (2019) are not cited in DRAFT_v2. They bear on these lines:
- Line 113 ("By carving white channels between character clusters, spacing gives the oculomotor system targets for the next saccade, and removing the spaces changes where the eyes land as well as how fast words are identified (Rayner et al., 1998)"). **Supports the targeting claim and narrows the general one.** Saccade length tracks the physical extent of words (Discussion), but wider channels gave no benefit (Table 1), and the cost of narrower ones fell mainly on older readers.

- Line 119 ("An ergonomic intervention … must match the visual search affordances of the medium to the cognitive tasks of the user"). **Supports** with an age-specific case.

## Caution

Small samples (20 and 15). Inter-letter and interword spacing changed together, so their separate effects are not identified; the authors' attribution of the age effect to letter spacing is a conjecture. English sentences in a monospaced font.
