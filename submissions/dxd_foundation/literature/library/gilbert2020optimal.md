## What it argues

The task offers, trial by trial, 10 points per remembered item using unaided memory or a lower value (1–9) using reminders. Because unaided accuracy was about 52–60% and reminder accuracy 92–98%, each participant has an optimal indifference point (OIP); the actual indifference point (AIP) is fitted from their choices, and the gap is the "reminder bias" (ms. pp. 17–18).

Experiment 1: OIP 5.5 and 5.6 in the two sessions, AIP 4.5 and 4.8; the bias was significant in both, d = 1.0 and 0.82, correlated across sessions, r = .47, and could not be explained by time, since the external strategy took longer in session 1 (61.2 s against 50.4 s) (pp. 19–21). Experiment 2 added pay and, for half, advice computed from the participant's own accuracy. Unadvised participants showed the bias (OIP 5.8, AIP 5.1, d = 0.98); advised participants showed none (both 6.1); the group difference was d = 0.55 (p. 28). Participants were underconfident in unaided memory, and underconfidence correlated with reminder bias, r = −.31 (p. 29). Experiment 3 manipulated practice difficulty and feedback valence; confidence shifted without any change in accuracy, reminder bias ranged from 1.2 (easy practice, positive feedback) to 2.9 (difficult, negative), all four groups were biased (d > .87), and mediation analyses put the effect of the interventions through confidence (pp. 35–38). Since the overconfident group was still biased, "the bias cannot be exhaustively explained by metacognitive error" (p. 40). The discussion links the result to automation complacency and automation bias, where "trust (in the abilities of an external tool) and confidence (in one's own abilities)" drive over-reliance (p. 42). It notes that in everyday life "there is typically a long delay" between a strategy decision and feedback on whether it worked, so forgetting "may not present a clear learning signal" (p. 46).

## Relation to the argument

**Costs and limits of offloading.** People use external aids beyond the optimum, stably, even when paid to choose well; the bias is driven partly by underconfidence and partly by something else, plausibly effort avoidance. For the paper this is the quantified version of the "drift away from reliance on internal capabilities" that `risko2016cognitive` predicts.

**D3 (disclosure).** Experiment 2 is a case where telling people something changed what they did: individually computed advice removed the bias. That looks like a success for a literacy-style intervention and the draft should say so. The qualification is that the advice disclosed nothing about the tool; it reported the person's own measured performance, which is feedback, not disclosure of a mechanism.

**"Ecological feedback immediacy" (P9).** The p. 46 passage is a direct statement of the abstract's condition from inside this literature: without feedback close to the decision, metacognition does not calibrate. `weis2019using` shows the calibration happening when feedback is prompt.

**Limits.** Dyadic, seconds-long, memory for intentions. The authors expect biases "in either direction" in naturalistic tasks (p. 46).

## Caution

In-press manuscript; journal pages differ. Experiment 1 is small (N = 41). The task is hard by design (about 50% unaided), which the authors say favours underconfidence; easier or longer-horizon tasks may show the reverse bias. Nothing involves a counterpart or a mediator.
