# Weis, P. P., & Wiese, E. (2019). Using tools to help us think: Actual but also believed reliability modulates cognitive offloading. *Human Factors, 61*(2), 243–254.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1177/0018720818797553 · **Read depth:** full_text (the authors' PsyArXiv manuscript, 30 pp. with running-head page numbers; saved as `dxd_foundation_local/pdfs/V/weis2018.pdf`; locators are manuscript pages, not the journal's 243–254; read in full including Tables 1–2, key points and references; Figures 1–5 read through captions) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article (Human Factors) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** one laboratory experiment, George Mason University undergraduates, N = 122 (126 recruited), 6 × 2 × 2 × 3 mixed design with reliability (50–100%) within and belief (naive, congruent, incongruent) between participants, 240 rotation trials each · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (person and a rotation knob of varying reliability) · **Facets:** V · **Read:** 2026-10-15

## What it argues

An extended mental-rotation task let people rotate the stimulus mentally or with a knob that failed on a known fraction of trials. Both the knob's actual reliability, F(5, 595) = 23.69, ηG² = .042, and the instructed belief about it, F(2, 119) = 3.49, ηG² = .035, changed how often people used it (ms. p. 12). Naive participants, told only that the knob "might not work all the time", used it as often as those told the true reliability (70.54% against 72.56% of trials), so "participants promptly picked up on the actual knob reliability" (p. 13). Participants told a reliability 30 percentage points below the truth used the knob on 55.71% of trials (p. 13), and this false belief faded over the 40-trial block only at low reliabilities (50–70%), not at high ones (p. 15). Usefulness ratings tracked actual reliability only, with a kink at 70%, which the authors note is Wickens and Dixon's crossover for automation worth having (p. 17). The introduction restates Risko and Dunn (2015): people wrote letters down on more than 40% of trials when two letters had to be held, where unaided recall was above 97%, and on about 90% of trials for ten letters, where unaided recall was below 1% (p. 4). The applied conclusion: "an external resource's utility should be made transparent and pre-existing beliefs should be adjusted prior to interaction" (abstract).

## Relation to the argument

**P9 and "ecological feedback immediacy."** The naive condition is a small demonstration of the author's claim in its favourable form: with prompt, unequivocal feedback (the knob either turned the shape or did not) people calibrated use within a block. The incongruent condition is the limit: a belief installed by instruction persisted where feedback was less diagnostic, and the drift back took dozens of trials even where it happened. For a platform, where feedback is slower and noisier, the second pattern is the more relevant.

**D3.** Here an instruction did change behaviour, in the wrong direction, by lowering trust in a reliable tool; and correct instruction added nothing to experience. That is a cleaner version of the author's point that disclosure acts on belief rather than on navigation.

**Costs.** The Risko and Dunn numbers quoted on p. 4 are the facet's only reachable statement of that study, which was not accessible in full: people offload at ceiling and under-offload when it would help most.

## Caution

Preprint; the journal version may differ. One session, students, an artificial device, beliefs set by a one-line instruction. The belief effect is modest (ηG² = .035) and the time-course analysis is exploratory with p ≈ .10 after correction. The paper cites Gilbert (2015) *Consciousness and Cognition*, not `gilbert2015strategic`.
