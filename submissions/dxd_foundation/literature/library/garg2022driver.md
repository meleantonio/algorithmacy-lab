## What it argues

The paper asks how a platform should pay drivers under surge so that accepting every trip is a driver's best strategy. The setting is "decoupled pricing": "the platform has under its control both the price p_i(τ) charged to the rider and the payment w_i(τ) paid to the driver for a trip of length τ — and the proportion of these two values may vary across trips" (p. 8). "In practice, the platform quotes the rider a price and 'guarantees' fulfillment if a ride is requested; driver payments should thus primarily ensure that all requested rides are fulfilled" (p. 8). Incentive compatibility (IC) means "an earnings-maximizing driver (who knows all the primitives, w, and the trip length τ at request time) accepts every trip request" (p. 6).

Theorem 1 (p. 9): in a single-state world, paying a constant rate per unit time is IC. Theorem 2 (pp. 10–11): with surge that comes and goes, multiplicative surge is not IC; drivers "benefit from rejecting long trips in a non-surge state, and short trips in the surge state" (p. 3). The intuition is quoted from a driver: "I thoroughly dislike short trips ESPECIALLY when I'm picking up in a waning surge zone" (p. 11). Theorem 3 (p. 13): IC prices exist of the form w_i(τ) = m_i τ + z_i q_{i→j}(τ), and "the non-surge state is difficult to make incentive compatible" (p. 13). Numerically, additive surge is IC "in much more of this regime than is multiplicative surge, supporting Uber's recent shift from multiplicative to additive surge" (p. 17). In the RideAustin data (surge factor 1 in 70% of trips, p. 19), under multiplicative surge "drivers would have often had higher earnings had they rejected short trip requests," while additive surge "increases the value of short trips by about $15 per hour" at high surge (p. 22). Two practice notes: "Drivers often cannot see the rider's destination or the trip length until they pick up the rider" (p. 9), and "destination discrimination is against Uber's guidelines and could lead to deactivation" (n. 8, p. 9).

## Relation to the argument

**Question 4 ("binds and optimizes").** The strongest formal statement of binding in the library: the platform sets two prices for one trip and guarantees the rider fulfilment, so the driver's payment is a design variable chosen to secure that guarantee. The platform's objective is explicit (profit or welfare, decomposed in Appendix A.1 into a rider-pricing problem and a driver-IC constraint, p. 8).

**P10 (a rule in the mediator that removes a strategic task).** This is a documented instance of the facet-P candidate convention. Cherry-picking is the driver's strategic task; a change in the payment *structure* (additive instead of multiplicative) removes the gain from it. The removal is partial (Theorem 3's feasibility range; non-surge periods may not be fixable), and the authors note that switching schemes "may be undesirable for transparency and communication reasons" (p. 18). It also shows what "irreducible" looks like operationally: the driver's best response depends on the rider-side demand process (λ_i, F_i) through the state transitions, so the driver–platform dyad cannot be priced without the rider side.

**Where it cuts against the author.** The remedy is pricing, not interface; nothing a driver reads changes. And the IC notion assumes a driver who "knows all the primitives" (p. 6), the opposite of the author's reader who cannot see the mediator.

## Caution

Second author affiliated with Uber; the paper presents "the theoretical foundation that has informed the design of Uber's new additive driver surge mechanism" (abstract). The empirical part is a simulation on completed trips, "not the result of an experiment" (p. 18). Read as a preprint; the journal version may differ. The model has no space and a binary Markovian surge (p. 9).
