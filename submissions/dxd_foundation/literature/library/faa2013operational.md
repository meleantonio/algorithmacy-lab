## What it argues

The working group updates the FAA's 1996 human-factors team report. It issues 29 findings and 18 recommendations on how flightcrews use autopilot, autothrottle, flight director and FMS (Executive Summary, pp. 1–10). Four findings bear on this project.

Finding 1: "Pilots mitigate safety and operational risks on a frequent basis, and the aviation system is designed to rely on that mitigation" (p. 2). The group says an exclusive focus on pilot error ignores these positive actions, which "are rarely noted explicitly" in data (pp. 30–31). LOSA data show "only one in ten flights is completed as originally planned and entered into the FMS," and adverse weather is a threat in almost 60% of normal flights (pp. 29-30).

Finding 2: vulnerabilities in manual flight skills; over 60% of the accident reports reviewed identified a manual handling error (p. 31).

Finding 4: pilots "sometimes rely too much on automated systems and may be reluctant to intervene." In roughly one quarter of the accidents reviewed pilots were overconfident in automation; pilot situation awareness was a factor in over 50%; pilots were "out of the control loop" in over 50% (pp. 36–38, Figs. 10–12). The group also names high system reliability as fostering "insufficient cross verification," and policies that favour automation as eroding manual skill (p. 38).

Finding 10 (p. 57): "task management, the maintenance of vigilance and avoidance of distraction are not trivial tasks," and the question "how could the pilot have missed that?" is the wrong frame. On training (3.2.5.1), a Boeing survey (Holder 2013) found 99% of pilots rate monitoring and cross-verification as important, while only 47% said detecting and managing errors was explicitly discussed in recurrent training, 34% said covered "somehow", and 19% marginally or not at all (p. 47). LOSA data suggest "lax procedures ... may have become normalized because of the underlying reliability of the data that is being cross verified," so "simply adding further cross-verification requirements will not address" the problem. Recommendation 2 (p. 108) asks, for the near term, for better training and procedures on autoflight mode awareness and, for the longer term, for equipment design with fewer and simpler autoflight modes and better feedback on mode transitions.

## Relation to the argument

On ruling D7 (pilots act rather than read the situation), the report supports the caution already flagged in SOURCE.md. The pilot's job is partly to supervise automation and cross-verify data, and the system is designed to rely on that. It also shows the failure mode of a mediator that works well: reliability erodes checking. That bears on P12 and on Nadella's reliance on human review of a reliable-looking system. Recommendation 2 (reduce mode complexity, improve feedback) is a design-over-training move of the kind the paper's precedent favours. The "Pilot mitigation" finding cuts the other way for any claim that people cannot cope: skilled operators do mitigate risk often.
