## What it argues

Smith and Hancock define situation awareness (SA) as "adaptive, externally directed consciousness" (p. 137). The abstract gives two moves. The goal that SA serves must come from "a normative arbiter in the task environment," and SA is "the invariant at the core of the agent's perception-action cycle that supports skilled performance" (p. 137). They take the cycle from Neisser (1976) via Tenney et al. (1992): "Knowledge directs the agent's activity in the environment. That activity samples and perhaps anticipates or alters the environment, which in turn informs the agent" (p. 141). SA is therefore "not resident in the agent but exists in the invariant interaction of the agent and his or her environment" (p. 146).

Two consequences follow. First, a private goal cannot yield SA: "If the agent were to dictate private, incontestable (but dynamic) goals, SA would always be perfect because whatever was perceived would be that goal" (p. 139). The car-in-the-parking-lot example shows the fix: state the goal to someone else and the performance can be judged (p. 140). Second, they reject SA as a "snapshot of the agent's current mental model" (p. 142) and take aim at Endsley's (1988) definition, arguing that her three levels (perception, comprehension, projection) "sound very similar to three stages of information processing" and so confound knowledge with process (p. 145). Air traffic controllers who "lose the picture" illustrate the point: SA builds the picture and signals when it is failing (p. 142).

The worked example is the "risk space" for commercial flight (pp. 143–144). Its axes are horizontal separation and relative velocity between two aircraft; lines through the origin give time to collision; two thresholds divide it into three decision regions (proceed, anticipate, resolve). The authors call it "an observer construct" and "a paramorphic representation" of skilled pilots and controllers, and say it is not claimed that any individual visualises it (p. 144). They end with a design remark: because "the behavior that SA generates is amenable to manipulation through change in the way information is displayed," the emergent properties of a task can be displayed "so that the critical aspects of a situation are brought to awareness" (pp. 146–147), citing Vicente and Rasmussen (1992).

## Relation to the argument

Ruling D7 holds that people understand "THROUGH deciding and quickly ingesting the results" and that "pilots dont spend time reading the situation." Smith and Hancock support a softer form. In their cycle, acting and perceiving are halves of one loop, since action "samples and perhaps anticipates or alters the environment" (p. 141), so "act or read" is a false pair. They also tie awareness to an external goal and to a task whose objectives "can be externally verified" (p. 146). That condition matters for the paper's mediated triad. On a platform the arbiter of performance (the owner) is also an interested party, and the person's own goal may be unstated. By the authors' definition, a person with no externally specifiable goal has introspection, not SA. Their pilots are not shown to "act without reading": the risk space is a representation pilots and controllers attend to, and the authors speak of "information" sources they "attend" to (p. 143). The design remark at pp. 146–147 sits close to premise P5 (re-represent a task so the artefact carries structure) and cites Vicente and Rasmussen, a source the paper already uses.

## Caution

A 1995 definitional paper that offers no data. The risk space is stated as a hypothesis for one aviation task. The paper is a reply to Endsley's mental-model account, so its claim that SA is an "invariant" is contested, not settled. It does not address monitoring workload, trust or platforms. The scan was read through OCR; page 141 interleaves two columns and a figure.
