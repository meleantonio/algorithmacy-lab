## What it argues

The study asks whether spaces help Chinese readers when they are placed where readers agree a word boundary lies. The authors start from the observation that Chinese readers disagree about word boundaries; one rating study found average inter-rater agreement of 64% (p. 2). Each character transition in their sentences carries the share of raters who put a boundary there. Experiment 1 compared normal unspaced text with "heavily spaced" text (a space wherever at least 5% of raters put a boundary). Experiment 2 compared unspaced text with "lightly spaced" text (a space only where at least 90% agreed) (pp. 3–5).

Experiment 1: words between spaces were read faster on every word-level measure (first fixation 221 vs 232 ms, gaze 236 vs 246 ms, total 294 vs 311 ms; Table 2), but sentence reading times were "numerically almost identical", 3,158 vs 3,159 ms, with a Bayes factor below 0.001 favouring the null (p. 7). The authors trace the cancellation to more saccades in spaced sentences (18.33 vs 17.30 per sentence) and a longer summed saccade time (788 vs 730 ms), so that "the processing advantage seen in the spaced condition at the word-level is canceled at the sentence level" (p. 8).

Experiment 2: lightly spaced sentences were read faster, 3,442 vs 3,511 ms, "a relative difference of 2%; d = 0.05", with a gaze-duration benefit at the threshold of significance (p = 0.041; p. 9) and more word skipping (26.0% vs 23.3%; p. 10). Comprehension did not differ in either experiment (mean 87.5% correct across experiments, all ps > 0.626, p. 7; condition means 0.88–0.89 in Table 2, p. 8).

Their conclusion: "spacing only becomes beneficial when readers find spaces at suitable word boundaries, and even then, the processing advantage is minute" (p. 11), and "spacing is not an effective segmentation cue in Chinese reading" (p. 11). The literature review reports that removing spaces slows English readers "by 30–50% (Rayner and Pollatsek, 1996)" and that Winskel et al. (2009) found Thai sentence reading times "5% longer in the spaced condition" (p. 2).

## Relation to the argument

This bears on P4 (for readers of unspaced scripts, added spaces have mixed effects; no gain for skilled Chinese adults). It **supports P4 and refines it.** The Bai et al. (2008) null replicates with a larger sample and a Bayes factor. The refinement is that the null is a sum of a real word-level benefit and a saccade cost, and that the sign of the effect depends on where the spaces go. That matters for P5 and the human-factors reading: a re-representation helps only if it marks units the reader already recognises. A space at a boundary readers dispute adds a saccade and no information.

It also bears on P3, from the review only: the 30–50% slowdown for English is quoted from Rayner and Pollatsek (1996), which is a secondary report here.

## Caution

Readers were Chinese students living in Canada (mean 4.4 years; p. 4), all young adults. The two spacing conditions were run on different groups, so heavy and light spacing are compared only through the shared unspaced baseline. Spaces were one character wide. Effects are small: the light-spacing gain is d = 0.05. The paper does not test learners or children.
