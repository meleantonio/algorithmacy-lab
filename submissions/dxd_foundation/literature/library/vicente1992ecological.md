## What it argues

Vicente and Rasmussen want to extend direct-manipulation interfaces to complex plants such as nuclear stations. The problem is the event nobody foresaw: "unanticipated events can and do happen" and are the major cause of life-threatening accidents (pp. 2–3), so a design that lists anticipated faults cannot help.

Two axioms organise the paper. The abstraction hierarchy (§III) describes the plant's constraints at several levels, so an operator can see when one breaks. Rasmussen's skills–rules–knowledge taxonomy (§IV) says information read as signals, signs or symbols triggers skill-, rule- or knowledge-based control. The goal that follows is "not to force cognitive control to a higher level than the demands of the task require" while supporting all three levels (p. 21). Three principles (§V.A): let the operator act directly on a display whose structure mirrors the structure of movements (p. 22); map plant constraints one-to-one onto display cues, to avoid "procedural traps" like the pressurizer-level cue at Three Mile Island (pp. 23–24); show the abstraction hierarchy as an external model for problem-solving (p. 25).

The evidence is a review. "perceptual processing is fast, effortless, and proceeds in parallel, whereas analytical problem solving is slow, laborious, and proceeds in a serial fashion," yet "No level is globally superior to any other" (p. 14). Lower levels "can only be activated in familiar situations because they require that the operator be attuned to the perceptual features of the environment," while knowledge-based control "allows operators to cope with novelty" (p. 14). Plant operators suit perceptual design because they "are highly skilled and have extensive experience" (p. 14). People also lean on what the display shows: an incomplete representation "can actually lead to worse performance than having no representation at all" (p. 18).

In DURESS (§VII, pp. 33–34), the interface showing all hierarchy levels gave better diagnosis than physical state alone, "primarily for experts." Whether that holds for non-experts "remains to be demonstrated" (p. 35).

## Relation to the argument

1. **Making constraints visible is mainstream artefact-side design** (facet E). Principle 2 turns inference into perception, the move the paper claims for word spacing, so it supports P5. It also bears on the "not legibility" line that ruling D3 replaced: EID is a legibility programme, so the paper needs the narrower statement.

2. **The limits fall on the weak joint, P7 to P8.** The framework works only "to the extent that designers understand the system they are building" (p. 26) and assumes skilled operators facing the same process (p. 14). An adaptive, interested mediator offers neither (`ARGUMENT_MAP.md`, exposure 2). The authors leave "detection, diagnosis, and compensation" with the operator (p. 10). They also state that "the complexity inherent in the process cannot be displaced," only handled "by the designer, the machine, or the operator" (pp. 4–5), which cuts against any picture where moving work to an artefact makes it vanish.

3. **D7.** Acting on a display is the fast mode for familiar tasks. For novelty the authors reserve slower, symbolic reasoning, so the source does not back "pilots act rather than read the situation" for unfamiliar events.

## Caution

A manuscript copy, so page numbers do not match the journal. The experiment gets a few paragraphs: no effect sizes or N, and the experts are "theoretical experts," not plant operators. The setting is process control, and the authors call generalisation beyond it "an open question" (p. 27). No basis here for gains by novices, or for any setting with an interested third party.
