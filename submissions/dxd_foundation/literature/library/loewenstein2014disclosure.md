## What it argues

Loewenstein, Sunstein and Golman review when mandated disclosure works. Psychology complicates the standard economic account in both directions: "limited attention, motivated attention, and biased assessments of probability on the part of information recipients can significantly diminish, or even reverse, the intended effects," and "In many cases, disclosure does not much affect the recipients of the information but does significantly affect the behavior of the providers" (abstract, p. 391). "A pervasive theme is that disclosure may have little effect on recipients but large effects on providers" (p. 398).

On recipients, they cite limited attention (fewer than 3% read website privacy disclosures, p. 399), inattention to missing information (p. 400), motivated attention (pp. 400–401), biased probabilities (p. 401), and two backfire effects of conflict-of-interest disclosure: moral licensing of advisors (§3.6, p. 402) and insinuation anxiety in advisees (§3.7, pp. 402–403). On providers, the "telltale heart effect" (§3.8): "In the classic account of how disclosure works (e.g., Fung et al. 2007), mandated disclosure leads to changes in the behavior of disclosees, which in turn cause disclosers to clean up their act" (p. 403). Los Angeles restaurant hygiene ratings are "a case often cited as a paradigm for successful disclosure," but "in many situations (to some extent including the case of restaurant hygiene ratings), an industry response can be found amid little evidence of a consumer response" (p. 403). Footnote 8 adds that the grade cards "also led inspectors to distort their ratings" (p. 403).

Remedies (§4): simplification, where categorical labels "such as stars or letter grades" aid comprehension (p. 405); standardised and comparative information (Kling et al. 2012: plan switching 28% against 17%, p. 407); social comparison (pp. 408–409); vividness (p. 410); and intermediaries who process standardised data (p. 411). Some simplified disclosures still failed: "making disclosure simple and accessible may provide insufficient help to consumers who lack the tools to process that information" (p. 408). Policy makers should not let disclosure substitute for "other, often more effective, regulatory interventions" (p. 412).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D3 and premise P8.** Strong support on the recipient side: disclosure often fails to change what recipients do, for psychological reasons, and even simplified versions can fail (p. 408). The review also qualifies D3, since standardised, comparative and personalised information sometimes did change choices (p. 407).

**Ruling D8 and premise P12.** The telltale heart effect gives the paper a second channel: disclosure acts on the discloser. Applied to the platforms the paper discusses, the discloser is the owner of the mediator, so mandated disclosure may matter more as pressure on the platform than as help to users.

**What it cuts.** §4.6 proposes that intermediaries read disclosures for consumers (p. 411). That is a remedy built on reading done by someone else, a fourth party the paper's triad does not include.

## Caution

A review by three self-described proponents of disclosure (p. 395), focused on buyer–seller and advisor–advisee settings; most evidence is about consumers, not platforms or algorithms. The authors note they know of no field experiment that studies demand and supply sides together (p. 398).
