# Heiland, H. (2023). The social construction of algorithms: A reassessment of algorithmic management in food delivery gig work. *New Technology, Work and Employment, 40*(1), 1–19.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1111/ntwe.12282 · **Read depth:** full_text (published version, open access, from the EconStor copy; article pagination 1–19; received 1 Feb 2023, accepted 5 Nov 2023; issue 40(1) is dated 2025) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article (special issue paper) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** qualitative, multimethod: 35 semi-structured interviews (riders in seven German cities plus platform managers, average 80 min), more than 500 hours of ethnography including auto-ethnography with A/B-style tests, and online ethnography of six rider chat groups; two platforms (Foodora, Deliveroo), February–October 2018 · **Parties modeled:** worker–platform with a customer and restaurant behind it (the allocation algorithm sits between worker and platform) · **Facets:** I · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues

Heiland argues that an allocation algorithm is not a fixed object but a social construction on four levels: planned by management, programmed, curated by dispatchers, and applied by users (pp. 4–5). The last level is where his data sit. Using Orlikowski and Gash's "technological frames", he separates three steps in how riders construct the algorithm: frames (broad assumptions), theories (about how the order allocation works) and practices (what they do because of the theories) (pp. 6–7).

The riders' frames are two: the algorithm is poorly built, or the platform uses it to watch and punish. Their theories are many and contradictory: cars are preferred, speed earns or costs orders, rejecting orders is penalised, performance reports drive allocation (pp. 10–13). Heiland tests several against the platforms' actual logic through manager interviews and his own trials, and finds them wrong. Foodora's performance reports did not affect allocation; he rode slowly for a stretch and fast a month later with no change in orders, "confirmed by an A/B-test" (p. 13). Deliveroo's "order acceptance rate" field was displayed without a value because one app served several countries, and riders "could 'reject as much as they want'" without consequence (p. 12). Riders still acted on the wrong theories: one rode faster when orders were scarce, others "hide[d] from orders", faked GPS, or feigned technical problems (pp. 12–14).

He draws two conclusions. Opacity itself controls: "The opacity of the algorithms is therefore a control instrument on its own, which can be described as black box power" (p. 15). And workers keep limited agency over what the algorithm sees, which he calls "management of visibility" (p. 15). In a survey he cites, 63% of riders said they felt very often or often at the mercy of the technology, against 39% of German employees (p. 9).

## Relation to the argument

D7. In facet I this is the nearest thing to a field test of "we understand THROUGH deciding", though it is qualitative. Riders acted for years under an adaptive allocator and built theories from what they saw. Many were wrong, and the wrong ones shaped what they did. The theories that were corrected were corrected by the author's deliberate paired trials, repeating an action in matched situations, and by managers' testimony. Plain exposure did not do it. That fits the argument map's note that learning by acting needs a regularity to learn from, and it adds that an interested allocator may leave misreadings in place: the "misunderstandings work in the interest of the platforms and are not corrected by them" (p. 15).

P7 and P10. This is the empirical anchor for the missing convention: riders infer what the mediator does and share the guesses in chat groups. They have a practice, not a convention, and the platform's owner benefits from its absence. The "agency laundering" complaint, riders saying the platform hides behind the algorithm (p. 11), also bears on who may withdraw a convention.

D3. Riders know that the algorithm exists and is used; their trouble is that its operating principles are not disclosed ("explicitly communicated without making their operating principles transparent", p. 5). That matches the argument map's point that awareness does not equal competence.

Facet check: facet I's bearing line, "trial and experience produced theories, some false, and false theories shaped behaviour", is right. It omits the author's own A/B trials, which show deliberate contrast as the exception that worked.

## Caution

Qualitative and German, two platforms in 2018, one researcher who was also a rider. Findings about which rider theories are false rest on manager interviews and the author's own trials; platform logic may have changed since. The paper shows beliefs shaping action; it does not measure learning outcomes or compare designs. Nothing about the counterpart (customer, restaurant) as a party who also reads the system.
