# Zhang, J., & Norman, D. A. (1994). Representations in distributed cognitive tasks. *Cognitive Science, 18*(1), 87–122.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1207/s15516709cog1801_3 · **Read depth:** full_text (journal article PDF, 36 pp., journal pagination 87–122 intact; the PDF has an OCR text layer with run-together words, so I read the text with spacing normalised and checked page assignments against the page headers; the 16 figures were read through their captions, not inspected as images) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** theory plus four laboratory experiments on Tower of Hanoi isomorphs, UCSD undergraduates, N = 24 (Exp. 1A), 24 (Exp. 1B), 18 (Exp. 2), 36 (Exp. 3); within-subjects in 1A, 1B and 2, mixed design in 3 · **Parties modeled:** person–artefact (a performer and the external representations of one task) · **Facets:** E · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues

The authors propose that "the representational system of a distributed cognitive task" is a set of internal and external representations that together stand for the task's abstract structure (Abstract; "Distributed Representations", pp. 89–90). Their method, representational analysis, splits a task into levels and varies one level at a time. They define the representational effect: "different isomorphic representations of a common formal structure can cause dramatically different cognitive behaviors" (p. 88). Their examples are Arabic against Roman numerals (73 × 27 against LXXIII × XXVII) and the Copernican revolution (p. 88).

They test the idea on the Tower of Hanoi, using cover stories (oranges and plates, straws, coffee cups). A rule is *internal* if the instructions state it and the subject memorises it, and *external* if the physical set-up enforces it without being stated (p. 93). Results:

- **Exp. 1A and 1B (problem-space structure).** With all rules internal, difficulty did not rise steadily with the number of rules: the hardest problem was the one with three internal rules, not the most constrained one (p. 107). With all but one rule external, difficulty rose with the number of rules (p. 107). The easiest and hardest problems differed in solution time "as large as a ratio of one to ten" (p. 107).
- **Exp. 2 (same three rules, distributed differently).** Difficulty fell as more rules were external: the all-internal version was hardest, and the versions with one or two external rules were easier (pp. 109–110). "External rules seem to be error proof": no subject broke an external rule (p. 110). In the coffee version, 16 of 18 subjects moved the smallest cup first, against 11 of 18 and 10 of 18 in the two versions where that rule was internal (p. 111).
- **Exp. 3 (nine isomorphs, dimensions carried by size, location or colour).** Location, a spatial dimension, beat size and colour on solution time and errors for the nominal dimension, and beat size for the ordinal one (pp. 114–116). Colour as ordinal dimension had to be memorised and did not differ reliably from size (p. 116).

The general discussion draws five properties of external representations (pp. 118–119): memory aid; information "directly perceived and used without being interpreted and formulated explicitly" (few subjects could state the external rules afterwards); anchoring and structuring behaviour; changing "the nature of a task"; indispensable part of the system. On the fourth, the authors follow Norman (1991): from the system's view an external representation makes a task easier, "from the person's view (internal representations only)" it changes the task (p. 119). They also note that "different processes are activated by different representations, but not vice versa", with perceptual processes tied to external representations (p. 118).

## Relation to the argument

**Premise P5** (the scribes' move re-represented the task). The source gives P5 its vocabulary and its best-supported mechanism: the same abstract structure, carried partly in the world, changes what the person must hold and compute. The p. 119 point that the person's task differs from the system's task bears on the "task the person keeps" test the argument map marks as unsourced (Exposure 1), and on the dropped claim that reading "no longer required the training".

**Facet check:** the facet and the argument map cite the definition of the representational effect at p. 89. In the journal pagination it sits at p. 88 (the heading "The Representational Effect" opens p. 88; p. 89 holds the list of problem-isomorph studies and the "Distributed Representations" principle). The p. 118 quotation is correct. The facet does not mention that the paper is silent on text, reading or writing; I found no passage on any of them.

**Cuts against, or limits.** (1) The isomorphs carry identical information by construction. Spacing may not: where an unspaced string has two lawful parses, the spaced text commits to one (the facet's own caution; the source does not address it). (2) Every external rule in the experiments is enforced by physics or by shared cultural knowledge, and the designer knows the rule set. The authors say of the coffee rule that it is "not a truly external rule with rigid physical constraints, but an external rule grounded in cultural knowledge" (p. 106). That case is the nearest thing in the paper to a convention, and it bears on **P10** and ruling D8: a rule the performer cannot violate, or that everyone shares, differs from a rule an interested owner can withdraw. The source does not take up that distinction. (3) It offers no evidence on mediators with interests of their own (**P7, P8**).

## Caution

Four lab experiments on one puzzle family with student subjects; the paper reports mean differences and p-values from a Fisher PLSD test, and the OCR garbles several p-value cells in Tables 1–3, so I give directions, not p-values. Starting and ending positions were not randomised (stated limitation, pp. 102, 109, 114). It is a 1994 paper and contains no field or reading data. Cite it for the representational effect, the internal/external decomposition and the system/person distinction, not for any claim about text.
