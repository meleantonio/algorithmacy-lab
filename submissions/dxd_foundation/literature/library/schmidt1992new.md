## What it argues

"Manipulations that maximize performance during training can be detrimental in the long term; conversely, manipulations that degrade the speed of acquisition can support the long-term goals of training" (abstract, p. 207). The reason is that acquisition performance confounds learning with temporary effects, so learning must be read off delayed retention or transfer tests (pp. 208–209). Trainers and trainees are both misled: in Baddeley and Longman's keyboard study "the schedules that were most preferred by subjects produced the least learning" (p. 209).

Three paradigms. (1) Random against blocked practice: blocked is better during acquisition, random better at retention after 10 days (Shea & Morgan 1979, pp. 210). Spacing: in Landauer and Bjork's name-learning study, tests with no intervening items averaged about 95% during acquisition and 33% at final retention; tests spaced by 4 or 5 items ran about 43% during acquisition and 41% at retention (p. 211). (2) Feedback: "any variation of feedback in practice that makes the information more immediate, more accurate, more frequent, or more useful for modifying behavior will contribute to learning, as measured during the acquisition phase" has "served as the basis for instructional practice in many settings, as well as for the design of simulators. Recent evidence, however, suggests that this generalization must be qualified" (p. 212). In Schmidt et al. (1989), feedback after every trial gave the smallest errors in practice and the largest on a two-day retention test; summaries over 15 trials reversed this (pp. 212–213). Feedback on 50% of trials, faded, beat 100% at retention (Winstein & Schmidt 1990, p. 213), and Schooler and Anderson (1990) found the same for learning LISP (p. 213). The explanations offered: "frequent feedback comes to be a part of the task," blocks processing of one's own response-produced feedback, or keeps performance too variable to stabilise (p. 213). (3) Variable practice transfers better than constant practice (pp. 214–215).

The general principle is transfer-appropriate processing: conditions that "force the learner to engage in processes that are also critical for test performance" produce learning, even when they hurt practice (p. 215).

## Relation to the argument

**The abstract's "feedback immediacy".** This review is the standing counter-case. It says the assumption that more immediate and more frequent feedback is better was built into "the design of simulators" and is false for retention (p. 212). The author's phrase, taken as a design rule, would repeat the assumption the review sets out to qualify. A narrower reading survives: frequent feedback helps while the person is acting (acquisition), and the triad is a setting where the person is always acting; whether there is a "retention test" in a market is a question the paper cannot answer.

**D7 and the "gauge and adjust in the moment" picture.** Supports it for performance during action; cuts against it as an account of how people come to understand a mediator over time, because the mechanisms named on p. 213 (feedback becoming part of the task, blocking one's own error detection) are exactly what a person leaning on a platform's instant signal would suffer.

**P9, "rapid and unequivocal" feedback.** Kahneman and Klein's condition is about validity; this review is about schedule. The two are compatible only if the paper says *when* immediacy helps and when it hurts, which is the facet's Q3.

## In conversation with

- `kluger1996effects` — the organisational meta-analysis four years later, finding over a third of feedback interventions lowered performance; this review supplies the learning-theory account.

## Caution

A narrative review from 1992 by advocates of the guidance hypothesis; the feedback findings rest on a handful of motor experiments with small samples (not reported here). McKay et al. (2022) find the literature "severely underpowered" and the reduced-frequency effect undetectable on average, so the review's feedback section should be cited as a hypothesis with mixed later support, not as settled. Nothing here concerns markets, counterparts or adaptive systems.
