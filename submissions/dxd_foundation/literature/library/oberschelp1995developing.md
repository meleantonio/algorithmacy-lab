## What it argues
Oberschelp argues that German schools should make informatics a compulsory subject in every course leading to university entry, and that it should teach competences nothing else teaches. He is sceptical of the general computer-literacy course (ITG), which he says was never agreed or implemented, and holds that mere handling skills "must not be the object of school instruction at all" (Introduction, p. 116). He lists ten bulleted competences (eleven named, since one bullet names both "programming" and "combinational"), among them "observation," "serving," "programming," "algorithmic," "evaluation," "test" and "parallel design" (General principles, pp. 117–118). Teaching tools, he says, must show intermediate steps, allow "zooming in time and space," keep "algorithmic transparency" and offer flexible interfaces; advanced commercial software lacks "transparency and motivation" (Software tools, p. 118; Abstract, p. 115). He warns that perfect tools can cause "mental atrophy and ... algorithmic disabling," comparing this to muscles unused (Algorithmic skill, p. 119), and recommends "mental sweat," occasionally stopping the computer "and running things on our own brain" (p. 121). Later sections ask for visible raster pixels, step-by-step execution speed controls, simulation and parallel computation (pp. 120–124). He ends by asking for a shared national or international choice of teaching material (p. 124).

The word at issue sits in one sentence on p. 121: "In the same way as we have to preserve literacy by not only relying on pictures or to maintain numeracy by sometimes avoiding the calculator, we have to preserve logicacy and algorithmacy by continuously training simple mental activities without artificial tools." It occurs once in the chapter (a text search finds one instance). He does not define either term, and uses them in the sentence's list of skills to be kept alive by tool-free practice.

## Relation to the argument
The facet file recorded this item as an unread database hit that "may contain the word (a prior coinage) or may be a fuzzy-match artefact," and asked that someone read it. It does contain the word. That is a prior printed use of "algorithmacy," in 1995, in English, in a chapter that sits on the shelf beside literacy and numeracy. The facet's note that the author's Substack says the word appeared before in an unpublished conference talk (citing Leese and Matzner, 2016) is a separate matter and this chapter does not touch it.

The sense differs from the lab's current definition (`../algorithmacy_ot/manuscript/PAPER.md:19-21`, per SOURCE.md). Oberschelp's term names a mental skill of carrying out algorithmic steps by hand, to be protected against tools that do the work. It is neither a capacity to navigate an algorithmic third party, nor a coordination competence. It is closer to the "skill decay" risk in the argument map's exposure list (item 4, Risko and Gilbert) and to Bainbridge's ironies than to the paper's engagement layer. This should be reported to the author, who rules on how the paper treats the coinage. I have not changed any text in the repo. Whether to cite it, and whether "algorithmacy" is the author's coinage or a revival, is the author's call.

Two other points touch the paper. Oberschelp's rule that tools should expose "intermediate steps" and be "transparent" is a disclosure position in the form the paper calls literacy-based (rulings D3, D4), and it is argued for pupils inside a classroom with a teacher coordinating, not for a platform's users. And his claim that interactive screens give "direct evidence that their actions cause an effect" (p. 118) agrees with the author's D7 on learning by acting and seeing the result; he does not extend it to a mediator with its own objective.
