# Singer, J., Tefft, B. C., Benson, A., Jenness, J. W., & Horrey, W. J. (2022). Driver expectations of a partial driving automation system in relation to branding and training. *Human Factors, 66*(5), 1531–1544.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1177/00187208221143024 (PMC10943610) · **Read depth:** full_text (the open-access author-version XML from Europe PMC, CC BY-NC; it carries no page breaks, so locators are section names, and I did not see the typeset pages or the supplemental appendices) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** between-subjects experiment, n = 90 licensed drivers (45 per branding condition, 30 per training mode) in the Washington DC area, followed by a 31-mile on-road drive; August to October 2019 · **Parties modeled:** person–system (driver and an SAE Level 2 driving-assistance feature; the manufacturer's description is the manipulated variable) · **Facets:** H · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Singer and colleagues ask whether how a driver-assistance feature is named and described changes what drivers expect of it, even when every statement is accurate. Drivers got materials for a real feature (Super Cruise, with brand marks hidden) under one of two names: AutonoDrive, with training that emphasised capabilities, or DriveAssist, with training that emphasised limitations and the driver's duties. Training came by brochure, video or in-person demonstration. The authors report that "neither version omitted any important safety information nor presented any false information" (Method, Training Materials). Example of the contrast: "Now AutonoDrive is in control and you can relax" against "Now DriveAssist is helping you to steer and maintain speed. You must continue to monitor the system and the road ahead."

Measures: 37 five-point items, reduced by principal-component analysis to eight composite expectation factors, measured after training and again after the drive (Variables).

Results. Immediately after training, the AutonoDrive group expected more on five factors: Specific Capabilities (p = 0.03), Environmental Changes (p = 0.01), Crash Avoidance Impossible (p = 0.003), Crash Avoidance Possible (p < 0.001) and Workload Reduction (p = 0.01) (Results, Initial Effects). After the drive, Specific Capabilities (p = 0.04), Crash Avoidance Impossible (p = 0.03) and Workload Reduction (p = 0.01) stayed higher, General Function became marginally higher (p = 0.048), and the mean was higher in the AutonoDrive group on every factor (Post-Drive Effects). Training mode mattered little: the video group expected less crash avoidance before the drive (p = 0.02), and no factor differed by mode after the drive. Expectations of both groups rose after driving; the authors call this "unexpected, particularly for participants who received training emphasizing limitations," and note that the narrowing of group differences came mainly from the limitations group's rising expectations (Discussion). The AutonoDrive group also expected more of the system's real abilities, so the capability framing helped on those items too (Discussion). The authors conclude: "Training that emphasizes a partial driving automation system's capabilities and downplays its limitations can foster overconfidence" (Abstract).

## Relation to the argument
Facet H uses this as a move-type (ii) case: framing the same accurate information changes expectations, and "accuracy of technical information does not guarantee understanding." The full text supports that and adds a limit the facet does not state. The measures are stated expectations and willingness, not driving behaviour, and the authors say the questionnaire "is not represented as a validated measure of mental models per se." The effect of the exposure to the real system pulled both groups upward, so the direct experience that the argument might expect to correct framing did not (Discussion).

For ruling D3, the study is evidence for the author's side in a specific form: what the supplier says about the system, even truthfully, does not settle the user's working model, and a short drive did not fix it. It tests a described, trained user, not a user given a tool for navigating the system, so it does not test algorithmacy-side interventions. The manufacturer controls the description, which fits P10 and the owner-held convention point. Note also that the contrast confounds name with description (Discussion), so the paper cannot say the brand name alone does the work.

## In conversation with
The paper draws on the training literature for driver-assistance systems (Abraham et al. 2017a, 2017b, 2018; McDonald et al. 2017; Manser et al. 2019), on anchoring and primacy effects (Tversky and Kahneman 1974; Jones et al. 1968) and on Beggiato and Krems 2013 and Beggiato et al. 2015 for learning from exposure. The nearest bib entries are on automation and complacency: `parasuraman2010complacency`, `parasuraman2000types`, `dekker2002maba`; the paper does not cite them. ## Caution
n = 90, one L2 feature, a single 31-mile drive, 37 items with no multiple-comparison correction (the authors say so). The published pagination is not preserved in the text I read. Self-reported expectations, not on-road behaviour.
