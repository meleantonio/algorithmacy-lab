## What it argues
Four authors argue that software should be "tools that users can reshape with minimal friction to suit their unique needs," because "mass-produced software is too rigid." Their evidence is stories: a team that lost a process step when it moved from index cards on a wall to a web tracker ("Computerizing work led to a loss of agency"), and Atul Gawande's account of doctors forced into fields they once skipped on paper forms ("Spending the extra time didn't anger her. The pointlessness of it did"). They test four existing routes (settings, plugins, modding, open source) and find each limited to what the developer exposes or to expert users. AI code generation helps but "AI code generation alone does not address all the barriers to malleability"; they offer the image of "a talented sous chef to a food court."

Three design patterns carry the essay. (1) "A gentle slope from user to creator": after MacLean et al. (1990), each step up in customisation should need only a small step up in skill, with spreadsheets and HyperCard's five "levels" as models. (2) "Tools, not apps": apps are "avocado slicers"; the aim is knife-like tools sharing data (the file system as precedent) and a shared workspace. (3) "Communal creation": "we use computers together," so local groups and enthusiasts who guide colleagues up the slope matter (they cite Nardi and Miller on "local developers" in spreadsheet use). The prototype section reports lab lessons, such as a section word-counter tool built with AI help "in just a few minutes." The closing section lists open problems the authors "mostly left out of scope": privacy and security ("untrusted strangers are sharing modifications"), business models, and culture.

## Relation to the argument
It shares the paper's premise that conventions of a medium decide who can act in it, and the demand that the person's burden fall as the medium changes. It cuts two ways. The "gentle slope" is a theory of how skill and power should grow together, which bears on ruling D10 (the history is a guide to moving from one literacy to another) but gives the paper no history to borrow. Its stance on control runs against the paper's: Litt et al. want each user to hold the rule-setting power, and say "consider tipping the balance towards seeing end-users as capable participants rather than passive users that need to be protected at all costs." That presumes the maker of the tool and its user share interests, or that the user can leave; the paper's premise P10 is that a mediator's owner may not share the user's interest, and the essay's own security caveat concerns outsiders, not the platform owner.

Facet check: the facet (summary only) says "one person and their tools" and "no history analogy." The full text confirms the missing history but contradicts "one person": a whole section ("Communal creation") argues that malleability is a group matter, with shared tools for teams, hospital departments and Mastodon instances.

## Caution
A manifesto with prototypes, not a study. Its examples come from the authors' lab and a few cited anecdotes. It leaves security and the platform business model unsolved by its own account.
