# Fisher, M., Goddu, M. K., & Keil, F. C. (2015). Searching for explanations: How the Internet inflates estimates of internal knowledge. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 144*(3), 674–687.

## What it argues

"Searching the Internet for explanatory knowledge creates an illusion whereby people mistake access to information for their own personal understanding of the information" (abstract, p. 674). After looking up answers to unrelated questions online, participants rated their ability to explain new domains higher than controls: Exp. 1a, M = 3.61 against 3.07 on a 1–7 scale, t(195) = 3.24, d = 0.50 (p. 676); Exp. 1b added a pre-test and found equal baselines (3.21 against 3.21) and a post-test gap (3.63 against 3.15, d = 0.35) (p. 677); Exp. 1c matched time and content by sending the Internet group to a named page and giving controls the same text, 3.78 against 3.07, t(193) = 4.30, d = 0.63 (p. 678). The effect held when the measure was a choice among fMRI images of brain activity (4.66 against 4.12, d = 0.43; p. 678) and when the question said "without any outside sources" (3.41 against 2.94, d = 0.36; p. 679). It vanished for autobiographical questions the Internet cannot answer (4.24 against 4.04, F(1, 276) = 1.37; p. 680), so it is not general overconfidence. Experiments 4a–c locate it in the act of searching: following a link instead of searching gave lower ratings (3.20 against 3.63, d = 0.37; p. 681); searching that returned no useful answer, or no results at all, still raised ratings above the pooled no-Internet baseline of 3.05 (pp. 681–682). The authors conclude that "what participants failed to accurately monitor was the proportion of internal and external memory comprising the sum total of accessible knowledge" (p. 682), and that "The boundary between personal and interpersonal knowledge is becoming increasingly blurred" (p. 684).

## Relation to the argument

**D3 (disclosure as a literacy intervention).** The study is the sharpest evidence in this facet that reading what a system returns can raise a person's sense of understanding without touching their ability. It measures self-assessment only, never performance, so it supports a narrow claim: an explanation retrieved from a system inflates felt understanding. That is consistent with the author's ruling that disclosure changes awareness, not navigation, and it adds a cost: miscalibration.

**Costs of offloading.** A second cost beside `sparrow2011google`: not forgetting, but believing one knows. `gilbert2020optimal` cites this paper (ms. p. 11) as grounds for predicting an opposite bias, towards internal strategies, after access to external resources, a prediction their data did not confirm.

**Limits.** Person and search engine; no counterpart, no decision, no feedback on a choice. The "illusion of knowledge" is about explanatory knowledge of the world, not about a mediator's rule.

## Caution

All dependent measures are self-ratings; no experiment tests actual knowledge. Participants were MTurk workers, "presumably heavier Internet users than average" (p. 677). Effects are moderate (d 0.35–0.63) and the baseline comparisons in Experiments 4b–c pool across earlier studies. Nothing in the paper concerns strategic settings.
