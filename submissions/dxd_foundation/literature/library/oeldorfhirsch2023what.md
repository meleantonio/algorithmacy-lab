## What it argues
Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum review work on how well users understand the algorithms that filter their social-media feeds. They state the problem as "twofold: (1) What is algorithmic literacy? and (2) How do we assess this?" (p. 683). They compare two definitions: DeVito's (2021), awareness of "both the presence and impact" of algorithmic systems and the capacity to turn that into "a strategic use" for the user's goals; and Dogruel et al.'s (2021), four steps from awareness and knowledge to critical evaluation and "the skills to cope with or even influence algorithmic operations" (pp. 683–684). They add Swart's cognitive, affective and behavioural split (understanding, sensing, engaging) and Lomborg and Kapsch's knowing, feeling and doing (p. 684), and ask for "further converging on algorithmic literacy" as an umbrella term (p. 685).

The empirical picture they draw: early Facebook studies found that 62.5% of users did not know their news feed was filtered (Eslami et al. 2015, p. 686). Later work finds near-universal awareness but little understanding of criteria; users build "folk theories" from their own experience and these are "malleable" (p. 687). Literacy tracks age, education and search skills, which they read as a new digital divide (pp. 687–688, 696). Measurement is hard because "the opacity of algorithms makes arriving at a 'ground truth' ... impossible" (p. 690, citing Hargittai et al. 2020). The Dogruel scale is "the most comprehensive attempt" but splits into awareness and knowledge sub-scales (p. 691).

Their agenda has four items: balance user literacy against platform transparency; engage users through curiosity, motivation, control and practice; develop the affective and behavioural facets; address the algorithmic divide (pp. 692–696). On transparency they say literacy "should not be solely the responsibility of the social media user" and that individuals "must endure most of effort in improving literacy" (p. 693). They also report that knowledge "is not strongly correlated with positive attitudes" and that "increasing this knowledge does little to change these attitudes" (p. 695; the first claim cites Araujo et al. 2020, Dietvorst et al. 2015 and Yeomans et al. 2019, the second Silva et al. 2022).

## Relation to the argument
This is the standard literature on the "algorithmic literacy" frame that the paper contrasts with algorithmacy (D3, D4). It supports the paper in three ways and complicates it in one.

It shows that the field defines literacy as an individual's awareness, knowledge, critique and use of an algorithm, which is the individual-user reading the paper opposes. Its interventions, such as Rader et al.'s short explanatory statements about what appears in a feed, are disclosure interventions (p. 692), consistent with the author's ruling that disclosure is literacy-based. And the finding that more knowledge changes attitudes little (p. 695) bears on facet H and on P9: it is evidence that "giving the person something to read" has weak effects on what they feel; it says nothing here about what they do.

It complicates the paper in one place. The review's own agenda moves beyond disclosure: it asks for "behavioral" literacy, for platform responsibility, for user control and "practice with algorithms" (pp. 692–695). Those items sit closer to the author's engagement-layer ruling (D7) than the "literacy = reading about the algorithm" caricature allows. The paper should cite the literature at its strongest, not only as a disclosure position. The review also never treats the mediator's own objective or a counterpart party; every case has one user and one platform.

The review is silent on writing history, word separation and any design precedent from literacy itself, so it does not support the analogy; it only names the field's term.

Facet check: the facet recorded this as a Crossref record "not read" and grouped it among literacy-critique sources. The facet's claim that none of these says "literacy is the wrong frame" for the triad holds for this review: it extends the frame and does not question it.

## Caution
A narrative review whose inclusion process is described but not systematic; the focus is "social media content filtering algorithms" (p. 683), so it does not cover search, scoring or coordination platforms. Its tallies of findings come from the reviewed studies and were not re-checked here. The article states no search dates; its focus on feed-filtering algorithms predates generative-AI interfaces. Year: 2023 online, 2025 in the volume.
