# Lyons, H., Velloso, E., & Miller, T. (2021). Conceptualising contestability: Perspectives on contesting algorithmic decisions. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, 5*(CSCW1), Article 106.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/3449180; arXiv:2103.01774 · **Read depth:** full_text (arXiv v1, 23 Feb 2021, 26 pp. plus the reference list; pagination is the arXiv manuscript's, not the journal's; appendices listing submitters and consultation questions not read) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article (preprint copy) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** qualitative; reflexive thematic analysis (NVivo 12) of 65 submissions to Australia's 2019 AI Ethics Framework consultation, out of 116 published (more than 130 were made); one coder · **Parties modeled:** other (decision subject, decision maker, and an internal or external reviewer) · **Facets:** H · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
The authors ask how people and organisations who answered Australia's consultation conceptualise "contestability" (Principle 7: "an efficient process to allow that person to challenge the use or output of the algorithm", p. 5) and how they imagine it working. The first author read all 116 published submissions, kept the 65 that used contest*, appeal, recourse, redress, remed*, compensation or "Principle 7", and coded them alone (pp. 5–6). Submitters came from academia (9), universities (8), industry bodies (10), small and medium firms (10), large firms (9), research institutes (6), individuals (6), community groups (4) and government (3) (Fig. 1, p. 6). The authors found no sector pattern and analysed the set as a whole.

Three themes (§4, p. 7):
1. The nature of contestability is disputed: an end in itself, or a means to accountability, fairness or autonomy.
2. It protects individuals, against a power gap that opacity widens. Submitters asked for enforceability and redress, and for contestation to sit inside broader regulation, since it puts the burden on the person who must start it.
3. It should resemble contestability of human decisions: internal review, then external review (tribunal, ombudsman, court), and human reviewers.

For operation, the submissions imply preliminaries to settle (what can be contested, who may contest, who is accountable, which review), process steps (notice, explanation, a pathway to contest, a review) and design requirements (accessible, lawful, context-aware; Fig. 2, p. 11). Only "a couple of submissions" consider contesting inside the system itself (p. 13); the authors observe that "a majority of the submissions viewed the ability to contest as a post-hoc mechanism such as an appeal process, rather than a mechanism that can be designed into an AI system" (§6, p. 15).

The discussion adds the authors' own views. "Contestability can act as a protection, but it should be considered to be the last line of defence" (§6.2, p. 16). They use Australia's "robo-debt" scheme to show individual appeals succeeding while the system stayed in force. They warn that appeal rights can become "an excuse by governments and organisations to avoid designing systems with due care" (p. 16). And explanation can in principle be manipulated "to decrease the likelihood that a person will contest" (§6.5, p. 18).

## Relation to the argument
**P10 and D8.** Lyons et al. quote Kaminski: "whether a company whose interests do not always align with its users' will be capable of providing adequate process and fair results" (p. 2). That is the P10 problem, stated for the decision subject. The submissions' answers (an external, independent reviewer; a law that makes the right enforceable; the Law Council's "no proper form of contestability without enforceability", p. 9) are candidate answers to D8's question of where a convention can be held. They resemble the court comparison the paper plans in facet J. They do not settle it. The paper's frame has two roles, a decision maker and a decision subject; it does not model a mediator that serves two parties.

**Facet H family 7 (structural refusal, exit, contestability).** The paper is a map of what people say contestability should be, with no measured effect. It supports the claim that contestability needs explanation, and it records the authors' caution that "counterfactual explanations are unlikely to provide enough information to be able to contest a decision in court" (p. 18). That cuts against treating disclosure as a sufficient route (D3).

**Cuts against the design-precedent story.** Submitters favour existing human-decision processes. The authors suggest this may reflect "mere exposure" or anchoring rather than fit (§6.3, p. 17). New conventions for algorithmic triads are, on this evidence, not what the consulted parties asked for.

## Caution
A thematic analysis of one consultation, by one coder, with a sample the authors call "not representative of the general public", dominated by academic and private-sector voices (p. 7). The 65 submissions are those that used the search terms; the analysis reports prevalence only as "many", "a number" or "a couple" of submissions, and quotations are chosen examples. Submitters wrote about "AI" in a policy discussion paper, so the results speak about stated expectations, not use. I read the arXiv v1 manuscript; the journal's pagination and any edits are not checked.
