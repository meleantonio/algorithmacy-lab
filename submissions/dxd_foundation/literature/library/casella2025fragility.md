## What it argues
Casella, Friedman and Perez Archila take to the lab the optimal mediation mechanism of Hörner, Morelli and Squintani (2015). Two players split a resource of 100; each is privately strong (H) or weak (L); if they fail to agree, the resource shrinks to 70 and the stronger takes it. The mediator, a computer, receives confidential messages, then recommends a split or walks out. It has "neither independent resources, nor superior information, nor enforcement power" (p. 2), and its "objective is to maximize the probability of agreement" (p. 7). Its program "was projected on the lab screen during instructions and remained on the screen throughout all rounds of the treatment" (p. 15). Subjects were told messages "did not need to be truthful" (p. 15, n. 18).

Theory predicts peace of 0.875 under mediation against 0.586 under direct talk when q = 1/2, and 0.78 against 0.444 when q = 1/3 (Table 1, p. 14; for q = 1/3 the table also lists a second direct-talk equilibrium at 0.345, so 0.444 is the better of two). In the lab, weak types were more sincere to the computer than to each other (l messages from L types rose from 31% to 57% at q = 1/2 and from 30% to 64% at q = 1/3, p. 20), but peace did not rise: {UC, CM} = {0.57, 0.55} at q = 1/2 and {0.49, 0.46} at q = 1/3, with no significant difference (p. 22). CM "brings no improvement when compared not only to UC but also to no communication at all" (p. 24).

The explanation is the paper's theoretical result. Optimal mediation beats direct talk only through obfuscation: leaving each side unsure of the other's type. Under obfuscation, any departure from full sincerity makes the peace probability fall discontinuously: "The best equilibrium under obfuscation is fragile" (p. 27; Proposition 2). Without obfuscation the mechanism is robust but no better than direct communication (pp. 3–4, 34). Sincere H types rejected more than a third of the 50–50 splits recommended to them (p. 26). Individual deviations cost subjects little (with q = 1/2, 93% of H subjects and 64% of L subjects lost less than 5% of their payoff, p. 34) but cost others peace (p. 41). A mechanism with strict incentive constraints did no better (p. 40). The authors also suggest that strong types may lie from "the desire to maintain control over triggering conflict, as opposed to having it imposed by the computer mediator" (p. 31).

## Relation to the argument
**Premise P10 (first candidate) and the "rulebook" idea.** This is the closest controlled test the project has of a triad in which a third party's rule is public and fixed. Its result cuts against an easy reading of P10. A displayed, optimal rule that removes a strategic task (the bargain) did raise sincerity, and still did not raise agreement, because the rule worked only if both sides trusted each other's sincerity. P10's matching-market evidence (`li2017obviously`) shows a rule helping when it removes the need to reason about others; here the optimal rule required that reasoning, and failed.

**Ruling D7 and premise P9.** Subjects got outcome feedback every round for 20 rounds, and behaviour was "far from erratic" (p. 41; random play rejected, p. 34) yet did not reach the optimal equilibrium. Feedback alone did not teach them the mechanism's best use.

**Ruling D8 and premise P12.** The mediator here has no interest of its own. That is the feature the argument map flags for matching clearinghouses (P10, weakest point). A platform mediator has a margin; this paper says nothing about one.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 452 calls the paper "The closest precedent... where two participants bargain through a computer mediator that maximizes the chance of agreement and shows its rule every round." "Shows its rule every round" is accurate if read as "keeps the rule displayed"; the paper does not say the rule was re-presented each round. The line continues "I give M a margin of its own and compare joint surplus between rulebook arms and menu arms." That is the draft's design, not the paper's; two points from the paper bear on it. (1) In Casella et al. efficiency is the probability of agreement (p. 6), so "joint surplus" is the right outcome. The draft does not report the paper's result; a reader may infer the precedent succeeded.
