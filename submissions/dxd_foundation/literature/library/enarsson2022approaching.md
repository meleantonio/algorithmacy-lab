## What it argues
"Through legal and practical incentives, humans will often need to be kept in the loop of such decision-making to maintain human agency and accountability, provide legal safeguards, or perform quality control" (abstract). Hybrid decision-making is defined as processes with both human and machine actors, including those where humans take "a different role, as the overseer and correcting mechanism for the algorithmic predictions" (§1.2, quoting Morison and Harkens) and those where humans are "a primarily rubber-stamping mechanism" (§1.2, Wagner's "quasi-automation"). Public-administration research is read through Bovens and Zouridis's shift from street-level to "system-level" bureaucracies, where "judgment is increasingly delegated to computer systems" (§2.1). The GDPR's travaux are quoted on the risk the authors say is still the issue: the machine's result "has an apparently objective and incontrovertible character to which a human decision-maker may attach too much weight, thus abdicating his own responsibilities" (§3.1, quoting the Commission's 1992 memorandum); the EDPB requires oversight to be "meaningful, rather than just a token gesture" (§3.1).

The Swedish case (§4.3) is the paper's one measured example. The Social Insurance Agency stopped full automation of simple sickness-benefit cases in 2016 on legal advice, then in September 2017 introduced a partially automated, rule-based system for diagnoses statistically likely to close within 60 days in at least 70 percent of cases, widened in October 2018 to 80 days in at least 50 percent of cases and to mental diagnoses. If any coded condition fails, "the automatic processing is interrupted and the case is transferred to manual handling", with "an automatically generated documentation on why it was interrupted"; assessments of work ability and the final decision remain manual. The agency's internal audit found "insufficient documentation about what information the system had collected and on how it had decided whether legal conditions had been met"; case investigations were "insufficient in as much as 67 percent of the cases"; of these a correct decision had still been made in 76 percent, and "only about one-fourth of the cases were both sufficiently investigated and correctly assessed". The authors' reading: administrators "meet the specific cases as a 'set table', where large parts of the investigation may have already been executed", and "a lack of documentation about what information the system has collected ... can result in an information deficit for the administrator when taking over a case" (§4.3). A pilot, "Skosa", would add machine-learning support on the very assessment that must be individual.

In moderation (§4.4), the EU Code of Conduct evaluation found 70–80 percent of reported material removed; Twitter's pandemic statement is quoted that automated systems "can sometimes lack the context that our teams bring". The evaluation (§5.1) quotes Yeung: human agency "cannot be systematically relied upon" to mitigate the concerns of algorithmic systems, and names the role conflict: overseers are "expected to ... exercise actual autonomy" yet do so "in relation to decision-making systems designed for making decisions at scale, faced with restrictions concerning both time and resources". The closing question is "if they are actually authorized, equipped, and given the opportunity to fulfill this role" (§5.2).

## Relation to the argument
This is a real read-to-check system with a measured failure, which the abstract's second sentence needs. The Swedish administrator receives a machine-generated document explaining the interruption and a "set table" of pre-collected facts, and the audit found a quarter of cases both properly investigated and correct. That supports the abstract: the person is sent something to read and checks badly. It also supports the argument-map's P12 inside a public agency: what the administrator sees is what the system's designers chose to log.

On parties the paper is the nearest thing in this facet to a triad. The structures it studies have three positions, decision-maker, system and subject, and in moderation a platform between a speaker and an audience. But the subject has no interface and does not coordinate with anyone through the system; the system serves the agency. So it supports the narrower form of the dyad claim: the oversight relation is one reader and one system even where a third person's rights are the point. For P8, the paper's remedy is socio-technical study of the humans in the loop, not disclosure to the subject.
