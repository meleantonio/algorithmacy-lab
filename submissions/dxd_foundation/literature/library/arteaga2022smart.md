## What it argues

Arteaga and colleagues start from a puzzle in school choice. Economists helped design strategy-proof assignment (deferred acceptance), so applicants need not guess their admission chances. Yet families still search for schools at a cost, and over-optimism about placement leads them to stop searching too early. The paper offers a model of that search (Propositions 1–2, pp. 8–13), survey evidence, and a field test of "smart matching platforms" that warn applicants whose list is at risk.

Survey findings (Chile 2020): only 17% say they know a random nearby school well, against 64% for their first choice (pp. 3, 23). Asked why they stopped adding schools, 35% say they expect a place from the list already, and 51% of the top belief quintile give that reason, against 9% of the bottom (pp. 23–24). Applicants overrate their chances: mean subjective placement 76% against a true 44%, 32 points too high (pp. 24–25).

Field results: crossing the 30% predicted non-placement risk cutoff made 21.6% of applicants (SE 1.0) add schools (Table II; p. 5). Compliers cut non-placement risk by 15.5 points, 58% of the below-threshold mean, and enrolled in schools with test-score value added 0.10 standard deviations higher (pp. 5, 27, 29). Enrollment conditional on placement did not fall (p. 28). Generic nudges with no risk information raised "add a school" by 1.5 points, statistically insignificant (p. 35). A warning about aggregate risk had about half the effect of the personal warning (p. 36). Forcing a second school ("costly shoving") left 17% (10 points) fewer placed students enrolled in it (p. 37). In New Haven, 13.8% of applicants near the cutoff lengthened their lists and compliers cut risk by 42% (pp. 6, 38).

The abstract states the lesson: "Reducing the burden of school choice requires not just strategyproofness inside the centralized system, but also choice supports for the strategic decisions that inevitably remain outside of it" (Abstract). The authors say the policies "are products already at scale" (p. 39).

## Relation to the argument

**Premise P8.** The map's own note is that "the removed task relocates (Arteaga et al.)", and the text bears that out. The strategy-proof rule removes one task (how to rank) but leaves another (what to search for and when to stop), and live feedback on the risk of the application aids the second. This is the clearest field evidence in the batch that a rule removing a strategic task does not remove every task.

**Premise P9, and the case against reading it as a blanket.** P9 says interventions that hand a person something to read or tune show weaker and less consistent effects. Here a personalised read-out is large for a light-touch policy (21.6 points; the authors set it against a mean of 1.4 points across 241 other trials, p. 27). It beats three generic nudges and beats an aggregate warning. So this source qualifies P9: what is read matters (a personal consequence, at the point of decision), and the aid works best when it reports the outcome of the person's own choice. It is not a disclosure of how the clearinghouse works.

**Premise P10.** The warning comes from the education ministry, built on data the platform already holds, and the paper reports no stake of the ministry in applicants searching less. The platform has no objective of its own in the placement. That differs from the commercial platforms in the project's argument, where the owner has an objective the users do not share. A co-author (Neilson) founded the NGO (ConsiliumBots) that ran the interventions (p. 17; the paper says NGO, not non-profit); the cover notes a disclosure.

**Ruling D3.** The intervention does not disclose the algorithm. It gives a person a prediction about their own application, so it is a different thing from "disclosure of the algorithm".

**Facet check:** the facet's quotations and numbers match. One caveat the facet gets right and I confirm: the 58% applies to compliers (the 21.6% who add schools), not to everyone who received the pop-up; for all warned applicants the risk fall is 3.3 points, 13% of the below-threshold mean (pp. 26–27).

## Caution

Two systems (Chile, New Haven) with a ministry-run platform; effects are local to applicants near the 30% (Chile) and 50% (New Haven) cutoffs, and the New Haven sample is small (740). The survey response rate is 13% and respondents are slightly less likely to be vulnerable or rural. Compliers are a selected group and the welfare reading rests on enrollment and school value added, not on a direct measure of satisfaction after placement. A working paper, and one co-author runs the implementing NGO.
