## What it argues

The guidance hypothesis (Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter 1984) predicts that feedback after every trial "enhances practice performance but degrades subsequent motor learning" relative to reduced frequency, a "reversal effect" (ms p. 2). The meta-analysis tests it. Effects (Hedges' g, positive favouring reduced frequency): acquisition 0.19 (95% CI −0.11 to 0.49; prediction interval −1.03 to 1.42; ms p. 26); immediate retention 0.01 (CI −0.30 to 0.32; ms p. 27); delayed retention 0.19 (CI −0.05 to 0.43; prediction interval −1.02 to 1.40; ms p. 29). The acquisition-to-delayed-retention change was −0.0008 (CI −0.32 to 0.32; ms p. 29). No preregistered moderator accounted for the heterogeneity (ms pp. 29–30). Bias-corrected estimates were smaller: selection model g = 0.06 at delayed retention; RoBMA g = 0.021 with moderate evidence for the null (BF01 = 4.22) and "very strong evidence of heterogeneity" (ms pp. 31–32). Z-curve put expected replication at 39% and expected discovery at 27% (ms p. 32). The median group size was 13; detecting g = 0.19 at 80% power would need 436 per group (ms p. 36).

"Our results are thus uniformly inconclusive" (ms p. 32): the data neither show the effect nor rule out effects of 0.10. The authors' stance is that "motor learning scientists should abstain from making substantive recommendations regarding feedback frequency to practitioners" (ms p. 35). One contacted author reported a published experiment that was "their ninth attempt to find a reduced frequency effect" (ms p. 33).

## Relation to the argument

**Q3 and the Schmidt–Bjork counter-case.** The facet's main argument against unconditional immediacy (Schmidt & Bjork 1992) rests on the guidance hypothesis. This meta-analysis says the hypothesis is neither supported nor refuted after four decades (ms p. 36). The draft therefore cannot cite "frequent feedback degrades learning" as established, and equally cannot cite "immediate feedback helps learning" from this literature. Both directions are open.

**For the abstract's sentence.** The result is a reason to write "feedback immediacy" as a design proposal to be tested, not as a principle the human-factors literature has settled. It also shows what a test would need: samples two orders of magnitude larger than the tradition's, or a multi-site design (ms pp. 36–37), which is the same lesson as ManyClasses.

**Scope.** Terminal augmented feedback on motor tasks with a no-feedback retention test; the environment is fixed and the feedback is accurate. It says nothing about noisy or adversarial feedback, which is where the triad evidence (Bereby-Meyer & Roth; Burton-Chellew & West) lives.

## Caution

Read in preprint; the published article may differ. Two databases and English only; ten potentially relevant articles could not be accessed (ms p. 34). Only studies with a 100% comparison group and fixed reduced schedules were included; summary and bandwidth feedback were excluded or analysed separately (ms p. 7). Heterogeneity is unexplained, so a real effect in some conditions is not ruled out. Motor tasks in healthy participants; no decision or market task.
