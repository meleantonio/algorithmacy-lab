## What it argues
Algorithmic management is "opaque and continuously evolving" and the authors ask "how and through which practices gig workers deal with algorithmic management and its opacity" (Algorithmic management in the digital economy). Workers develop "anticipatory compliance practices ... according to the assumed (but not yet proven) material design of the algorithm" (Empirical findings). Direct practices aimed at the algorithm: "staying under the radar" (avoiding "gray area words" in chat; not reporting bugs for fear of review) and "purposely curtailing client outreach" (a worker's heuristic: "Upwork has a 20/1 rule. If you do not get selected to a job after 20 proposals, you may get suspended"). Indirect practices aimed at clients: undervaluing work (free fixes, lower rates) and keeping emotions in check (pleading with clients not to leave bad feedback). The practices sit in a "triadic relationship between algorithm, client and worker" (Pacifying the algorithm), and "satisfying the client – while necessary – is by no means sufficient to ensure participation". They amount to "extra work", "additional cognitive, social, and emotional effort" (Agency and extra work; the Introduction says "work"), and "Paradoxically, as workers employ anticipatory compliance to reclaim control over their own work process, they reaffirm the algorithm's power" (Introduction).

## Relation to the argument
**Q3 — the coordination cost, counted.** The paper's own term, "extra work", is the abstract's cost of coordinating through the mediator: workers forgo paid proposals and underprice themselves to keep a score that lets them reach clients at all. The authors locate it in a triad explicitly.

**P8 / D3.** Upwork publishes the inputs of the Job Success Score but not the calculation (see `rahman2019invisible`); the comments show workers acting on assumed rules. The sentence holds in the form: partial disclosure plus an opaque threshold produces gaming by guesswork.

**P9 — learning is communal and often wrong.** The "20/1 rule" is a folk heuristic of the kind Rahman calls superstitious reactivity; the paper also finds that "seasoned workers ... are less likely to be scared", so experience changes the response.
