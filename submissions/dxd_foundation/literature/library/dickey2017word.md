## What it argues

Dickey opens against the common view that word division is a modern advantage: "The ancients were fully aware of the possibility of word division and, on the whole, chose not to use it" (p. 159). Writing without word division is "fundamentally a Greek phenomenon": the Romans, like the Etruscans and Oscans, "originally divided their words and only gave up this practice under Greek influence around 100 AD" (p. 159, citing Wingo 1972, pp. 14–17, and Ammirati 2010). Roman division "relied not on spaces but on raised dots known as 'interpuncts', which were used between nearly every word", with clitics and prepositions joined to their hosts (pp. 159–160). Interpuncts appear in inscriptions, early Latin papyri and most Pompeian graffiti (p. 160).

The chapter's thesis is that, after AD 100, word division survived in one context: texts used by language learners. Bilingual school texts set Latin and Greek in narrow parallel columns, and "the line breaks thus provided about half the word divisions" (p. 162). Scribes then added interpuncts, hypodiastolai, double points and wide spaces to give "the maximum possible assistance: the learner was guided both in where each word began and in which words should be taken together" (p. 163). In one papyrus of the *Aeneid* (P.Ness. II 1), extracts from books I–II carry word dividers but the book IV extracts carry none: "clearly word division was one of the things the student was supposed to have mastered by that stage" (p. 167). Later readers, usually Greek speakers, added division by hand to Latin texts written in *scriptio continua* (P.Oxy. XXIV 2401; Vat. Reg. Lat. 886, pp. 172–173).

Her conclusion: "It was not a bilingual text per se but rather a text used by language learners that triggered the division of words" (p. 173); bilingual texts not used by learners (a court record, PSI XIII 1309) run in *scriptio continua* (pp. 173–174). Hence the "paradoxical situation that although Greek influence was responsible for the abandonment of Latin word division, it was particularly Greek speakers who tended to divide words in Latin" (p. 174). She uses this to redate PSI VII 743 to the second century, since interpunction in learner contexts outlived its "regular use in monolingual Latin texts" (p. 175).

## Relation to the argument

This source bears on premise P1 (writing in the Roman West ran without word boundaries) and on the causal story in P2 (Insular scribes separated words because they read Latin as a foreign language).

It **narrows P1 and the draft's dating**. Latin was word-divided, by interpuncts, until about AD 100. The span of Latin *scriptio continua* before Insular separation is therefore roughly six centuries, not "more than a millennium". Only Greek fits the longer span.

It **supports the learner mechanism in P2, with a twist the paper should state**. Dickey finds the same pairing in late antiquity: readers working in a foreign language got word division, and fluent readers did without it. That fits Parkes's and Saenger's account of the Irish. It also cuts against the draft's framing of separation as a new, "uncoordinated ergonomic change". The ancients knew the device and confined it to beginners. On Dickey's evidence the Insular change looks like a learner's aid becoming the norm for everyone, not an invention.

It bears on ruling D2 (spacing "ignited literacy") only by analogy. The ancient learner texts show that division helped people who were still learning a language, and that teachers expected students to outgrow it (the book IV evidence, p. 167). That is closer to a scaffold for learners than to a convention that "pays once readers have come to depend on it" (draft line 119).

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Dickey is not cited in DRAFT_v2. It bears on these lines:
- Line 79 ("For more than a millennium across Greco-Roman antiquity and the early Middle Ages, texts were transcribed in *scriptio continua*. Words were strung together in unbroken lines without spaces between them"). **Narrows/contradicts for Latin.** Latin divided words with interpuncts until about AD 100 (p. 159). The sentence holds for Greek. For the Latin West, the continuous span before Insular separation was about six centuries. "Without spaces" is literally true of interpunct Latin, which used dots, not spaces (pp. 159–160), so the draft should say "without word division" if it means the reader's task.
- Line 83 ("Scribes in Ireland and Anglo-Saxon Britain learned Latin as a foreign language … and Insular copyists began inserting gaps between words"). **Supports the mechanism by ancient parallel** (learner texts divided words, pp. 162–174). The chapter says nothing about Insular manuscripts.
- Line 87 (Saenger's "shortcuts" quotation). **Consistent.** Dickey shows that ancient learners got division as an aid that skilled readers were expected to do without (p. 167).
- Line 119 ("A convention pays once readers have come to depend on it"). **Complicates.** In the ancient material division served learners and was withdrawn as they progressed (p. 167), which is the opposite pattern to dependence.

## Caution

The chapter rests on a small, selected set of papyri, most from Egypt, where learners were Greek speakers learning Latin; Dickey notes that Latin-speaking provinces left no papyri (fn. 25). It covers antiquity to the 7th century and says nothing about Ireland or Insular scribes, so any link to the draft's medieval claims is an inference by analogy. "Around 100 AD" is a round date resting on Wingo and Ammirati. The CentAUR file is the published version but lacks the bibliography, so full references for the authors Dickey cites were not checked.
