# Luca, M., & Zervas, G. (2016). Fake it till you make it: Reputation, competition, and Yelp review fraud. *Management Science, 62*(12), 3412–3427. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2304

**Identifier:** doi:10.1287/mnsc.2015.2304 · **Read depth:** full_text (the accepted version dated 1 May 2015 as deposited in Boston University's OpenBU repository, 36 pp.; I read the text, pp. 1–27, and the reference list; the tables at the end (Tables 1–8) were not read beyond the figures quoted in the text; pagination is the deposited version's, not the journal's) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** panel of 316,415 Yelp reviews of 3,625 Boston restaurants, 2004–2012, of which 50,486 (about 16%) were filtered by Yelp's algorithm, used as a proxy for fraud; difference-in-differences at the restaurant-month level with restaurant and time fixed effects; a second data set of 126 businesses that had received a Yelp consumer alert, after Yelp's sting operations, in the three months to March 2014 (pp. 6–11) · **Parties modeled:** two parties + third party (restaurants and consumers, with Yelp's filter between them; competitors as a further party) · **Facets:** U · **Read:** 2026-10-15

## What it argues
Filtered reviews "tend to be more extreme" and come from reviewers with fewer prior reviews (p. 9); 1- and 5-star reviews are "roughly 3 percentage points more likely to be filtered than 3-star reviews" (p. 10). Businesses caught in the sting had 79% of reviews filtered against 19% for the average Boston restaurant (p. 11), and had 2.6 stars and 18 reviews on average against 3.5 stars and 86 (p. 19).

Incentives: an extra published 1-star review in the previous period is associated with "an extra 0.01 filtered 5-star reviews" in the current month, about 10% of the monthly mean of 0.1 (p. 16); more reviews reduce positive fraud; chains leave fewer fake reviews (p. 17); claimed pages leave more (p. 18). Competition: a unit increase in same-type independent competition is associated with 0.0016 extra filtered 1-star reviews a month, "roughly ... a sustained 3% increase" on a mean of 0.05 (p. 23); chain competitors reduce it. No advertiser interaction was significant, so "neither 1- nor 5-star reviews were significantly more or less likely to be filtered for businesses that were advertising" (p. 26). Conclusion: "Organizations are more likely to game the system when they are facing increased competition and when they have poor or less established reputations" (p. 25).

## Relation to the argument
**Q3 — gaming as a coordination cost.** The strongest quantitative evidence found this round that a seller games the mediator in order to reach buyers: fraud rises when the seller's standing with buyers falls and when rivals compete for the same buyers. The authors frame it as fraud and incentives, not coordination; the coordination reading is the author's.

**P8.** Yelp's rule is undisclosed ("Yelp purposely makes the filtering algorithm difficult to reverse engineer", p. 8) and gaming is large anyway (16% of reviews). So the gaming the abstract describes happens without disclosure; the sentence's "does not spare them" is about what disclosure adds, which this paper cannot test.

**The mediator's own objective.** The filter protects Yelp's credibility; the paper documents a third party that "binds and optimizes" and whose rule is a response to the parties' behaviour (retroactive refiltering, p. 8).
