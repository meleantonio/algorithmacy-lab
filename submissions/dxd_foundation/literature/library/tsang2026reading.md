## What it argues

The study replicates `pan2025printing` with older readers of the visually denser traditional script, using ordinary sentences whose word boundaries readers sometimes dispute, and asks whether vocabulary and boundary agreement change the effect of colour cues (Introduction).

Nonword colouring disrupted reading on every measure: reading speed fell from 402 to 351 characters per minute, total reading time on words rose from 370 to 422 ms, and first fixations moved toward the start of words (Table 1; Results). Word colouring "did not produce a general, overarching benefit" (Discussion); reading speed actually fell slightly, to 392 characters per minute (estimate −11.06, p < .001; Table 2), which the authors decline to interpret because it appeared in only one measure (Results).

Two interactions qualify this. First, "a benefit was observed only in readers with lower vocabulary knowledge" (Discussion): fixation durations under word colouring rose with vocabulary score, and only readers below the mean showed shorter fixations than baseline (Results; Table 2, interactions in first fixation, gaze and total time). Second, the benefit "increased with higher segmentation agreement", and where readers disagree about boundaries, "providing external cues interfered with reading, presumably because the cues may not always align with each reader's segmentation" (Results). The authors' explanation for skilled readers: "external visual cues may be redundant and even introduce irregularities that disrupt their highly practiced and efficient reading routines" (Discussion). They propose cues for "low-fluency readers" and suggest that once readers' lexical quality improves "they may read standard text fluently without such aids" (Discussion).

## Relation to the argument

This bears on P4 and on the draft's closing claim in line 119 about who gains from a convention. It **complicates P4's learner clause**. In this sample the readers who gained from boundary marks were the less fluent ones, which is the opposite of "learners do not reliably gain". The authors also find that a boundary cue costs the expert reader, which fits the familiarity half of the draft's claim. Read together, the study says a boundary convention is a scaffold for the less fluent and a disruption for the fluent reader who has internalised the unmarked convention.

It **supports the representational point** in P5 with a condition: the external mark helps only where it matches the reader's own segmentation. Where boundaries are contested, imposing one hurts. That is a direct analogue to a mediator that imposes a representation the user does not share.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Tsang et al. are not cited in DRAFT_v2. They bear on these lines:
- Line 117 (inserting boundaries into unspaced scripts gives a mixed effect). **Supports**: no general benefit, consistent harm from wrong boundaries (Table 1; Results).
- Line 119 ("Learners do not reliably gain: child learners of Chinese read spaced text more slowly and less accurately (Bassetti & Masterson, 2012), and for adult second-language learners the difference … was not significant (Cui, 2023)"). **Complicates.** Among native older readers, the less fluent gained and the more fluent did not (Discussion). The draft's learner evidence concerns children and L2 learners, so the two are not in direct contradiction, but the general lesson the draft draws needs the other half.
- Line 119 ("A convention pays once readers have come to depend on it"). **Narrows.** For fluent readers of the unmarked convention the added cue was redundant or disruptive (Discussion), which fits; the gain went to readers who had not fully mastered it, which the sentence does not cover.
- Line 119 ("An ergonomic intervention … must match the visual search affordances of the medium to the cognitive tasks of the user"). **Supports**, with the boundary-agreement finding (Results).

## Caution

No young comparison group. Older readers only, and only native readers of traditional Chinese in Hong Kong. Colour, not spacing, is the cue. Each participant saw both valid and invalid cues, which may weaken trust in the word cues; the authors discuss this (Method). The vocabulary and agreement interactions rest on fixation measures; reading speed showed no vocabulary interaction (p = .061, Table 2).
