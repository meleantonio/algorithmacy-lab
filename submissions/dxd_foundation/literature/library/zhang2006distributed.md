## What it argues

Zhang and Patel define distributed cognition narrowly, as "the scientific discipline that is concerned with the distribution of information and knowledge between and across internal and external representations" (p. 334), and decline the stronger claim that a distributed system thinks (p. 334). The components of a system are internal representations (knowledge in minds) and external representations (structure in the environment) (p. 334).

The paper's main use here is its list of the properties of external representations "reviewed and summarized" from Zhang (1997b) (p. 335). The nine entries include: "Provide information that can be directly perceived and used such that little effortful processing is needed"; "Anchor and structure cognitive behavior without conscious awareness"; "Change the nature of a task by generating more efficient action sequences"; "Stop time and support perceptual rehearsal to make invisible and transient information visible and sustainable"; and "Determine decision making strategies through accuracy maximization and effort minimization" (p. 335).

On groups, the authors set the reductionist view against the interactionist view (p. 335) and report that Zhang (1998) "demonstrated empirically that whether two minds were better or worse than one mind depended on how the knowledge was distributed across the two minds" (p. 336). The affordance section defines affordances as "distributed representations extended across the environment and the organism" (p. 337) and sorts them into biological, physical, perceptual, cognitive and mixed. A cognitive affordance is "provided by cultural conventions. For example, for traffic lights, red means 'stop'" (p. 339); the mailbox is mixed, because it "does not provide the affordance of mailing letters for a person who has no knowledge about postal systems" (p. 339).

## Relation to the argument

**P5.** Zhang (1997), the paper the facet was asked to read, is not reachable in full (see the facet file), so this summary is the best available statement of its property list. Three of the nine properties describe word separation by function: perceived and used without effortful processing, structuring behaviour without awareness, changing the nature of the task.

**P10 and D8 (custody of conventions).** The cognitive affordance is the paper's name for exactly the kind of thing a word space is: a structure that affords only for someone who holds the convention. That sharpens Larkin and Simon's caveat that a diagram helps only those who know the processes (existing card, p. 99) and places the convention, not the mark, as the thing that must be held. The authors do not ask who holds it.

**D9 (the triad).** The Zhang (1998) result is the nearest thing in this facet to a representational effect across more than one mind: the same abstract task, distributed differently across two people, produced "dramatically different group problem solving behaviors" (abstract of the 1998 paper, seen in the Consensus record and confirmed word for word in the OpenAlex record on 2026-10-15; the paper itself was not read). It is cooperative problem solving with no mediator, and the 1998 paper itself was not read.
