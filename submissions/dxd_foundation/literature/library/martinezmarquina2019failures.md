## What it argues

The Acquiring-a-Company problem: a firm is worth vL or vH with equal chance; a bid p ≥ v buys it for 1.5v − p. The authors build a deterministic twin in which both firms exist and one price goes to each, so payoffs equal the expected payoffs of the lottery but nothing is uncertain. They stress that the two "are truly different, and not just different frames of the same problem" (p. 5). Classifying by the last five rounds of part 1 and all of part 2, 41.5% of subjects in the deterministic treatment used the payoff-maximising strategy against 19.7% in the probabilistic one (Table 3, p. 15); the treatment effect is 0.244 with risk-lottery choices controlled (Table 4, p. 17). Against a one-value baseline of 62.6%, the loss of certainty "accounts for more than half" of the Complexity of Contingent Reasoning (p. 19), or 44.2% among subjects who never took a risky lottery; in payoff terms about 70% (p. 19). Advice written for a later participant mentioned all four outcomes in 52.5% of deterministic subjects against 26.6% of probabilistic ones (Table 6, p. 22), and controlling for this removes most of the treatment gap (p. 22). Advisees who read five pieces of advice reached 72.5% optimal play in the deterministic problem against 41.5% without advice, but 24.4% in the probabilistic problem against 19.7%: "the advice [is] helpful in deterministic but not in probabilistic problems" (p. 26). A probability-matching replication shows a 10-point effect (Table 9, p. 28). Feedback was withheld because "feedback may be more informative in det than in prob", where it "is the outcome of a lottery" (p. 8). The discussion links the result to Li (2017): p = vL is obviously dominant in the deterministic problem and not in the probabilistic one (pp. 30–31), and proposes a second-price auction in which the bidder faces every opponent type at once as a way to restore certainty (p. 32).

## Relation to the argument

**The mechanism behind "strategic offloading (Li, 2017)" (P10).** This paper says what the clock auction removes: not information, but the uncertainty that stops people considering every contingency. A mediator that resolves the lottery before the person acts carries the contingent reasoning for them. That is the abstract's design move stated as a result, though in a decision problem rather than a game, and the authors themselves note that obvious dominance may not be "exactly the right concept" for their data (p. 31).

**D3 and P8 (disclosure).** Advice, the nearest thing here to disclosure, raised optimal play by 31 points under certainty and by 5 points under uncertainty. The author's ruling that disclosure does not help people navigate holds in the uncertain case, which is the platform case, and fails in the certain one.

**P9 ("ecological feedback immediacy").** The authors' reason for giving no feedback is the facet's sharpest qualification of the abstract's third condition: when the outcome is a lottery over a counterpart's type, prompt feedback is still noisy, and "learning may be slower when feedback is the result of a lottery rather than the expected outcome" (p. 9, citing Bereby-Meyer and Roth). Immediacy is not enough; the feedback must be about the rule, not about the draw.

**Not a representational effect.** Because the two problems differ in substance, the result is a task change, not Zhang and Norman's isomorphic re-representation. That is the right category for a change in the mediator, and the draft should keep the two apart.

## Caution

Working draft; AER pagination and possibly numbers differ. MTurk, no counterpart, no feedback, no repeated interaction; the "strategic" extension to auctions is a proposal, not an experiment. The share of payoff-maximisers in the probabilistic problem may be understated if some subjects were risk-seeking, which the authors address with two controls but cannot rule out entirely.
