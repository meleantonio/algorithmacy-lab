## What it argues
Joint activity among people has four requirements: participants must "Enter into an agreement, which we call a Basic Compact", "Be mutually predictable in their actions", "Be mutually directable" and "Maintain common ground" (p. 91). The Compact "involves a commitment to some degree of goal alignment", typically "one or more participants relaxing their own shorter-term goals" for team goals (p. 91); it "has to be continuously reinforced or renewed", and a party who drops out "must signal this to the other parties" (p. 91). From these the authors derive ten challenges for automation. Challenge 1 is the Compact itself: "No form of automation today or on the horizon can enter fully into the rich forms of Basic Compact that are used among people" (p. 92). Challenge 2 is modelling the other participants. Challenge 3 is predictability: an agent "should act neither capriciously nor unobservably", and "Ironically, by making agents more adaptable, we might also make them less predictable" (p. 92). Challenge 4 is directability through policies that regulate behaviour "without changing code" (p. 93). Challenge 5 is revealing status and intentions: flight management systems "often leave commercial pilots baffled in some situations, wondering what the automation is currently doing, why it's doing that, and what it will do next"; so agents "must make their own targets, states, capacities, intentions, changes, and upcoming actions obvious", a demand that "runs counter to the advice sometimes given to automation developers to create systems that are barely noticed" (p. 93). Challenge 6 is interpreting the humans' signals: "Few existing agents are intended to read their operator teammates' signals with any degree of substantial understanding, let alone nuance" (p. 93). Challenge 7 is goal negotiation: "agents must convey their current and potential goals so that appropriate team members can participate in the negotiations" (p. 93). Challenge 8 is collaborative planning, "incremental, subject to negotiation, and forever tentative" (p. 93). Challenge 9 is attention management: "Automation can compensate for trouble ... but currently does so invisibly", so crews take over too late (p. 94). Challenge 10 is cost control: "Coordination doesn't come for free", and "the agents must conform to the operators' needs rather than require operators to adapt to them" (p. 94). The challenges can be read as a design blueprint, as "cautionary tales" ("Simply relying on explicit procedures, such as common operating pictures, isn't likely to be sufficient"), and as the human-centred alternative to emulating human capabilities (p. 94). Agents "might eventually be fellow team members with humans in the way a young child or a novice can be" (p. 94).

## Relation to the argument
This is where the supervisory lineage admits more than one human and admits the machine as a party with goals (facet question 3). The paper's unit is an ensemble of people coordinating, and its agents have "current and potential goals" that must be negotiated (p. 93). That cuts against reading the lineage as nothing but an operator paired with a tool. But it supports the narrower claim the abstract needs: the machine's goals are to be aligned to the team's through the Compact; an agent is a junior teammate, "a young child or a novice". The algorithmic mediator of the abstract, which "binds and optimizes" between two counterparts whose goals differ from each other and from its own, is not a case these authors consider.

On the read-to-check sentence the paper is double-edged. Challenge 5 asks the machine to make itself legible, which is the literacy-side remedy; Challenges 4, 6, 7 and 9 ask for directability, signal reading and negotiation, which are closer to the engagement the abstract wants, and the authors say explicit procedures and shared displays alone "isn't likely to be sufficient" (p. 94). For D7 and P9: common ground "has to be continuously reinforced" through action and repair, not read once.

## Caution
A five-page position piece with no data; the FMS "baffled" finding is cited from Woods & Sarter (2000). Copy from the author's archive, not IEEE Xplore, but journal layout and pagination. "Team player" here means an agent aligned with a human team; do not cite it as evidence about adversarial or interested machines, nor for 2020s AI.
