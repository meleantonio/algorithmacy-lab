## What it argues

Körber builds a model of trust in automation from Mayer, Davis & Schoorman (1995) and Lee & See (2004), and a questionnaire (TiA) to measure it (pp. 1–4). The model has Reliability/Competence, Understandability/Predictability and Intention of Developers as perceived trustworthiness, plus Propensity to Trust and Familiarity (Figure 1, p. 4). He defines trust in automation as the attitude of a user "to be willing to be vulnerable to the actions of an automated system based on the expectation that it will perform a particular action important to the user, irrespective of the ability to monitor or to intervene" (p. 4).

The exploratory factor analysis (n = 58) found four factors; no factor for Intention of Developers emerged (p. 7). Fit was mediocre: RMSEA 0.09, TLI .85 (Table 3, p. 6). In the second study, participants rated the reliable system more reliable (d = 1.05), but the full questionnaire did not differ between conditions (t(46.92) = 1.21, p = .23), while the single item "I trust this system" did (d = 0.71) (p. 5). In the simulator study, higher trust scores went with more reliance across behavioural measures (p. 9).

On mental models he follows Lee & See: operators "probably rely less on analytic calculations" and use heuristics, and "A situation might occur where operators cannot form a complete mental model of an automated system as it is too complex to perfectly predict its behavior" (p. 10). Citing Lee & See (2004), he describes the conceptualisations of trust in automation as treating it as an attitude, "a mainly affective response closely related to beliefs and expectations" (p. 2).

## Relation to the argument

**Premise P8 and ruling D3.** Körber's scale includes Understandability/Predictability ("I was able to understand why things happened", item 11), so the instrument assumes that understanding a system is one basis of trust. It does not claim that showing internals produces an accurate mental model; it says complete mental models may be out of reach and that trust is mainly affective (pp. 2, 10). That is closer to D3 than to the "classical" view the draft attributes to it.

**What it cuts.** It is a measurement paper about a single driver and a single automated system. It has nothing to say about mediated coordination or a third party.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 31: "The implicit cognitive model was classical: if an interface can render the internal state, training weights, or decision boundaries of an automated system visible, the human operator will form an accurate mental model, calibrate their trust appropriately, and make better decisions (Lee & See, 2004; Körber, 2018)." **Not supported.** Körber develops a trust questionnaire; he makes no claim that rendering internals visible yields an accurate mental model or better decisions, and, following Lee & See (2004), he states the opposite tendency: operators may be unable to form a complete mental model and trust is "a mainly affective response" (pp. 2, 10). The source could support a narrower sentence, that the trust-in-automation literature treats understandability as one basis of trust (Figure 1, p. 4; items 2, 7, 11, 16).

## Caution

A preprint of a short proceedings chapter. Samples are small (n = 58 for the factor analysis, a case-to-item ratio of about 3:1, which the author calls "the absolute minimum", p. 8) and participants watched videos instead of using a system (p. 8). The criterion-validity results are reported from another paper. The abstract (p. 1) says the first online study eliminated 40 items and left 19; the body says 32 items were cut to leave 25 (p. 5), then merging cut them to 17 (p. 5) and two trust items were added after the factor analysis (p. 8). The card follows the body. The draft cites the preprint; the version of record is the IEA 2018 proceedings chapter (Springer, AISC 823, pp. 13–30, 2019), which the author may prefer to cite.
