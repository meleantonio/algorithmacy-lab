# Enqvist, L. (2023). 'Human oversight' in the EU artificial intelligence act: What, when and by whom? *Law, Innovation and Technology, 15*(2), 508–535. https://doi.org/10.1080/17579961.2023.2245683

## What it argues
Human oversight is "a type of counterbalancing act between the aims of automation and authentic human reasoning" (pp. 508–509) and a shift "from a substantive and proactive approach to a procedural and reactive approach" (p. 511). Following Koulu, "for law, human oversight provides an attractive, easily implementable and observable procedural safeguard" (p. 512). The AI HLEG's inverse rule is quoted: "the less oversight a human can exercise over an AI system, the more extensive testing and stricter governance is required" (p. 512). The forms are catalogued as human-in-the-loop, on-the-loop and in-command, with "no consensus on how to categorise the particular responsibilities" and "no real consensus on what they should do" (pp. 513–514).

On the "what": Article 14 obliges providers to design systems so that overseers can understand capacities and limitations and "duly monitor its operation", "remain aware of the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output", "correctly interpret" the output, decide not to use or to "disregard, override or reverse" it, and "intervene ... or interrupt the system through a 'stop' button" (p. 519, quoting draft Art. 14(4)(a)–(e)). The Council draft dropped "fully" before "understand" (p. 519). Oversight works "Through human intermediaries as interpreters of the data produced by the operating algorithms"; "Without a sufficient level of system transparency ... the human overseers would have nothing substantive to review" (pp. 517–518). "There is no real point in performing the oversight if the overseer is not provided with information (or system output) in such a representation that it can interpret or understand" (p. 521). Article 12 logs feed this, but the draft "is not very detailed on the specific content of these logs" (p. 523), and Article 13's transparency rests on provider-written instructions that users must follow (p. 524). The conclusion on the "what": the Act "would ... leave a great deal of room for the providers to determine the detail on what human overseers will be presented with, and thus be able to direct their attention to" (p. 525).

On the "when": the Article "only prescribes the technical capabilities for overseers to actively (thus on human initiative) intervene" (p. 527), except the two-person rule for biometric systems; "the particular human overseer may have difficulties identifying those stages of the process that would benefit from human input – without help from the system itself" (p. 528). On the "by whom": Article 14 is addressed to providers and "does not, in itself, address whom in particular should perform the oversight at the user's end" (p. 529); the Council and Parliament drafts add competence and training duties for users (p. 530). The Act "addresses the question of human agency primarily through technical architecture requirements" (p. 533), and the risk is "a human oversight infrastructure that chiefly allows for mere superficial human involvement" (p. 534).

## Relation to the argument
For facet question 1 this is the clearest statement in the 2020s literature that the regulated human's role is reading: the overseer is an "interpreter" of system output and logs, and without something to review the role is empty (pp. 518, 521). That supports the abstract's second sentence for the "enterprise audit log" case and gives it a statutory basis, since Article 12 logs and Article 13 instructions are what the law provides for the person to read. The provider-decides-what-is-shown finding (p. 525) is the legal form of the argument-map's P12: the interface owner sets the convention.

On the dyad, the paper cuts against the literal claim and supports the narrower one. The Act's structure is a chain, provider to user to assigned person to system, with affected persons protected but given no interface; so the model is organisational, not a pair. Yet the act of oversight itself remains one person facing one system's output, which is the operator–tool pair restated in law. Nothing in the paper treats the system as a party with its own objective between two counterparts; the platform settings of the abstract (drivers and riders) are not high-risk systems under the Act as discussed here, so the Article does not reach them (compare `eu2024regulation`).

## Caution
Analyses the drafts (Commission 2021, Council 2022, Parliament 2023), not the Regulation as enacted in 2024; article numbers for users/deployers (29) differ in the final text (26). A doctrinal paper with no empirical data on how overseers perform. Do not cite it for the Act's treatment of general-purpose models or platforms, which it does not cover.
