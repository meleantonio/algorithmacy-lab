## What it argues

Dranove and Jin define quality disclosure as a certifier's effort to "systematically measure and report product quality" for a market (§I.1, pdf p. 4) and review three questions: why sellers do not disclose voluntarily, when government should mandate disclosure, and whether certifiers report accurately (abstract).

On theory (§II): the "unraveling" result, under which every seller but the worst discloses, rests on strong assumptions; it fails with disclosure costs, some market structures, inattentive or naive consumers, and multidimensional quality. Mandated disclosure can raise welfare, but it can also be excessive or induce gaming, "as may be the case for hospital report cards that encourage providers to avoid the sickest patients" (§II.1, pdf p. 17). Certifiers have their own incentive problems, and competition, reputation and monitoring do not reliably fix them (§II.2).

On evidence (§III):
- **Consumers.** In health plans, schools and cereals, consumers move toward higher-rated sellers (§III.3). Effects can be small: health-plan ratings changed the choices of 0.7 percent of federal annuitants (pdf p. 31). Consumers respond to overall indices and discrete rankings more than to specific measures, which the authors read as consumers who "may have limited cognitive ability and therefore tend to focus on a subset of measures that are easier to understand" (pdf p. 30). In sum, consumers "respond to quality disclosure when rankings differ from preconceptions" (pdf p. 32).
- **Sellers.** Some studies find quality improvements: Los Angeles restaurant grades (Jin and Leslie 2003), with hospitalisations down 20 percent "largely because restaurants and consumers preparing meals at home improved hygiene as opposed to consumers choosing more hygienic restaurants"; and drinking-water reports that cut violations by 30–44 percent (pdf pp. 32–33). Others find gaming: effort shifts to reported dimensions in nursing homes; providers select healthier patients under cardiac report cards; schools reclassify students (pdf pp. 33–35). For schools, publishing report cards "has zero impact" once accountability laws are controlled for, suggesting improvement "is more attributable to consequential accountability rather than disclosure per se" (pdf p. 35).

Their conclusion is two-sided. Disclosure "has allowed consumers to find sellers who best meet their needs, including restaurants, education and healthcare", but "There is less evidence that sellers respond by boosting quality. Instead, most studies of seller responses seem to focus on gaming behavior that often harms consumers" (§IV, pdf p. 39). "It is difficult to state with confidence that disclosure in such important sectors as healthcare, education, or finance has unambiguously helped consumers" (§IV, pdf p. 40).

## Relation to the argument

**Line 164 and line 500.** The survey supports the simple-summary half of the draft's claim: consumers use overall grades and rankings more than detailed measures (pdf pp. 29–30). It cuts against the other half, that working disclosures act through the discloser: the authors find seller improvement less common than gaming. On their reading, a working disclosure works mainly through buyers' sorting, and sometimes through sellers, and the seller channel is where it most often goes wrong.

**Ruling D3 and premise P8.** As a defence of disclosure this is moderate: disclosure helps people choose among sellers when the rating contradicts what they believed. That is a reading task with a measurable behavioural effect, which narrows the draft's claim (line 162) that people do not use disclosures. The settings are dyadic purchase choices, not a triad in which the mediator sets the terms.

**Premise P12.** The finding that school improvement comes from "consequential accountability rather than disclosure per se" (pdf p. 35) supports the paper's §7.3 claim that outside compulsion, not disclosure, moves an interested party.

## Caution

A survey by two authors whose own studies are central to it. Its evidence is about quality disclosure for buyers choosing sellers; it does not address disclosure of a mediator's rules, algorithmic systems or platforms. Read as the accepted working paper; the JEL text may differ in wording.
