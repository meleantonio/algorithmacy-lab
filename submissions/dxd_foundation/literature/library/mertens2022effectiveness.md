## What it argues

Mertens and colleagues estimate how much choice architecture changes behaviour and which kind works best. They sort interventions with Münscher et al.'s taxonomy into three categories (Table 1): decision information, which addresses "Limited access to decision-relevant information" and includes "Translate information: adapt attributes to facilitate processing of already available information", making information visible, and social reference points; decision structure (defaults, option-related effort, range or composition of options, option consequences); and decision assistance (reminders, commitment).

Corrected results (Correction, Table 2 and p. 3 replacement). The overall effect is d = 0.43 (95% CI 0.38 to 0.48), "small to medium". By category: information d = 0.34 (k = 130), structure d = 0.54 (k = 223), assistance d = 0.28 (k = 94); structure beats information by b = 0.19 (95% CI 0.08 to 0.31, P = 0.001) and assistance by b = 0.26; information and assistance do not differ (P = 0.21) (Correction, p. 4 replacement). By technique, translation is d = 0.28 and defaults d = 0.62. Finance is the least responsive domain (d = 0.24) and food the most (d = 0.65). The original version had d = 0.45, information 0.38, structure 0.55, assistance 0.31; the authors say the corrections changed neither "the pattern of the results or any of the conclusions" (Correction, final paragraph).

Two qualifications from the authors themselves. Publication bias: Egger's test is positive, and under a severe one-tailed bias assumption the overall effect shrinks to d = 0.08 (corrected; p. 4); "the true effect size of interventions is likely to be smaller than estimated" (p. 4, quoted again in the Reply). Mechanism: their account of why structure wins — lower processing demand and less dependence on individual values — "remains somewhat speculative" (Discussion). Heterogeneity is very high (I² = 99.52% corrected).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D3 and premise P8.** Mertens supplies the measured contrast the draft wants: interventions that change the structure of a choice outperform those that change the information about it. The margin is modest (0.54 against 0.34), both are positive, and the Maier et al. reanalysis that the authors answer reports, after bias correction, evidence *against* an effect for information and assistance interventions and undecided evidence for structure (Maier et al., 2022, Europe PMC PMC9351501, main text; the Reply itself does not break the letters' results down by category). So the source supports "the informational kind is weaker", not "informational interventions do nothing".

**Claim 2 and Convention A.** The taxonomy cuts two ways for DXD. Re-representing information ("translate information") sits in the information category and is the weakest information technique (d = 0.28, corrected). That helps the draft's claim that DXD must differ from re-presentation, and it warns that a re-representation counted by this taxonomy has a weak record. Convention A — removing a strategic task from the mechanism — looks more like decision structure (changing "the format of decision making", Table 1), which is the strongest category. Nothing in the meta-analysis tests a mediated triad or a strategic setting.
