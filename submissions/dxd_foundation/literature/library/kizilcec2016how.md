# Kizilcec, R. F. (2016). How much information? Effects of transparency on trust in an algorithmic interface. In *Proceedings of the 2016 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 2390–2395). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858402

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/2858036.2858402 · **Read depth:** full_text (the ACM-formatted PDF as hosted on a McGill course page, 6 pp., pagination 2390–2395, read whole) · **Source-tier:** proceedings (CHI, peer-reviewed; a short paper) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** online field experiment in a Coursera MOOC; 120 learners took part, 103 analysed (33% women, mean age 37.15, SD 10.85, from 79 self-reports); random assignment to low (n = 39), medium (34) or high (30) transparency about an algorithmically adjusted peer grade; outcome a four-item trust index (α = 0.83); expectation violation measured as self grade minus peer grade (p. 2392) · **Parties modeled:** person–system (learner and grading algorithm; peers grade but are not a strategic counterpart) · **Facets:** U · **Read:** 2026-10-15

## What it argues
Three levels of transparency: a bare grade; the grade plus a procedural explanation that it was "adjusted for their bias and accuracy in grading" by an expectation-maximisation algorithm; and that explanation plus the raw peer grades and the adjustment (p. 2392). Trust was lower when the grade was worse than expected (F(1,97) = 13.4, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.12) and the gap depended on transparency (F(2,97) = 3.48, p = 0.035); there was no main effect of transparency (p. 2392). In the low condition, violated versus met expectations gave trust 3.02 against 4.18 (d = 1.01); in the medium condition 3.72 against 3.70; in the high condition 2.77 against 3.89 (d = 1.08) (p. 2392). Expectation violation correlated with trust in low (r = −0.59) and high (r = −0.55) but not medium (p = 0.79) conditions (p. 2393). "Ratings of system comprehension were in fact higher in the medium than high transparency condition" (p. 2393). Conclusion: "Designing for trust requires balanced interface transparency—not too little and not too much" (abstract, p. 2390). Limitations: "the small sample size, the focus on self-report outcomes" (p. 2393).

## Relation to the argument
**Counter-evidence for Q4 (where disclosure helped).** A procedural explanation restored trust among people whose expectations were violated; this is a case where disclosure did something. But the outcome is attitude, not behaviour or decision quality, and more disclosure undid the gain. So it supports P8 in a narrow form: disclosure moves trust, has an inverted-U, and is not shown to improve navigation.

**Ruling D3.** The paper's own frame is "algorithm awareness"; it is a literacy intervention (an explanation to read) and the author can cite it as such.

**Triad.** None; the peers are graders, not a counterpart with whom the learner coordinates.
