## What it argues

People typed heard word lists into a text file, did a 30-s distractor task, then recalled with the file open. On the fourth trial the experimenter inserted an unrelated word into the file. The authors frame this as the "endorsement" problem of distributed memory (ms. p. 4, after Arango-Muñoz): deciding whether to trust what the store returns.

Experiment 1: 100% of participants wrote the inserted word down (p. 10). Mean confidence that it had been presented was 79% when inserted mid-list and 46% when inserted first (p. 10). Asked afterwards to pick the added word, 53% (first) and 28% (middle) did so (p. 13); 19–33% met every criterion for complete unawareness (p. 15). Experiment 2 added a surprise recognition test without the file: participants said "yes" to the inserted word 60% of the time against 66% for words actually on the list, t(39) = 0.77, BF01 = 4.43, and 2.5% for new words (pp. 20–21). Experiment 3 (N = 120) replaced recall with item-by-item recognition: with the file open, 78% (middle) and 77% (first) endorsed the inserted word, most "definitely" (pp. 26–27); without the file, 45% and 50% endorsed it, between the 70% for list words and 4% for new words (pp. 28–29). The authors' summary: "'new' words were rarely endorsed, except when those 'new' words were inserted into individual's external memory store" (p. 34). Susceptibility was not total: confidence in inserted items was lower than in presented items, and responses to them were slower (pp. 31, 36). Because the delay between encoding and retrieval was under a minute, the result "almost certainly underestimates" everyday susceptibility (p. 34).

## Relation to the argument

**D8, D9 and P13 (who holds the store).** This is the facet's most direct evidence on a cost that the dyadic offloading literature cannot show: when the external store is in someone else's hands, its contents can be changed, people seldom notice, and the change passes into their own memory. In a triad the mediator holds the record. The paper does not study an interested manipulator, but it shows the mechanism that would let one work. It bears on the author's reading of Nadella's audit log (P13): a record kept by the deploying organisation is, on this evidence, a store whose edits its reader will endorse.

**Clark and Chalmers' conditions.** The paper is the experimental Twin Otto (`clark1998extended`, §4): change the notebook and you change the belief. It turns the extended-mind criterion "automatically endorses it" into a liability.

**Costs of offloading.** Add to `sparrow2011google` and `fisher2015searching`: forgetting, illusory knowing, and now false memory from a manipulated store.

## Caution

Accepted manuscript; the Cognition pagination (article 103954) was not checked. One manipulated trial per participant, between-subjects, student samples, single inserted words, no motive on the manipulator's side. Results varied between experiments (confidence 79% against 50% at the same position; endorsement without the file 60% against 45%), which the authors attribute partly to the one-trial design (p. 37).
