## What it argues
Von Hippel argues that "innovation is being democratized": "users of products and services—both firms and individual consumers—are increasingly able to innovate for themselves," and "Users that innovate can develop exactly what they want, rather than relying on manufacturers to act as their (often very imperfect) agents" (p. 1). The opening case is high-performance windsurfing, where a user group added footstraps to boards in 1978 and "a large fraction of the boards sold" came to carry user-developed features by 1998 (pp. 1–2). The chapter 1 summary lays out the book: studies find 10 to nearly 40 percent of users modify or develop products (p. 4, as the author reports it; I did not read the studies); user needs are heterogeneous, so "a few sizes fit all" leaves many dissatisfied (p. 5); in a sample of Apache users, 19 percent innovated, and those who did were more satisfied (pp. 5–6); users often "freely reveal" what they build (pp. 9–10), form innovation communities (pp. 10–11), and policy should not favour manufacturers (pp. 11–13).

Two parts bear on this paper. First, agency costs. Chapter 1 (p. 6) and chapter 4 (pp. 46–49) apply Jensen and Meckling (1976) to the choice between innovating for oneself and hiring a custom manufacturer: "when a user develops its own custom product that user can be trusted to act in its own best interests" (p. 6). A hired manufacturer is an agent whose interest diverges, since it "wants to lower its development costs by incorporating solution elements it already has" (p. 6). Chapter 4 adds that manufacturers know more than users about which solution is best and "have a strong incentive to provide biased information to users" (p. 48); contracts cannot close the gap because neither side can specify the need fully in advance (p. 48). Second, toolkits (chapter 11). A manufacturer hands users a design toolkit tied to its production system, so users do the need-heavy design work while the maker keeps the solution-heavy work. Toolkits "can channel innovative effort into directions supported by toolkits" (p. 147), and the freedom they give is bounded by the maker's "solution space": designs fall in the space because "custom products can be produced at reasonable prices only when custom user designs can be implemented by simply making low-cost adjustments to the production process" (p. 157). Cited figures: more than $15 billion of custom chips in 2000 were designed with toolkits (p. 148), and 100 toolkits in computer gaming (Prügl and Franke 2005) were studied, with success judged by independent experts, with success tied to trial-and-error learning, solution-space fit, user-friendliness and module libraries (pp. 161–162).

## Relation to the argument
The facet cites the book for the opening page's point that users rely on manufacturers "to act as their (often very imperfect) agents." That is on p. 1 as quoted. The book also supports premise P10, in a form the facet does not use: it applies the same agency-cost source (Jensen and Meckling) to the user–supplier relationship and says the supplier has reason to bias what the user is told. Chapter 11 is nearer still to what this paper cares about: the party that owns the production system decides the solution space of the toolkit it lends, and a toolkit channels what users build. That is a case where a convention (the toolkit) is offered by an interested party. Von Hippel treats the arrangement as workable and profitable for both sides, so he gives no support for the claim that a withdrawable convention is no convention (P10's `[inf]` step). He is also about product design, not mediated coordination between two users, so the triad is out of scope (ruling D9).

## In conversation with
Cites and builds on Jensen and Meckling (1976) (`jensen1976theory`), Aghion and Tirole (1994) and Bessen (2004) on contracts (named in chapter 4, p. 48, not chapter 1), Raymond (1999) on open source, Shah (2000) on windsurfing, Franke and Shah (2003), Lessig (2001), Benkler (2002), and Pinch and Bijker (1987), as named in chapter 1. None except Jensen and Meckling is in the bib. Leads: Thomke and von Hippel (2002) on toolkits; Franke and von Hippel (2003) on Apache; Henkel and von Hippel (2005) on welfare.

## Caution
Partial read; see above. The book's claims on user innovation come from studies I did not examine. Its tone is advocacy for user innovation, and its toolkit chapter reports commercial cases where manufacturer and user interests are treated as aligned enough to trade.
