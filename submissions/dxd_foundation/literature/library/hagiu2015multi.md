## What it argues

Hagiu and Wright give a definition of multi-sided platforms (MSPs) and a model of when a firm should be one. The definition has two parts: MSPs "enable direct interactions between two or more distinct sides" and "Each side is affiliated with the platform" (p. 5). "By 'direct interaction' we mean that the two or more distinct sides retain control over the key terms of the interaction, as opposed to the intermediary taking control of those terms. Where the interaction involves trading, the key terms … could be the pricing, bundling, marketing and delivery of the goods or services traded" (p. 5). The MSP mode "involves contractual relationships between buyers and professionals, to which the focal firm is not a party, but merely an enabler of those contractual relationships" (p. 4). They list Uber and Lyft among MSP-mode firms whose "independent professionals control the nature and quality of services offered, as well as (sometimes) the prices charged" (p. 8). Business models between the poles exist: an intermediary may hold "residual control rights over some key decision variables, while residual control rights over other decision variables remain with A (or B)," a form "intermediate between a pure MSP model and a pure reseller model" (p. 21).

The model: N professionals, a transferable action a_i (controlled by the firm in VI mode, by the professional in MSP mode) with spillover x, and non-transferable effort e_i with weight γ. Proposition 1 (p. 14): without bonuses or variable fees, "the MSP mode is preferred to the VI mode if and only if x² < γ² + Var(θ)," that is, when spillovers are small relative to moral hazard and the professionals' private information. Bonuses shift the trade-off toward VI (Proposition 2, p. 14); variable fees shift it back toward MSP (Proposition 3, p. 15); pessimistic expectations favour VI (Proposition 4, p. 18). Their earlier work is summarised: Amazon resells "more than 50% of new books listed on its site, whereas for electronics Amazon sells closer to 1% of the new items listed" (p. 22). MSPs "cannot easily aggregate the bargaining power of their many users" (p. 23).

## Relation to the argument

**Question 1 (the platform as a party with its own objective).** Supported: the MSP sets a fixed fee T and a variable fee t to maximise its own profit (p. 12–13), and the variable fee is chosen to correct spillovers among professionals (eq. 5, p. 13). The platform is a third party with its own objective by construction.

**The abstract's "binds."** Here the literature cuts against the author's wording. On the Hagiu–Wright definition an MSP is one whose sides "retain control over the key terms"; a mediator that *binds* prices and matches is, by that definition, moving toward the reseller or vertically-integrated pole (p. 21). The author's "binds and optimizes" therefore describes a hybrid, not a pure platform, and the paper names the hybrid. This is a useful correction: the triad the abstract describes exists where the intermediary has taken control of key terms while leaving the parties formally independent.

**Question 3.** The model is explicitly decomposable: the firm's problem separates into N professional–firm relations with a spillover term x. The triad's non-decomposability, if any, lives in x and in the network-effect term m(n), not in anything about the client–professional pair. That is a precise sense in which an economist would resist "irreducible."

## Caution

Read as a working paper; the published IJIO article may differ. A stylised model with exogenous per-client revenue π and no buyer-side pricing (p. 9). The empirical sentences about Amazon are from the authors' other paper. The definition is the authors' proposal and they note "disagreement among those in the literature" (p. 4).
