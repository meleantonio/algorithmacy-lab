## What it argues
The paper states the standard prescription and then questions it. The "almost mandatory statement" is that automatic devices will control the process "and that the human operator will be relegated to the post of monitor and decisionmaker, keeping watch for deviations and failures, and taking over when necessary" (p. 1). The second mandatory statement is that the human "is not so good at the monitoring task and is highly likely to miss critical signals" (p. 2), which tempts designers to "automate human error out of the system"; the authors reply that "the assumption that automation can eliminate human error must be questioned" and that flight-deck automation "may have already passed the point of optimality" (summary, p. 1).

Section 3 gives cases. A Swift Aire Nord 262 autofeather, built to stop crews shutting down the wrong engine, fired on a broken hydraulic hose; the crew shut down the good engine and ditched: "a device designed to automate human error out of the system had triggered a chain of events that was compounded by the very human error it was supposed to prevent" (p. 6). A Texas International DC-9 crew aborted a take-off on a spurious stick-shaker (p. 7). An Eastern L-1011 crew, absorbed in a landing-gear bulb, never noticed the autopilot had disengaged and flew into the Everglades "even with full cockpit instrumentation ... [and] a C-chime altitude alert that sounded" (p. 8). A training manager wrote of "a tendency to breed inactivity or complacency" in first officers after 8–9 months on automated L-1011s, so command training time was doubled (p. 9).

Section 4 lists problem areas as questions. "When control tasks are automated, the operator's role becomes one of a monitor and supervisor" (p. 9); will a monitor be a better or worse failure detector than an active controller; is there a "warmup" delay from passive monitor to active controller; should the automation "tell the operator why it is making the change or not?" (p. 10). An intermediate level of reliability "may be quite insidious since it will induce an impression of high reliability" (p. 10). Monitoring of complex systems is cognitive, not perceptual: the fuel example needs arithmetic, and the question is "What makes an automatic system more 'interpretable,' that is, easier to detect and diagnose malfunctions?" (p. 11). On alerting: will "smart" alerting logic "be too complex for operators to perform validity checks, and thus lead to over-reliance on the system?" (p. 12).

Section 5 separates two dimensions (Figure 1, p. 13): "Automation of control tasks implies that the operator is monitoring the computer, whereas automation of the monitoring tasks implies that the computer is monitoring the operator"; the corners are labelled boredom, complacency and erosion of competence against high workload and fatigue. Autopilots fail "gracefully," so gracefully that a decoupling may not be noticed; a B-707 lost 30,000 ft after a graceful disengage (p. 15). Even in the most automatic mode, "the equipment would process information and present alternatives to the pilot, who would weigh the results and make the command decision" (p. 16). The B-707 carried 188 warnings and caution alerts, the B-747 455, the DC-8 172 and the DC-10 418 (p. 17); pilots "generally perform many of their tasks as single-channel processors" (p. 17). The guidelines read include: "System operation should be easily interpretable or understandable by the operator to facilitate the detection of improper operation" (guideline 1, p. 19); "System monitoring is not only a legitimate, but a necessary activity of the human operator; however, it generally is second in priority to other, event-driven tasks" (guideline 3, p. 19); "Operators should be trained, motivated, and evaluated to monitor effectively" (guideline 5, p. 19). The conclusion: "the rapid pace of automation is outstripping one's ability to comprehend all the implications for crew performance" (p. 21).

## Relation to the argument
With `bainbridge1983ironies` this is the origin of the read-to-check frame the abstract's second sentence describes, written for the factory headset's ancestor, the automated flight deck. It supports the claim: the human is "monitor and decisionmaker"; the remedies are interpretability, alerting and training to monitor, all literacy-side. It supports the dyad: the unit is the crew and its automation, and the paper's two-dimensional figure has only those two parties watching each other.

It bears on D7 and P9 directly, and in both directions. Against the author's "pilots don't spend time reading the situation", the paper treats monitoring as "a necessary activity" that is "second in priority to other, event-driven tasks" (p. 19), and the Everglades crew's failure was a failure to read. For the author, the paper's own worry is that alerting logic grows "too complex for operators to perform validity checks" (p. 12), which is the limit on checking by reading that the abstract wants a design change to escape. The authors' answer is better design of the signal, not a change in what the person does. No third party and no interested mediator appear.

## Caution

The evidence is accident narratives and a letter, not experiments; the fuel-price and alert-count figures are 1980 industry numbers. Do not cite for anything after 1980, for ATC or for platform coordination, which the paper excludes or does not discuss.
