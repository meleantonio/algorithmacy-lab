## What it argues
Rees-Jones asks whether the benefits of strategy-proofness "are fully realized" in the NRMP, where "the optimal strategy in the student-proposing DAA is simple" but "the strategic environment remains quite complex" (p. 3). Applicants had abundant help: the NRMP website "explicitly describes the incentive compatibility of reporting true preferences, contains tutorials on the DAA, and advertises that research on it's algorithm contributed to the awarding of the 2012 Nobel Prize" (p. 8). Asked whether they reported "the exact ordering of your true preferences", 83% said yes; "The remaining 17% indicated that they pursued nontruthful reporting practices"; "5% of respondents report that their true preferences and submitted preferences differ due to an attempt at 'strategic behavior'"; under 1% called it a mistake; 11% gave "other reason", often family or location constraints that may reflect a narrow reading of "preferences" (pp. 10–11). Strategic misreporting was claimed by 7% of men against 4% of women (p. 12). In a multinomial logit, one SD of an academic-ability index cut strategic misreporting by 2 percentage points; women were 3 points less likely (p = 0.098); a one-unit rise in specialty competitiveness added 1 point (p = 0.053) (pp. 13–14). He reads the low rate as both "a success" and a sign that "the requisite levels of intelligence, information, and incentivization needed to ensure full compliance may never be achieved in practice" (p. 5), which calls for "the design of mechanisms that can minimize misunderstanding" (p. 5).

## Relation to the argument
The field counterpart to the abstract's "game the mediator" clause in a setting designed to make gaming pointless: one in twenty of highly trained applicants still tried, after tutorials and a Nobel-citing website, and the attempt tracked lower ability and fiercer competition. It supports the narrow reading of P10 (a strategy-proof rule removes most, not all, of the strategising) and ruling D3 (the disclosures and tutorials did not reach the residue). The 17% figure that Li (2024) and others cite is the total of all non-truthful self-reports; only 5% is avowedly strategic, and the card keeps that split. Nothing here tests a change of representation.
