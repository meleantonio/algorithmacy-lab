## What it argues

The paper's claim is that keeping a decision rule secret does not stop gaming. It makes people game a rule they have guessed, and the guess costs both the firm and the qualified users. The authors say standard strategic classification assumes users know the classifier. In practice, credit scores and similar rules are secret, "which leads agents to attempt to learn the classifier and game it too" (abstract, p. 1). In their model the user, denied the rule *f*, "is coerced to estimate *f* from data available to her (e.g., by observing the features of friends and the associated predicted outcomes)" (pp. 4–5). In theory this is *m* labelled samples. In the main experiments too, each user's *m* samples are drawn from a held-out pool, not from her network (pp. 10–11). Only the final "stories" experiment has users learn from network neighbours "up to two hops away" in the Prosper social network (p. 12; Appendix B gives the usable network as a subset of 994 users). They cite the OpenSCHUFA crowdsourced effort to "reverse-engineer" the German credit score as anecdotal evidence (p. 2).

The "price of opacity" (POP) is the extra error a firm suffers when users respond to their estimate rather than the true rule (Def. 2, p. 6). Small estimation errors can be enlarged by strategic movement (the "enlargement set", pp. 6–7). Theorem 2 gives a sufficient condition for POP > 0 (p. 8). Corollary 4 shows that every user in the enlargement set would be accepted under transparency and is rejected under opacity, which "disproportionately affects qualified users" (p. 8). On the split Gaussian, POP is about 0.5 for small *m* and plateaus near 0.3 (p. 11). On Prosper, POP is about 9% for small *m* and still about 4% at *m* = 4,096 (pp. 11–12). For low *m*, "roughly 50%" of qualified users who would be correctly accepted under full information are rejected in the dark (p. 12). The authors read this as "a clear form of inequity towards individuals who are not well-connected" (p. 12). That reading treats a small sample size *m* as a stand-in for few connections. It is an inference from the sampling experiment, not a result measured on the network. Five "stories" of individual users (A–E) show the ways a wrong estimate costs a user (pp. 12–13).

The discussion concludes that the work "provides formal incentives for firms to adopt transparency as a policy" (p. 14). It notes that the user models are simple and that richer models (Bayesian, bounded-rational) are future work (p. 14).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D7 and premise P9.** The model formalises learning a mediator from others' outcomes, the social learning the draft describes for drivers. In the model, though, people learn a hidden rule by statistical inference from neighbours' features and outcomes. That is closer to the draft's "deciding and taking in the results" than to reading. Yet it is not deciding either: the users do not experiment. They classify the outcomes they observe.

**Ruling D3 and premise P8 (it cuts against).** The paper's policy conclusion is to *publish the rule* (p. 14). In its model, disclosure of the classifier removes the price of opacity and the harm to poorly connected users. That pulls against the paper's view that disclosure is a literacy intervention with weak effects. The model assumes users who, once shown *f*, compute their best response exactly (Eq. 1, p. 4). A real person would need to read and use the disclosed rule, and the draft's evidence (P8) says many will not. The authors list bounded-rational users as future work (p. 14). The draft can use the inequity result (the less connected lose most) as support for a convention that does not depend on who a person knows. It should not cite the paper as indifferent to disclosure.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 266: "Formal models of strategic classification under opacity describe the same situation: when a decision rule is hidden, people infer it from the observed outcomes of others (Ghalme et al., 2021)." Supported: the user estimates *f* "by observing the features of friends and the associated predicted outcomes" (pp. 4–5), and in the illustrative "stories" experiment from network neighbours (p. 12). The main POP experiments sample others' outcomes at random, not through a network (pp. 10–11). Two qualifications. The inference is a modelling assumption, not an observation of people. And the paper's conclusion favours transparency (p. 14), which the draft does not say. The draft's own repair note (line 743) records that an earlier version cited Ghalme for drivers sharing screenshots. The present wording is the correct one.

## Caution

The evidence is theory and simulation. Prosper's own risk scores serve as labels and users are simulated. No person was observed learning a rule. The users are rational best-responders, which overstates what a disclosed rule would achieve with real people. Appendix B was not read. The draft lists the paper as an arXiv preprint, but it appeared at ICML 2021 (PMLR 139:3672–3681).
