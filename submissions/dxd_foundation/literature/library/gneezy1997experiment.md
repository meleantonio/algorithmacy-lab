# Gneezy, U., & Potters, J. (1997). An experiment on risk taking and evaluation periods. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics*, *112*(2), 631–645. https://doi.org/10.1162/003355397555217

**Identifier:** doi:10.1162/003355397555217 · **Read depth:** full_text (author-hosted copy at UC San Diego Rady School, journal pagination 631–645 with the instructions appendix; local copy dxd_foundation_local/pdfs/AA/gneezy1997experiment.pdf) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** pen-and-paper laboratory experiment at Tilburg; 84 students in 14 sessions of six; two treatments, H (feedback and choice every round, n = 41 after one exclusion) and L (feedback and a fixed bet every three rounds, n = 42); twelve rounds, lottery with probability 2/3 of losing the stake and 1/3 of winning 2.5 times it (pp. 634–637) · **Parties modeled:** person–lottery (one decision-maker and a fixed stochastic environment) · **Facets:** AA · **Read:** 2026-10-15

## What it argues

A direct test of myopic loss aversion. If people weigh losses more than gains and evaluate outcomes over the period in which they receive them, then "the more frequently returns are evaluated, the more risk averse investors will be" (p. 631). The manipulation is feedback frequency and freedom of adjustment: in H subjects learned the outcome after every round; in L they bet the same amount for three rounds and saw only the aggregate, so that "it was not possible for them to attribute a gain or a loss to any particular round in the block of three" (fn. 7, p. 637).

Results (Table I, p. 639): across rounds 1–9, subjects bet 50.5% of the endowment in H against 67.4% in L (SD 26.7 and 27.3; Mann–Whitney z = −2.86, one-tailed p = 0.002). The difference held in every block and was present in round 1 (50.1% against 66.7%, p = 0.009), before any feedback had arrived, which the authors read as forward-looking evaluation (p. 639). Bets after a loss were not lower than after a gain; "for such a backward looking hypothesis we find no support" (p. 639). In part 2, with own earnings at stake, L again bet more (887 against 707 cents, p = 0.016), and L earned more in total (2,134 against 1,822 cents, p = 0.038; Table II, p. 640).

The practical remark: "providing investors with less frequent information feedback about how a particular risky fund is doing might make the fund appear more attractive by decreasing the likelihood that a loss will be experienced," and "tying their hands" may help them "resist the temptation to drop out after the occasional setback" (p. 641). The authors flag the stylisation: known probabilities, under an hour, low stakes (p. 641).

## Relation to the argument

**Q3, when immediacy hurts.** This is the standard case in which more frequent feedback produced worse outcomes by the subjects' own measure: lower bets on a positive-expectation gamble and lower earnings (Table II, p. 640). The mechanism is not slower learning; it is evaluation. People shown each result separately experience more losses and choose more timidly. For the abstract's sentence the lesson is that feedback immediacy changes what a decision is *about*, not only how fast one learns it.

**The triad.** Drivers and sellers receive outcome feedback trip by trip and sale by sale, with the platform choosing the unit of account. On this paper's evidence, the mediator's choice of evaluation period is itself a convention that moves behaviour, which belongs under P12 (who holds the convention) as much as under P10.

**Caveat for the author.** The design confounds feedback frequency with freedom to adjust (p. 635); later work (Bellemare et al. 2005, listed in the facet as not opened) separates them. The round-1 effect (p. 639) means the result is partly about anticipated, not received, feedback.

## Caution

Eighty-four students, guilder stakes of a few dollars, a 40-minute session, known probabilities. Treatment L bundled two changes, feedback frequency and commitment, and the paper cannot separate them. One-tailed tests. The authors themselves list these as "a cause for caution in extrapolating" (p. 641). Nothing in the design involves a counterpart or an adaptive mediator.
