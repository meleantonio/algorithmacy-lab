# Boelhouwer, A., van den Beukel, A. P., van der Voort, M. C., & Martens, M. H. (2019). Should I take over? Does system knowledge help drivers in making take-over decisions while driving a partially automated car? *Transportation Research Part F, 60*, 669–684.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1016/j.trf.2018.11.016 · **Read depth:** full_text (publisher-typeset PDF from the University of Twente repository; journal pagination 669–684 intact; I read all of it including the two appendices and the reference list) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** between-participants experiment with repeated measures; 28 adults (14 per group, mean age 38.6) in a video-based driving simulator, 556 take-over decisions in total; one group read a manual-style system description for 15 minutes, the other received nothing (pp. 671–672, 675–676) · **Parties modeled:** person–system (driver and a partially automated car); no third party · **Facets:** H · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues

The authors ask whether owner's-manual information lets a driver judge when a partially automated car can no longer cope. Group 2 studied a written description of the car's functions, sensors, ranges and limits, then passed an eight-item knowledge test (mean 92.31 out of 100, SD 9.60; p. 675). Both groups then watched 23 filmed urban scenarios and pressed a button when they would take back control. The researcher had coded each scenario beforehand as requiring a take-over or not (p. 672).

The result is a null on the main measure. Group 2 scored 73.29% correct (SD 9.53) against 68.67% (SD 12.68) for group 1, and "the GEE modelling showed no significant effects of system information on take-over score" (χ²(1, N = 556) = 1.190, p = 0.275; p. 676). Correct decisions rose over the session in both groups (p < 0.001), and the group with no information improved more (interaction p < 0.001; p. 676). The authors credit this to watching how the car handled the scenarios the participant did not interrupt (pp. 679–680).

Three observations carry the discussion. First, "even information from the system description that was directly replicated in driving scenarios was not straightforward for drivers to act on in practice" (p. 678). Several participants showed surprise when the car stopped at a traffic light the manual said it detects (p. 677). Second, participants who could state a rule still struggled: four knew the car missed objects under 30 cm, and three of them did not know whether the object in view was above or below that height; the authors write that "knowing the limitations of the vehicle may not be enough if critical information about a situation is not available" (pp. 677–678). Third, in the safety-critical pedestrian scenario "all 19 participants (67.9%) that actually took back control were too late to intervene" (p. 678). The authors recommend real-time in-car support tuned to driver and situation, which they did not test (p. 679).

## Relation to the argument

Supports P9 and the weak form of D3: a document that tells people what a system does and cannot do did not improve their decisions about the system. It also fits the author's "disclosure is a literacy-based intervention" reading, since the intervention is a text the driver must reduce to the case in front of them. Two cautions. The study never measured mental models directly; the knowledge test went to group 2 only (p. 679). And the contrast it implies (an artefact that carries the judgement) is the authors' recommendation, not a result. Their closing call for "in-car support" is the nearest thing here to P5's re-representation move.

The learning curve bears on D7 ("we understand through deciding and quickly ingesting the results"). Experience with feedback did more than the manual. The setting is a fixed system with unequivocal visible outcomes, so it does not touch the Part 1 worry that an adaptive intermediary degrades such feedback.

Facet check: facet H says the "mental models did not change." The authors write that the mental models "did not seem to (sufficiently) change" (abstract), inferred from decision accuracy and comments; they did not elicit the models. The facet's "28 participants, null" stands. I add that group 2 was nominally higher and that the information group learned less over the session.

## Caution

A video simulation with no physical risk; the authors say this likely explains late reactions (p. 679). Participants were mostly highly educated (15 of 28 held a master's or higher). The text is inconsistent on the scenario count: 23 scenarios, 8 requiring take-over, and 15 (p. 672) or 13 (p. 673) not; Table 1 lists 21. A software fault skipped some scenarios, so participants saw different numbers. With 14 per group, the null is a failure to detect, not evidence of no effect. The information was a long technical description of a hypothetical car, which the authors call mainly "structural" and demanding of reasoning (p. 672); it is one kind of disclosure, and a shorter, scene-based statement of limits is a different treatment that this study did not test.
