## What it argues

Jin and Leslie ask whether more quality information for consumers makes firms raise quality. They answer yes, in a setting chosen because the disclosure was mandatory and in a "standard format" (p. 4).

Findings:
- **Scores.** Mandatory grade cards raised inspection scores by 4.40 points, or 5.3 percent, about 0.4 of a standard deviation; voluntary posting raised them by 3.25 points (3.9 percent) (p. 11).
- **Demand.** Before the cards, "restaurant revenue is unaffected by changes in hygiene quality"; with mandatory cards an A-grade restaurant's revenue rose 5.7 percent, a B-grade's 0.7 percent, and a C-grade's fell about 1 percent (p. 14).
- **Inspectors.** Scores spike at 90, the A threshold, and the authors call inspector leniency the "undoubtedly more plausible" explanation (p. 17).
- **Health.** Mandatory cards cut food-related hospitalisations by about 20 percent and voluntary posting by 13 percent, with no fall in other digestive admissions (p. 21). The decline "is not fully explained by consumers switching from bad to good hygiene restaurants" (p. 27), and building-structure violations, which are harder for an inspector to overlook, fell by 19 percent (p. 27, n. 68).

Their conclusion: "the increased provision of product quality information to consumers causes firms to improve their product quality" (p. 27). They flag that the spike "supports the concern that grade cards induce grade inflation" and that inspector behaviour "might undermine the usefulness of the grade cards in the longer term" (p. 28). On why restaurants had not posted scores before: "It seems remarkable that simply providing a standard-format for disclosure, with no change in inspections or closure criteria, would be sufficient to change the equilibrium from zero disclosure and low hygiene quality, to high hygiene quality with potentially full disclosure" (p. 29). The clause "with no change in inspections or closure criteria" sits uneasily with Ho (2012, pp. 599–601), who reports more inspectors and a closure crackdown around the reform.

## Relation to the argument

**Line 164 and the paper's theoretical claim at line 500.** This is the study behind the paper's example of a disclosure that works. It supports the draft's mechanism: the effect runs largely through the discloser, which improves hygiene (pp. 21–28). It does not support the idea that consumers ignore the card; demand responds (p. 14). The card works on the discloser because diners read it.

**Ruling D3 and premise P8.** A one-letter grade in a window is the paper's Target 3 or simpler: a summary signal at the point of decision. The study is evidence that such signals can change outcomes, consistent with the draft's distinction between Target 4 and simple signals. It is not evidence that the letter works as a "perceptual" rather than an informational signal; the authors' mechanism is reduced search cost and an end to information asymmetry (pp. 3–5).

**Where it is contested.** Ho (2012) questions the hospitalisation result (small counts, a possible pre-existing decline, poor comparability of the rest of California) and finds grade inflation and noise elsewhere (`ho2012fudging`, pp. 597–598). Dranove and Jin (2010) summarise this study as finding the decline "largely because restaurants and consumers preparing meals at home improved hygiene" (`dranove2010quality`, §III.4).

## Caution

One county, one year after a television exposé that also triggered more inspectors, more closures and a new state food-temperature law (Ho 2012, pp. 599–601); the authors cannot separate grading from that package. Hospitalisations are a small, noisy count. The working paper may differ in detail from the journal article; check numbers against the QJE version before citing pages.
