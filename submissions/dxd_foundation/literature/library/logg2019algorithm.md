## What it argues

Logg, Minson and Moore find that "lay people adhere more to advice when they think it comes from an algorithm than from a person" (p. 2). Identical numeric advice, labelled either as an algorithm's output or as other people's estimates, drew more weight when labelled algorithmic: WOA .45 against .30 for a weight estimate (p. 13), .37 against .21 for song-rank forecasts (p. 17), and .38 against .26 for predictions of romantic attraction (p. 18). Researchers asked to predict Exp. 1C expected the opposite (p. 20). Given a choice, 75% chose the algorithm over another participant (39 vs 13; p. 23), and in a replication of Dietvorst et al.'s control condition 88% chose the algorithm over another person and 66% over themselves (p. 26).

Appreciation had limits. It fell when the alternative was the participant's own judgment (p. 26) and among experts: "experienced professionals, who make forecasts on a regular basis, relied less on algorithmic advice than lay people did, which hurt their accuracy" (p. 2; Exp. 4, pp. 31–34). Even lay people underweighted the algorithm relative to a normative benchmark (p. 37). The authors reconcile their results with Dietvorst et al.: "participants were quite willing to rely on algorithmic advice before seeing the algorithm err" (p. 36). They end on a warning: because people rely on systems they do not understand, "we all ought to be concerned about our vulnerability to manipulation" (p. 41).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D3 and premise P8 (disclosure as a literacy intervention).** The study's participants were given no account of how the algorithm worked, and lay people still leaned on it more than on people. That supports the author's claim that disclosure is not what drives use: reliance on a "black box" algorithm was high without any explanation (p. 14).

**Premise P10, Convention B.** It complicates the sandbox design. A pre-commit display that reports the mediator's own estimate of a move's outcome (line 385, "P(Match) = 88%") is algorithmic advice from an interested party. Lay users weight such advice more than human advice; experts discount it. The convention would need to say who computes the number and how users are protected from over-weighting a mediator's self-report, which is the manipulation risk the authors name.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** This paper is not cited in DRAFT_v2.md (grep "Logg", "Minson", "appreciation": no hits). The lines below are where the draft makes claims this source bears on.
- Line 283 ("The user is left relying on folk theories and defensive gaming"): narrows. Where people get an algorithm's output without performance history, lay users tend to rely on it, not on folk theories (pp. 13–26); this holds for advice, not for a mediator whose rules they must infer.
- Line 368 and the diagram at line 385 (sandbox reporting the mediator's boundary reaction and match probability): complicates. Mediator-labelled estimates attract more weight from lay users and less from experts (pp. 2, 31–34).
- Line 242 (AI literacy as conceptual knowledge): supports weakly and indirectly. Self-reported familiarity with algorithms (one item, Exp. 4) did not raise reliance: participants who claimed greater familiarity took less advice overall (p. 31). This is a self-report of familiarity, not a measure of conceptual knowledge.

## Caution

A working-paper version. In the benchmark study (p. 37), algorithmic-advice WOA is M = .34 (SD = .34). The later "M = .66, SD = .34" for the failure to weight the algorithm at 100% reads as the shortfall (1 − .34), not as a second WOA. Human-advice WOA is given as .24 and, two sentences later, as .26 (p. 37). Check the published version before using any number from this study. Advice is given once, with no repeated feedback; the advisor is not a mediator acting on the person. Several analyses are flagged by the authors as not pre-registered.
