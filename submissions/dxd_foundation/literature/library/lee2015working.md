## What it argues

Lee and colleagues coin "algorithmic management" for "software algorithms that assume managerial functions and surrounding institutional devices that support algorithms in practice" (p. 1603). They study three features: proximity-based assignment, the in-app display of surge areas, and evaluation by acceptance rate and ratings (pp. 1604–1605).

On assignment, drivers could not choose passengers and could reject only passively, by letting a 15-second window lapse (p. 1604). Whether a request "made sense" shaped cooperation; drivers rejected distant requests they read as system errors (p. 1606). Companies communicated "only general rules of assignment, e.g., 'the closest drivers get assigned,' and this general understanding helped drivers create their work strategies" (p. 1607). Under the heading "More knowledge more advantage", a driver who knew that Lyft widened the pickup radius over time turned the app off and on at traffic lights to avoid distant requests; drivers without that knowledge could not (p. 1607). Surge information failed for more than half the drivers: it "changed too rapidly and unexpectedly to utilize the information in a strategic way", sometimes "by the second" (p. 1607).

Forums were drivers' "primary knowledge sources and places for socialization" (p. 1605). Forum sensemaking worked for ratings advice (p. 1608) but was "less successful in terms of informational usefulness" for assignment and surge; when questions went beyond what the company had said, replies gave "social and emotional support, rather than informational support" (p. 1609). The authors call this "fragmented social sensemaking" with "no central authority figure" (p. 1611). Transparency, they argue, could raise cooperation and help workers build strategies, but full disclosure may not be viable, since companies "may also desire a degree of user ignorance to prevent the system from being gamed" (p. 1610).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D7 and premise P9 (learning by deciding).** The paper supports learning a mediator through action and shared observation: drivers' strategies come from their own moves and from forums. It also complicates it in two ways. First, the general rule the company published was part of what drivers used (p. 1607). Second, collective sensemaking failed exactly where the mediator was fast and opaque (surge, assignment) and succeeded where outcomes were stable (ratings) (pp. 1608–1609). That is close to the condition in P9 that feedback be valid, and it is evidence that action alone does not teach a volatile mediator.

**Ruling D3.** The authors' own design recommendation is more transparency and explanations (pp. 1609–1610). The finding "More knowledge more advantage" (p. 1607) is a case where *knowing the rule* helped drivers navigate, which cuts against the strong form of D3 ("disclosure of the algorithm doesn't affect our ability to navigate it"). The knowledge in question was a specific, actionable rule (radius widens with wait time), not an explanation of the system. That distinction may be the one D3 needs.

**Ruling D8 and premise P12.** The company decides how much to disclose and may want "a degree of user ignorance" (p. 1610): the platform holds the convention.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:**
- Line 73: "…commits a binding allocation that neither party directly controls (Lee, Kusbit, Metsky, & Dabbish, 2015)." **Mostly supported.** Drivers "cannot choose or set preferences for specific passengers or rides" and can only let a request lapse (p. 1604); passengers request and are matched. "Binding" overstates: drivers could and did decline, at a cost to their acceptance rate (pp. 1605, 1608), and drivers could ignore requests from low-rated passengers (p. 1605). The rest of the sentence ("independent objective function (J_M) — such as platform profit maximization…") is the draft's model, not Lee et al.'s; the closest they come is surge pricing meant "to meet passenger demand and maximize the total number of transactions" (p. 1605).
- Line 266: "Ride-hail drivers do not learn dispatch and surge behavior from company documentation. They form working theories from what the system does in response to their own moves, and they trade those theories in online forums (Lee et al., 2015; …)." **Partly supported.** Supported: drivers built strategies from their understanding of assignment (pp. 1606–1607) and used forums as primary knowledge sources (p. 1605). Not supported as written: the company's general rule was one source of that understanding (p. 1607), and the paper reports that forum sensemaking about assignment and surge mostly failed to inform (p. 1609). "Trade those theories" fits ratings strategies better than dispatch or surge.

## Caution

A small qualitative sample of US drivers in 2014, recruited by flyers and online posts; the authors could not interview company staff (p. 1611). No measure of how well drivers' theories matched the algorithm. Platform features (passive rejection, surge display) have changed since.
