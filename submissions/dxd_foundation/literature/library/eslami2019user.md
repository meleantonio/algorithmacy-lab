# Eslami, M., Vaccaro, K., Lee, M. K., Elazari Bar On, A., Gilbert, E., & Karahalios, K. (2019). User attitudes towards algorithmic opacity and transparency in online reviewing platforms. In *Proceedings of the 2019 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (Paper 494, pp. 1–14). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300724

**Identifier:** doi:10.1145/3290605.3300724 · **Read depth:** full_text (the ACM-formatted PDF from the third author's site, 14 pp., "Paper 494"; read whole; locators are the paper's page numbers 1–14) · **Source-tier:** proceedings (CHI, peer-reviewed) · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** Study 1, qualitative and quantitative analysis of 458 Yelp forum posts by 242 users, 2010–2017, about the review-filtering algorithm; Study 2, interviews with 15 Yelp users (ages 18–84, nine women) in which a tool, ReVeal, showed them which of their reviews had been filtered (pp. 4–5); 150 users' stances coded (69 challengers, 61 defenders, 20 hybrid) and correlated with 13 engagement features (p. 10) · **Parties modeled:** two parties + third party (reviewers and business owners, with Yelp's filter between them) · **Facets:** U · **Read:** 2026-10-15

## What it argues
Yelp hides both the operation and, for a logged-in author, the existence of its review filter (p. 3). Yelp's stated reason for opacity "is a design choice aimed to prevent malicious gaming" (p. 2, citing Yelp's blog). Users split: 24 questioned the hidden existence, 33 questioned the filter's existence, 32 defended it; 19 questioned the opacity of its operation and 4 defended that opacity, 60 questioned and 23 defended the operation (Table 1, p. 5). Defenders repeated the platform's gaming rationale: publishing the factors "would be like putting a security system on your house and then posting the PIN on the front door" (O44, p. 8). Users built folk theories (n = 87), chiefly a "reviewer engagement theory" (n = 66) and a "review format theory" (n = 37), and acted on them, adding photos, length and friends (pp. 8–9). Engagement correlated with defending the algorithm (review count r = 0.460; elite status 0.586; business owner −0.227; p. 11, Table 2).

Only three of fifteen interviewees knew of the filter. After ReVeal, six said they would "write for the algorithm as my audience" (P3) and about seven said they would use Yelp less or leave (p. 11). The discussion: "no user defended the opacity of the very existence of the algorithm" (p. 12); transparency in operation "affects users' usage behavior" and carries "the potential for malicious users gaming the system" (p. 12); and transparency in *impact* (showing a business its rating with and without filtered reviews) could answer the advertising-bias suspicion, since prior work found "no significant difference" in filtering between advertisers and non-advertisers (p. 12, citing Luca & Zervas).

## Relation to the argument
**P8 / D3 — supports the sentence.** When the filter was disclosed, the reported response was to write for it (six of fifteen) or to exit (seven of fifteen). Disclosure did not spare reviewers from the strategic task; it created it. Note the limit: intentions, not behaviour.

**Q3 (gaming as a coordination cost).** Reviewers write to be read by other users and business owners write to attract customers; the filter stands between them. The business owners' complaints about filtered positive reviews (p. 9) and the reviewers' "why did I even write a review then?" (P15, p. 6) are both costs of reaching the other party through the mediator.

**P9.** Folk theories came from comparing filtered and unfiltered reviews and from forum talk, a social form of learning by acting; the authors call the forums "a collective audit" (p. 12).

## Caution
Forum discussants self-select; 15 interviewees recruited partly after Yelp stopped the authors' first recruitment; the behaviour change is self-reported intention ("A longitudinal study should investigate", p. 11). The paper cannot say whether writing for the algorithm worked.
