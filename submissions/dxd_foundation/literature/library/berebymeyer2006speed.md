## What it argues

The paper imports partial reinforcement from psychology into games: "adding randomness to the link between an action and its consequences while holding expected payoffs constant" slows learning (p. 1029). In the probabilistic condition each cell of the matrix was a probability of winning $1 rather than a sure number of cents with the same expectation (p. 1033). The sunspot condition kept deterministic payoffs but showed the lottery draws, so that information sets matched the probabilistic condition (p. 1031).

Nonrepeated game: players learned to defect faster with deterministic payoffs. Both conditions ended near 10% cooperation, but "in the deterministic condition it took participants only 20 trials to reach this level of cooperation, while in the probabilistic condition it took them 180 trials" (p. 1033). Sunspots tracked the deterministic condition.

Repeated game: with deterministic payoffs, period-1 cooperation rose from 0.61 in the first supergame to 0.93 in the last (t(146) = 4.62, p < 0.001), and period-10 cooperation fell from 0.22 to 0.07 (pp. 1035–1037). With probabilistic payoffs "there was no significant change in cooperation in the first period, from the first to the last supergame (0.4 to 0.48)," and "the total level of cooperation never rises above 60 percent" (p. 1037). Players conditioned on their own lottery: after mutual cooperation, losing the lottery cut the next-period cooperation probability from 0.83 to 0.68 (t(46) = 3.81, p < 0.001) even though the partner's action was fully observed (Table 1, p. 1038). In the sunspot condition they did not condition on the draws (p. 1038).

The interpretation: "a small change in the payoff environment, which changes the speed of individual learning, can have a large effect on collective behavior" (p. 1029), because "the game provides a feedback loop between what players learn and what there is to learn" (p. 1040). On designed markets: "Newly designed markets won't have any experienced players, and so their performance may depend critically on what they make it easy and fast and safe for participants to learn" (p. 1040).

## Relation to the argument

**Q4 of the facet, feedback when the environment contains another person.** This is the cleanest evidence the facet found that noise in *outcomes* defeats learning to coordinate even when the counterpart's *actions* are perfectly visible. Monitoring was noiseless; only the payoff was random (p. 1031). People still read their own lottery as information about the other (Table 1, p. 1038). For the triad the implication is direct: a mediator that adds variance to what a driver or seller receives, with the counterpart's choice unchanged, is on this evidence enough to stop the pair from learning to cooperate.

**P9 and the "unequivocal" condition.** Kahneman and Klein require feedback "both rapid and unequivocal." Here the feedback was rapid in every condition and equivocal in one; the equivocal condition is where learning failed. The paper supports reading the author's phrase with the weight on *ecological* (valid, low-noise) rather than on *immediacy*.

**P10, candidate conventions.** The authors' closing remark (p. 1040) is a market-design statement of the author's second convention: make it "easy and fast and safe" to learn. It is a conjecture in their text, not a tested design.

## Caution

Laboratory students, small stakes ($15 show-up plus earnings), binary lotteries. The repeated-game probabilistic condition had three sessions and started from lower first-period cooperation (0.40 against 0.61), which the authors address by comparing sessions at matched starting levels (fn. 18, p. 1035). The paper is about a fixed payoff rule with added noise; nothing in it is an adaptive mediator, and the extension to platforms is the project's.
