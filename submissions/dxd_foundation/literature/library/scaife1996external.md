## What it argues

Scaife and Rogers hold that "little is known about the cognitive value of any graphical representations" (abstract, p. 185). Studies of diagrams, animation and virtual reality test whether a format helps and say little about how (pp. 186–187). They call the mechanism-free style "black-box" (p. 187).

They propose "external cognition," the relation between external and internal representation, and abstract three properties of it (pp. 188–189):

- "Computational offloading. This refers to the extent to which differential external representations reduce the amount of cognitive effort required to solve informationally equivalent problems" (p. 188). Their example is Larkin and Simon's diagrams, from which solutions can be "read-off."
- "Re-representation. This refers to how different external representations, that have the same abstract structure, make problem-solving easier or more difficult" (p. 189). Their example is Zhang and Norman's multiplication in Roman against Arabic numerals (LXVII x X against 68 x 10).
- Graphical constraining: the layout limits which inferences a reader can draw (p. 189, after Stenning and colleagues).

The critique has three strands. (1) Reading Bauer and Johnson-Laird's results, the authors argue the diagram plus instructions "rerepresented the original deduction problems into simpler and different tasks" (p. 195). (2) They name a "resemblance fallacy," the assumption that a picture helps because it resembles an image or mental model (pp. 199–201). (3) They stress expertise. Larkin and Simon's "perceptual inferences" may seem easy because they are well practised (Koedinger and Anderson, p. 195), and "A circuit diagram, an architectural plan or a mathematical notation comprise a set of meaningless symbols to the uninitiated; they only take on their intended meaning through learning the conventions associated with them" (p. 199).

On Zhang and Norman's Tower of Hanoi, fewer rules to internalize meant easier performance, which the authors read as offloading by embedding rules in the display (pp. 202–203). They add that people who learn to produce diagrams read new ones better (p. 208), and that annotating a diagram ("cognitive tracing") moves load outward (p. 206). In the discussion they say the "presumed benefits" of static diagrams "were considered to be due to years of practice of perceptual processing of visual stimuli and the learning of graphical conventions," and that "we have even less understanding of how (and if) computational offloading works in such dynamic environments" (p. 210).

## Relation to the argument

Premise P5 and ruling D6/D10 (a convention bundle, history as a guide). The vocabulary fits: the Roman/Arabic example is the plainest case in the source of one task, two external forms, and a difference in effort, which is the shape the paper gives the scribes' move. "Re-representation" describes what the page does; "offloading" describes what the reader is spared.

The source also limits the claim. Its theme is that gains from a good representation rest on practice with its conventions, which cuts against any reading of spacing as needing no training; it agrees with the dropped claim list ("'Reading no longer required the training'"). ## Caution

A 1996 critique of diagram, animation and virtual-reality research, with no data and no treatment of writing systems. The concept list is the authors' proposal, not a tested model. Its examples are physics, geometry, logic and mechanics problems with student subjects. It never discusses word separation, alphabetic script or reading.
