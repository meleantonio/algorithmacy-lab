## What it argues
AR is "a prominent and prospective tool for aiding human operators to understand robots' intentions and interact with them", and it "is expected to fill this gap and solve the 'human in the loop' integration problem by augmenting information properly into the operators' field of view and acquiring its inputs to the cyber-physical system" (p. 3). Of the 32 papers, 78% used collaborative robots (p. 22); 27 applications were assembly, 5 pick-and-place (p. 24); HMDs carried 59% of the systems and projectors 29%, with handhelds and fixed screens "fallen into disuse" since 2017 (p. 35); the automotive industry led (p. 24). By application (Table 13, p. 26), safety was the most frequent, then feedback, then guidance, then programming and quality control.

The safety features mostly inform: the holographic safety volumes and trajectory previews in the Greek automotive line "are for informational purposes only, meaning that the AR system does not detect whether the operator breaches the non-safe space" (p. 27); the authors sort 13 papers as "passive approaches" that "present only safety informational content" and 7 as "active approaches" that stop or slow the robot (p. 29), and note that "most of the above-mentioned AR safety functionalities are not certified" (p. 29). Guidance applications show "textual and 3D modeled holographic instructions" (p. 29); feedback applications show production status, cycle counts, robot state, and in two cases the operator's own mental load and gaze (Paletta et al.) or ergonomics (Andronas et al.) (p. 31).

Research type was 43.75% experimental, 43.75% case study and 12.5% concept; "most of the experimental research found is still being tested on a laboratory scale, not yet reaching real production sites" (p. 38). Evaluation used SUS and NASA-TLX in 26.66% of works each, customised questionnaires in 40%, task time in 69.23% of quantitative works (p. 38). Reported effects: Michalos et al. cut production cycle time by "almost 36%"; Papanastasiou et al. raised operator saturation from 88% to 97% and cut cycle time from 45 s to 42 s (p. 40); Hietanen et al.'s projector and HMD both beat a no-assistance baseline, but their HMD was rated "the unsafest by a clear margin" and "the most difficult to understand" (p. 41); Paletta et al. found AR "implied a slight increase in completion time" (pp. 39–40); Kalpagam et al.'s projection beat mobile and printed conditions on completion and errors (p. 40). The conclusion restates the frame: "The capacity of augmented reality to display real-time information to the user field of view is essential to insert the human in the loop" (p. 45), and guidance works by "abstracting the user of the necessity of memorizing numerous instructions ... lowering their cognitive load, and enabling an untrained operator to be able to conduct any operation if needed" (p. 45).

## Relation to the argument
This is the factory-headset half of the abstract's second sentence, stated by the field itself: the headset's purpose is to "display real-time information to the user field of view", and inserting the human in the loop means showing the human things. The review supports the abstract's claim that the design remains read-to-check: most safety features are informational and uncertified, and the operator is the one who must see the red volume and keep out. It also supports the narrower reading that the content is instruction and warning more than checking: guidance tells the worker what to do next; only the trajectory previews and digital twins let the worker check what the robot will do.

Two findings complicate the author's position. The guidance applications claim cognitive offloading, "abstracting the user of the necessity of memorizing" (p. 45), which is offloading of the kind the abstract wants, achieved by presentation inside the dyad; and the HMD that showed the most information was rated the least safe and hardest to understand (p. 41), which is Bainbridge's "most compatible display" irony in 2020. The parties are operator and robot with a shared task; no third party has an objective, so the review supports the dyad claim for this field.

## Caution
Thirty-two papers, many from one Greek group whose works are "extensions of the same work" (p. 23); most results are laboratory; the percentages are the authors' codings; effect reports are quoted from primary studies I did not open. The review's "cooperation" and "collaboration" labels follow ISO 10218 definitions. No bearing on platforms or mediators.
