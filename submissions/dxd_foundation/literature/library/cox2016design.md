## What it argues
The authors define "design frictions" as "points of difficulty encountered during users' interaction with a technology," note that the "standard argument is that they should be removed" to reduce disengagement, and say that "usability best practice follows this line of thought" (Introduction). Their case is that "frictions that are designed with intention, and introduced with care" can make interaction "reflective, informed and safe" (Mindful everyday interactions). Removing friction, they argue, produces "mindless forms of interaction," with two named costs: speed-accuracy trade-offs that cause errors, and responding to cues (email notifications) against one's own values (Introduction).

The mechanism is Kahneman's two systems. A *microboundary* is "an intervention that provides a small obstacle prior to an interaction that prevents us rushing from one context to another," implemented as "a short time cost" and prompting a switch from System 1 to System 2; the metaphor is "a credit card in a block of ice" (Microboundaries). They contrast it with dark patterns, which "nudge users into behaviours which are not necessarily desired, by making sure the users do not leave System1" (Microboundaries).

Evidence offered, all from other studies and none re-analysed:
- Soboczenski et al. (2013): participants copying numbers made fewer errors from a degraded font with "no measurable difference in task completion metrics" (so not a speed-accuracy effect), which the authors read as a more deliberate strategy (Mindful everyday interactions).
- Food diaries lead to weight loss, more so when logged within 15 minutes of eating, and even photographing food works; automated wearable capture would remove the effortful step (Design Frictions to support behavior change).
- Knowledge workers in the authors' own earlier interviews (Cecchinato et al.) used separate email apps on phones to avoid answering work email on Saturday night (Design Frictions to support value-led behaviour).
- Task lockouts improve attentiveness, but if long "people will instead use the time to work on other tasks," so length must be controlled (Design Frictions to increase data quality).
No N, effect size or confidence interval appears for any cited result.

Conclusion and future work: "small barriers that are relatively easy to overcome can lead to large impacts"; the authors "urge designers to consider the trade-offs between facilitating efficient everyday interface interactions and making people more mindful." Open questions: habituation to microboundaries, and "how the level of autonomy a user has over the introduction of the microboundary affects both its effectiveness but also engagement and retention."

## Relation to the argument
This is the counter-case source for the principle behind the paper's word-separation precedent: "take the work off the person" cannot be a general principle, because removing effort can remove care. It speaks to counter-case objection 8 and to the paper's rulings D3 and D7, where reading and tuning are replaced by deciding and ingesting results: friction is the position that understanding comes through a cost the user pays. It gives the author a stated opponent (usability best practice) and a reply (microboundary), at argument strength only.

It does not touch the historical precedent: nothing in it concerns reading or scribes. Its mechanism requires the user to want to be more careful; it says nothing about a mediator with interests of its own. Its point about who controls the friction ("the level of autonomy a user has over the introduction of the microboundary") is listed as untested, which matters for P10: a friction imposed by the platform owner is the dark-pattern case the authors say they are not describing.

Facet check: the facet's two quotations ("The standard argument is that they [frictions] should be removed from technology in order to reduce the risk of user disengagement"; "The problem with this design approach is that it can also result in mindless forms of interaction that can have negative consequences") are both in the Introduction of the accepted text and match, apart from the bracketed gloss, which is the facet's. The facet's page, 1390, is the proceedings pagination, which this accepted version does not show.

## Caution
A nine-page extended abstract (pp. 1389–1397) with no data of its own; the supporting studies are summarised in a sentence each. The scenarios (data entry, food logging, email) are not mediated triads; the authors say nothing about disagreement between parties. "Mindful" is defined as "deliberate and intentional," not as mindfulness practice. Accepted version, not the version of record.
