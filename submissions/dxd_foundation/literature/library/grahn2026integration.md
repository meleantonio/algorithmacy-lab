## What it argues

The review asks how human–machine interaction can be kept human-centric as it deepens into integration. The claim, in the authors' words, is that classical automation models are scaffolding to be extended: they "were developed for supervisory control settings in which a human operator, clearly separated from the machine, allocates functions and monitors discrete levels of automation through external displays," and those assumptions "become strained at deeper levels of integration" (p. 2). In Grant and Booth's typology the article is "a narrative critical review rather than a systematic review" (p. 3). Selection was purposive. The authors say a specialist should not expect the review to advance the state of the art inside one subfield (p. 3).

They order four levels by the cost of exiting the coupling, not by physical proximity (Section 2, pp. 3–5; Table 1; Figure 1). Level 1 is external tools and interfaces: exit is stepping away from a screen or console, and the design focus is that "outputs are transparent and interpretable to human operators," with trust calibration (Table 1, p. 5). Level 2 is wearables and near-body systems. Level 3 is embedded or neural interfaces. At Level 3, "in a bidirectional neuroadaptive interface there is no discrete level of automation to select, no external display through which oversight is exercised, and no stable boundary at which the human ends and the machine begins" (p. 7). Level 4 is environmental and societal-scale integration, split by structural exit cost (Table 2, p. 8). Level 4a is discretionary ambient AI (a person can decline it and still work). Level 4b is institutionally mandated AI: "algorithmic hiring and performance management, work allocation on labor platforms, credit and insurance scoring, algorithmic scheduling"; the person "cannot decline the system without forgoing an economic or social role" (Table 2). Level 4c is infrastructural AI, where "no non-algorithmic pathway remains within the polity," and "the classical assumption of a supervisory operator standing outside the system is least tenable" (p. 8).

Section 3 sets three scenarios along Raisch and Krakowski's automation–augmentation paradox: competitive displacement, collaborative synergy, and full integration (pp. 9–12). On collaboration they write that "current human–AI collaboration remains far less collaborative than the rhetoric suggests, with most deployed systems still following asymmetric patterns in which the AI proposes and the human merely approves" (p. 10).

Section 4 states five principles (Table 3, p. 13): preserve human agency and rights; clearly define human benefit; ensure inclusive technology and social cohesion; guide evolution through foresight and adaptive policy; reform education. Of twenty cells in Figure 4, twelve are marked established, five transferred, and three new (p. 13). One transferred cell is "civic AI literacy with participatory competence (education at Level 4)." Agency is glossed through Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven: tracing conditions and tracking conditions, plus "human override," "opt-out rights," and "human authorization of critical AI decisions" (p. 13). Floridi et al.'s explicability is cited under the benefit principle (p. 14). The discussion treats explainable AI as "essential for maintaining the principle of human agency" (p. 16).

Section 5 asks four communities for one reporting or regulatory addition each: pacing authority for worn systems, a deployer-facing non-coercion clause, an alternative-availability test beside risk-based regulation, and exit cost reported as a design variable (pp. 15–16). The conclusion asks, of any system, whether it advances welfare and rights and whether it preserves agency and dignity (p. 18).

## Relation to the argument

This is the 2026 field review the essay has to meet before it argues with Nadella. It supports three claims and cuts against a fourth.

It supports the claim that the standing human-factors model is a separated operator reading a display. Grahn et al. say so about Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens, and about Endsley (p. 2). Nadella's "tamper-proof human readable evidence" and authorized brake are that model, written for the deploying firm.

It supports placing platform work in a class where exit is no longer the person's. Level 4b names "work allocation on labor platforms" (Table 2). That is the triad's setting. The essay's inference, not the review's sentence, is that a rider and a driver are in 4b: they cannot decline the allocator without declining the role.

It supports the claim that the field's own remedy is still something a person takes in or authorizes. Table 1 asks for interpretable outputs. The education row names civic AI literacy at Level 4. The discussion asks for explainable AI so that agency can be maintained (p. 16). The asymmetric pattern they criticize is "the AI proposes and the human merely approves" (p. 10), which is still a reader of a proposal.

It does not support treating integration, neurorights, or exit-cost reporting as the smoking gun. Those change governance and what gets reported. They do not take a calculation off the person in the triad and show, on the surface, that it is gone. The essay's inference: the review sees that the external display fails at Level 3 and is least tenable at Level 4c, and its substitute is still oversight, explicability, and education.
