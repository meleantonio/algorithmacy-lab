## What it argues

Lee and colleagues extend Rich and Gureckis's (2018) "learning trap" to a world whose rule changes. Learners approached or avoided cartoon bees whose danger was set by a conjunction of two of three features; approaching a friendly bee gained 1 point and a dangerous one cost 3 (p. 2320). After block 4 the dangerous combination switched on one relevant dimension, without warning (p. 2320). The prediction was that people already using a one-dimensional rule that ignored the changed dimension would not see the change.

That is what happened. In Exp. 1 the contingent-feedback group often fell into a one-dimensional trap before the change; afterwards, "Most participants using a 1D unattended rule in learning phase 1 (19 out of 21) failed to learn the new rule and continued to use the same 1D rule" (p. 2321), and "no one in the 1D unattended sub-group noticed the rule change" (p. 2322). Participants on the correct two-dimensional rule before the change were 8 times more likely to learn the new rule than the 1D-unattended group (OR = 8.00, 95% CI [1.61, 39.69]) (p. 2321). With full feedback, over 60% eventually learned the new rule (p. 2321).

Warning people did not rescue the trapped. In Exp. 2, instructions that the rule might change "at most ONCE" (p. 2322) raised the odds of learning the new rule among those already on the correct rule (OR = 14.25), but among the 1D-unattended group 93% stayed on their old rule, against 90% in the unsignalled condition (p. 2322). The authors conclude that "those who have fallen into an early learning trap are likely to be insensitive to environmental change" (p. 2323), and note that the one remedy reported so far is frequent "summative feedback" comparing current earnings with optimal earnings, a result "obtained using a static version of the learning traps task" (p. 2323). The text cites it as Lee et al. (2022); the reference list gives Lee, Li and Hayes (under review).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D7 and premise P9.** The draft defines algorithmacy as coordinating through a mediator "whose internal rules shift dynamically" (line 219) and expects people to track it by acting and observing. This paper is direct lab evidence about that combination: choice-contingent learning plus a shifting rule. It finds that learners who settled early on a simple rule did not detect a later change at all, and that telling them a change might come did nothing for them. This is a problem for the draft's mechanism of "dynamic recalibration" (line 263, diagram) and for its reliance on enacted sensemaking in a moving mediator. The source does not show that a mediator causes the trap; the trap came from the learner's own avoidance.

**Premise P10, Convention B.** Two findings bear on design. Full feedback on avoided options let most people relearn after a change, and summative feedback against an optimal benchmark is reported to help in a static task (whether it helps after a rule change is, in the authors' words, "an open question", p. 2323). Both are forms of feedback that reach beyond the moves the person chose, which fits the sandbox idea better than it fits "low latency".

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** This paper is not cited in DRAFT_v2.md. The "Lee et al." citations in the draft are Lee, Kusbit, Metsky and Dabbish (2015) and Lee et al. (2025), different works. The lines below are where the draft makes claims this source bears on.
- Line 219 (mediator "whose internal rules shift dynamically"): complicates. People who learn such a mediator by acting can be blind to its rule changes (p. 2322).
- Line 250 (understanding a mediator by acting through it): complicates, for the reason above.
- Lines 266–268 (continuous behavioural experimentation): complicates. Trapped learners stopped approaching the options that would have revealed the change (pp. 2321–2323).
- Line 281 (noise and delay remove the conditions for learning by acting): narrows. The change blindness arose with immediate, deterministic feedback; the missing ingredient was feedback on unchosen options.
- Line 368 (pre-commit sandboxes): supports by analogy (full-feedback relearning, p. 2321; summative feedback, p. 2323). Not tested in a mediated setting.

## Caution

A six-page conference paper with brief reporting: the per-condition N for Exp. 1 is given only as "equal numbers" of 214, and several results are shown only in figures. Simple artificial categories, small stakes, online participants. The later journal version (Lee, W.-J., Li, A. X., Lee, J. E., & Hayes, B. K., 2024, "Learning traps and change blindness in dynamic environments", *JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 50*(9), 1345–1360, doi:10.1037/xlm0001390; its abstract on OpenAlex reports N = 416) was not opened beyond the abstract; its numbers may differ from those above. No platform, counterpart or mediator objective is modelled.
