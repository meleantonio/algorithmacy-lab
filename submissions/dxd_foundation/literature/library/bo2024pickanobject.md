## What it argues
The authors start from the evidence that "Many participants use dominated strategies, thus distorting the market allocations" (p. 2) and from two limits of OSP: common rules such as TTC and stable rules "cannot be implemented via OSP mechanisms" (p. 2), and some OSP strategies require "extensive foresight" (p. 3, citing Pycia and Troyan). They conjecture that what helps people in OSP and in dynamic DA is "the simple mechanics of the equilibrium strategy, in which agents 'pick' the object they would like to have from a menu, as opposed to submitting a ranking" (p. 3). A pick-an-object (PAO) mechanism asks agents to pick from individualised menus; a pick may later be rejected and a smaller menu offered; "When the procedure ends, agents are assigned the last object they picked" (p. 3). The truthful behaviour contrasts as: OSP, "Wait until you can pick your best feasible object"; PAO, "Pick your best feasible object and wait to see if you can keep it" (p. 3). They characterise which rules PAO can sequentialise (monotonic discoverability; DA and TTC qualify) and which can be implemented truthfully in a robust equilibrium (Theorems 1–2, p. 4).

Experiment (Table 3, p. 31). Proportions of truthful strategies: TTC with cyclic priorities, Direct 35% against PAO 55% (p = 0.00); TTC with acyclic priorities, Direct 56%, PAO 67%, OSP 77% (all pairwise p = 0.00); SD, Direct 72%, PAO 75% (p = 0.47), OSP 98%. "OSP implementations lead to the highest truth-telling rates across the board" (p. 4). Within OSP TTC, Result 2 (p. 32) and Table 4 (p. 33): when the obviously dominant path consisted only of "clinching" actions, 93% were truthful (N = 836); when it required a "passing" action, 56% (N = 620), "not significantly different from truthful rates under direct TTC (p=0.94)". "This result strongly supports the strong OSP concept of Pycia and Troyan" (p. 5). On efficiency, OSP did not beat PAO (p. 5). The authors acknowledge the within-subject order: "We acknowledge the bias of our design in favor of the OSP treatment" (fn. 42, p. 31) and that non-truthful strategies can be mistaken for truthful ones in PAO (p. 29).

## Relation to the argument
The paper gives the abstract's "strategic offloading" its most concrete present-day form: the rule is re-sequenced so that the person's move is "pick your best feasible object", and the mechanism does the contingent work. That supports P10 in a narrower form than the abstract's. The 93% against 56% split inside the same OSP mechanism (p. 33) is the sharpest evidence in the facet that it is the shape of the person's decision, not the incentive label, that removes the task: where the obviously dominant move still required foresight, truth-telling fell to the direct-mechanism level. The authors' own gloss supports the abstract's representational line and cuts against a reading that OSP as such is the design change to look for. Two other points bear on the triad. First, PAO's other benefits are stated as information acquisition, perceived transparency and a shorter task in large markets (p. 5), each a claim about the person's experience, not the rule's incentives, and none tested here. Second, the mediator is still a neutral designer; nothing speaks to a mediator that optimises against the person.

## Caution
Read as a preprint and only in part; the theory and the design section were not read, so the participant total, payment and session details are not on this card. Rules were varied within subjects and OSP ran first, which the authors say favours OSP. The lab environments have four to eight objects; the paper's claims about large markets (p. 5) are argued, not tested. "Truthful" in PAO is inferred from a few observed picks and may be overstated (p. 29).
