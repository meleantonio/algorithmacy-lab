## What it argues

The paper argues that short notes anchored to the text of API documentation help both the person who writes them and later readers. Its case for annotations over forums is context: "annotations provide a tight coupling between the original source of information and the commentary on it, so the context is inherent in where the annotation is anchored" (p. 2). By contrast, a study of unanswered Stack Overflow questions found "a lack of clarity in the question due to lack of context" (p. 2).

Results of the main study (pp. 9–12). Readers of annotated documentation completed 2.5 of 4 steps on average, against 1.5 for control and 1.4 for authors, a difference the authors report as significant (p < .01 against each; pp. 8–9). That is the abstract's "67% more of the task" (p. 1). Authoring did not change the author's own task performance: 1.4 against 1.5, "two-tailed T-test, p = 0.78" (p. 11). Authors wrote 91 annotations, averaging 9.31 words (p. 11). Readers found 45% of the annotations they encountered helpful and 8% not helpful (p. 11). Answered questions were the most helpful type (54%, p. 11), explanations of code helped 63% of the time, and hypotheses 16% (p. 12). In the control condition only 2 of 10 participants left any artefact for a teammate (p. 9).

On context, the discussion says the least useful annotations "were the ones that lacked enough context to be reusable" (p. 13). Its example is a note on "columns 10" whose author knew why it mattered while the note did not say so: "the annotation is missing this full context" (p. 13). The authors propose that, if the tool were integrated with the IDE, it could capture the code and its output when a note is made, so that later readers can see "why they made the annotation" (p. 13). They also warn that annotations may go out of date when the API changes, "at which point they may be more harmful than helpful" (p. 13).

Limitations they state (p. 13): one 45-minute session; a small, poorly documented library; the researchers curated which annotations readers saw (31 kept of 91, plus one written by the first author because no participant reached the last step, p. 7). Removal was mostly for redundancy, not quality.

## Relation to the argument

**Premise P5 and ruling D7.** The study is about a person learning a tool by working with it and leaving notes at the point where they hit trouble, which fits the paper's picture of learning a mediator by acting on it rather than reading about it. The useful annotations were short (the best one was five words, "Use this to create rows", p. 12) and placed exactly where the task stalled. That fits the draft's preference for small aids at the point of use over long explanation.

**What it does not show.** The tool runs in a browser over documentation pages. IDE integration is future work (p. 14). The study measured transfer to *other* people (readers) and found no task-completion gain for the authors themselves (p = 0.78, p. 11), though the authors argue from revisits and self-explanation that authoring helped the annotator (pp. 11–12). There is no triad and no party with an interest in the outcome. The readers' gain is a gain from reading curated notes, which is closer to the draft's "literacy" side (D3) than to deciding.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 39: "In software development environments, annotation made at the point of use preserves context that is hard to reconstruct afterward (Horvath et al., 2022)." Partly supported, and partly cut against. Horvath et al. do say the anchor carries the context (p. 2). But their own example of the least useful annotations shows a note made at the point of use that did *not* preserve its author's context: "the annotation is missing this full context" (p. 13). Their remedy is to have the tool capture code and output automatically, in a future IDE integration (p. 13). The paper therefore supports "context matters and is easily lost", not "annotation at the point of use preserves it". They do not say that context is "hard to reconstruct afterward". Two further points. "Software development environments" is loose: the setting is documentation in a web browser, not the development environment, which the authors defer (p. 14). And the measured benefit went to other readers (2.5 against 1.5 steps, p. 9), not to the annotator (p = 0.78, p. 11).

## Caution

N = 30, with 10 per condition. The reading condition ran after the others, so assignment to it was not random (p. 8). The paper reports "paired T-test" for comparisons between separate groups of participants (p. 9), so the stated test is odd. The annotations were curated by the researchers. The setting is one small library. Read from the arXiv v2 copy, not the ACM PDF. The paper cannot be cited for annotation inside an IDE, for any benefit to the annotator's own task, or for "context that is hard to reconstruct afterward" as a finding.
