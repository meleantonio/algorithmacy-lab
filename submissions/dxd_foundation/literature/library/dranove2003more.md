## What it argues

Report cards may help patients choose and push providers to improve. They may also lead providers to "decline to treat more difficult, severely ill patients", because providers know more about a patient's risk than any risk adjustment can capture (abstract, pdf p. 2; Introduction, pdf pp. 3–5). The authors test both effects on the whole population at risk (AMI patients), not just those who received surgery.

Findings:
- **Selection.** Report cards were followed by a fall of 2.43 to 5.30 percent in the prior illness severity of CABG patients relative to other states, with no such fall among AMI patients (pdf p. 14). Earlier surveys had found that 63 percent of New York cardiac surgeons said they accepted only healthier candidates after report cards (Background, pdf p. 7, citing Schneider and Epstein 1996).
- **Matching.** Sicker AMI patients moved toward teaching hospitals, a sign of better sorting (pdf pp. 14–15).
- **Quantity.** The chance that an AMI patient received CABG within a year rose by 0.60 or 0.91 percentage points (base 13.1 percent), all among healthier patients; angioplasty fell by 1.69 or 1.22 points (base 12.43 percent); and all three procedures were delayed (pdf p. 16).
- **Outcomes.** Spending rose; among sicker patients, readmission for heart failure rose by about 2.3 points on a base of 9.4 percent, and in one specification mortality rose by 0.82 points (pdf p. 17).
- **Why earlier studies looked good.** Restricting to CABG patients reproduces the earlier finding of lower mortality; the authors attribute it to "a shift in incidence of CABG surgeries toward healthier patients, not because CABG report cards improved the outcomes of care for individuals with heart disease" (pdf p. 19).

They conclude that "at least in the short run, these report cards decreased patient and social welfare" (abstract, pdf p. 2). They add that "Caution should be exercised in interpreting our results too negatively": they measure only the first few years, and "our results do not imply that report cards are harmful in general", since report cards on an illness population rather than a procedure might reduce the room for selection (Conclusion, pdf p. 21).

## Relation to the argument

**Line 164.** The draft's working-disclosure mechanism is that the discloser "changes its conduct to avoid publishing a poor grade". This study shows that mechanism working and doing harm. Hospitals and surgeons changed conduct to protect their published number, by choosing whom to treat. A simple, published grade can act on the discloser in the wrong direction when the discloser controls who is measured.

**Premise P12 and the triad.** The structure is close to the paper's: the provider sits between the patient and the report card's audience and can shape the measured population. The report card is a disclosure about the intermediary that the intermediary can game. That supports the paper's broader claim that the interested party bends a convention, but it complicates the paper's positive example.

**Ruling D3.** This is not evidence on whether patients read report cards; it is evidence that a simple published signal changes what the measured party does, for better or worse.

## Caution

Short run only (at most four years of the programmes). Severity is proxied by prior-year hospital spending and days. The identifying assumptions are that report cards did not change who had an AMI and that NY and PA trends would otherwise have matched other states'; the authors test both partly. The working paper may differ from the JPE article, so check numbers against pp. 555–588 before citing.
