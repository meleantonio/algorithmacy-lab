# Chou, E., McConnell, M., Nagel, R., & Plott, C. R. (2009). The control of game form recognition in experiments: Understanding dominant strategy failures in a simple two person "guessing" game. *Experimental Economics, 12*(2), 159–179.

## What it argues

In the two-person guessing game each player picks a number from 0 to 100 and the one closer to two-thirds of the average wins; 0 weakly dominates. Earlier work read the widespread failure to choose 0 as a cognitive bias. The authors argue instead for "game form recognition": subjects have "an incomplete, inaccurate or perhaps absent grasp of the relationships between the choices made in the game and the incentives" (p. 1). At Caltech, 46% chose 0 without a hint and 87% with the hint "Notice how simple this is: the lower number will always win" (Table 4, p. 16; the text rounds to 50% and "close to 90%", p. 5). At the community college, none chose 0 without the hint and 7% with it (p. 5; Table 4). Simplifying the instructions, adding bullets, a figure, cash on display and Caltech t-shirts made little difference (5% chose 0; 32% within 5 of it with the hint; Table 5, p. 16). Recasting the same game as placing troops on a 100-foot hill, where the higher position wins, raised the share choosing the dominant strategy to 46%, with 65% within 5 of it (Table 4), "no changes in incentives, information or subject pool" (p. 6), a rate statistically indistinguishable from Caltech's no-hint play (p. 7). Result 4: changes did not move averages gradually; "they either perceive the game form correctly or do not" (Result 4, pp. 7–8). The battle frame brought its own distortions, as questionnaire answers show: "no oxygen at the peak of the hill" (Table 2B, p. 14). The authors conclude that "What might appear as a lack of subject rationality becomes a failure by the experimenter to implement appropriate controls" (p. 8).

## Relation to the argument

**P5 carried to a strategic setting (question 3 of this facet).** This is the cleanest representational effect in a game that the facet found: an isomorphic re-presentation moved dominant-strategy play from 0% to 46% in one subject pool. It supports the abstract's wager, in a two-person, no-mediator, classroom form.

**D3 (disclosure).** The hint is a disclosure of the strategic structure, one sentence long. It worked on subjects who already read instructions well (Caltech: 46% to 87%) and failed on those who did not (college: 0% to 7%). The representation change worked on the second group where the disclosure did not. That is the pattern the author's ruling predicts, and it is the facet's best single piece of evidence for it, with the caveat that the college sample was also inattentive ("I did not read", Table 2B).

**D6 and the "smoking gun."** Result 4's all-or-nothing shift is the shape the author is looking for: a design change that flips recognition rather than nudging it.

**Limits.** No mediator, no repeated play, no feedback. The "game" is a one-shot classroom exercise for $8.

## Caution

Working-paper text; the 2009 article may differ in numbers and wording. Cells are small (15–26 in most), the community-college sessions lost experimental control by the authors' own account, and the protocol changes confound several factors at once. Percentages for "chose 0" and "chose ≤ 5" differ; cite the table and the measure.
