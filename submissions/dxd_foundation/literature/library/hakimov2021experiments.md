## What it argues
On the founding result: Chen and Sönmez's finding that truth-telling under BOS is lower than under DA or TTC "was replicated in almost all subsequent studies", while their DA > TTC ordering "is not robust" (p. 444). Information about others' preferences lowered truth-telling in all mechanisms in Pais and Pintér (2008), so "truthful revelation in the incomplete information environment represents an upper bound"; there "The truth-telling rate in DA was just 66.7%" (p. 445). Guillen and Hakimov (2017): 85% truthful under TTC when told others were truthful, "Only 31% of subjects were truthful in both decisions" once told others might not be, which "demonstrates that subjects do not perceive truth-telling as a dominant strategy but are influenced by the behavior of others" (p. 448). Guillen and Veszteg (2019) found much higher "rational" play in DA and TTC than in reversed versions where the dominant strategy is to list in reverse, suggesting some subjects "play the dominant strategy of truthful reporting as a default strategy, without understanding the incentive property" (p. 448). Summary: "the sensitivity of the rates of truthful reporting to the information provided and to the market environment raises concerns regarding the successful implementation of these mechanisms in practice" (p. 449). Learning: truth-telling under repeated DA rose from 65 to 77% over 20 rounds in Ding and Schotter (2019) and from 38% to 56% in Bó and Hakimov (2020a); longer lists go with lower truth-telling (pp. 449–451). Advice: in Guillen and Hing (2014) third-party advice cut truth-telling under TTC from above 70% to 50% (correct advice), 28% (wrong) and 42% (both); in Guillen and Hakimov (2018) an instructor's advice raised it from 81 to 94% while "the disclosure of the mechanism reduced the rate of truth-telling among a subsample" (pp. 453–454); Koutout et al. (2018) found advice to be truthful raised DA truth-telling by 19 points (p. 454). Dynamic mechanisms: iterative DA raised truth-telling against static DA in Bó and Hakimov (2020a) but not significantly in Klijn et al. (2019) (55% against 47%); the authors conclude the advantage of dynamic DA "is the feedback it provides after every step... and the possibility to re-strategize given this feedback" (p. 468); posting tentative cut-offs after each step raised truthful behaviour further (p. 469); Stephenson (2016) found continuous feedback on the would-be allocation raised stable outcomes from 83% to "almost 99% of markets" (p. 469); "The experimental evidence weakly favors dynamic mechanisms over their direct counterparts" (p. 470). On OSP: Li's sequential RSD result is "explained by obvious strategy-proofness", and "An empirical test of the predictions of OSP versus strong OSP has yet to be conducted" (p. 477). Conclusion: "the robust finding that people often do not play their dominant strategy under DA challenges the concept of implementation in dominant strategies", and "market design is needed to come up with mechanisms that are simpler in the sense that more people understand that truth-telling is optimal" (pp. 479–480).

## Relation to the argument
The survey ties the two halves of the abstract's last sentence together from the matching side. On "strategic offloading": the field's own summary is that strategy-proofness does not deliver truth-telling reliably, that the gains of dynamic mechanisms come through step-by-step feedback and a chance to re-strategise (p. 468), and that OSP against strong OSP had not been tested as of 2021 (p. 477; `bo2024pickanobject` has since done so). On "ecological feedback immediacy": the Stephenson and cut-off results (p. 469) are the closest lab analogues the facet found to the author's second candidate convention, and they are reported as favouring it. On D3: the advice evidence is mixed by source and framing (pp. 453–454), and in one study disclosing the mechanism lowered truth-telling, which supports the ruling's spirit while showing that advice from a trusted source can help. The survey covers only settings where the mechanism has no objective of its own.
