# Crosetto, P., & Mantovani, M. (2018). Representation effects in the centipede game. *PLOS ONE, 13*(10), e0204422.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0204422 · **Read depth:** full_text (open-access journal PDF, 13 pp. in the article's own pagination; saved as `dxd_foundation_local/pdfs/V/crosetto2018.pdf`; read in full including Tables 1–2, the appendix instructions and references; Figures 1–4 read through captions) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** laboratory experiment, Jena, June 2012, N = 210 in 8 sessions (74 Tree, 72 Formula, 64 Decomposed), mostly students, mean age 23.5, 63% women; twelve-legged centipede game played 12 times with perfect-stranger matching; 1,260 games · **Parties modeled:** two parties, no third party · **Facets:** V · **Read:** 2026-10-15

## What it argues

Three presentations of one game: the standard tree with terminal payoffs; a verbal and algebraic formula (stopping at round r pays 4r to the stopper and r to the other); and a decomposed tree showing stage changes. "the same information is available to players in each treatment, although it is presented in different ways" (p. 2). The hypothesis is that less accessible payoff information "throw[s] sand in the gearbox of strategic thinking" (p. 2) and delays stopping. Mean endnode was 3.52 in Tree, 4.18 in Formula and 4.17 in Decomposed, "about 2/3 of an endnode later" (p. 5); Wilcoxon z = −5.6 for each comparison with Tree, no difference between the other two (Table 1, p. 6). OLS with session clustering gives 0.556 and 0.646 nodes (Table 2, p. 7). All treatments learned towards equilibrium at about one-fifth of a node per repetition, with no treatment difference in slope (p. 6). Controls for risk attitude, trust, perceived complexity and quiz errors did not change the estimates, and interactions of treatment with risk and trust were null, so "treatment differences are not mediated by preferences" (p. 8). The discussion contrasts Cox and James (2012), where a formula presented as a Dutch auction made subjects stop earlier, and concludes that behaviour "may react in a non-monotonic and discontinuous way to marginal increases in complexity" (p. 9).

## Relation to the argument

**Question 3 of this facet.** A second demonstration that the representation of a two-player game changes play when information is held constant. It supports the abstract's premise that representation does strategic work, in a narrow form: a small shift (two-thirds of a node on a twelve-node game), in a lab, between two students, with no mediator.

**Cuts against design optimism.** The authors' own comparison with Cox and James shows the sign of a representational change depends on the baseline game and frame. For the author's "smoking gun" search this is a caution: the same manipulation can move behaviour either way, and the paper offers no theory that predicts which.

**D7.** Learning with feedback was identical across presentations; the representation set the level, repetition set the slope. That separates the two mechanisms the abstract joins, representation and feedback.

## Caution

A small effect from a manipulation that adds a step of arithmetic; it shows that removing a computed payoff hurts, not that adding one helps. Students in one German lab; perceived complexity measured on a five-point self-report the authors themselves distrust (p. 7). No field data, no mediator.
