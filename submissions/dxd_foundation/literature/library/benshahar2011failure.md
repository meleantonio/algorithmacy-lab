## What it argues
Ben-Shahar and Schneider call mandated disclosure "the single most common technique for protecting personal autonomy in modern society" and argue that it fails (abstract, p. 647). Their scope is narrow on purpose: they do not say "information never helps"; they test a technique "in which a lawmaker requires a discloser to give a disclosee a standard disclosure" (p. 651).

Part I catalogues the "Disclosure Empire" (pp. 652–665). Part II summarises the evidence of failure in three steps: "disclosees often do not read disclosed information, do not understand it when they read it, and do not use it even if they understand it. Third, mandated disclosure does not improve disclosees' decisions" (p. 665). Examples: roughly 1 in 1,000 users scrolls through online boilerplate, at a median of 29 seconds (p. 671); patients recall a third to a half of consent information (pp. 668, 719); only 38.5% of detainees achieving "good comprehension" of Miranda warnings at the easiest (below sixth-grade) reading level, a passage the authors quote from Rogers (p. 678). The authors concede "some moderate successes of disclosure regimes, particularly those that rely on ratings systems" (pp. 678–679).

Part III explains the failure through three actors. Lawmakers respond to "trouble stories" and expand mandates (pp. 679–691); disclosers misinterpret, pad or resist (pp. 691–704); disclosees face the "accumulation problem," illiteracy, innumeracy, memory limits and decision aversion (pp. 704–729). Two passages bear on this project. Expertise "comes with practice and cannot be taught simply by instruction" and rests on recognising situations as typical, quoting Klein's *Sources of Power* (pp. 725–726). And education, the usual companion of disclosure, "consistently disappointed" (p. 733). Part IV adds unintended harms: crowding out other information, anticompetitive fixed costs, weakened unconscionability protection, and inequity, since disclosure "helps most those who need help least" (pp. 737–742).

The conclusion floats three paths, each hedged. Very simple signals: "perhaps to the point of using symbols instead of sentences," with Los Angeles restaurant grade cards that "seem to have influenced consumers—and, in turn, led to cleanlier restaurants" (p. 743). Advice and aggregated ratings rather than data: people seek "not data, but advice" (p. 746). Channelling by defaults and plain-vanilla options, or outright prohibition (pp. 748–749). They warn that "history is not reassuring" about simplification (p. 744).

## Relation to the argument
**Ruling D3 and premise P8.** This is the broadest legal-empirical support for D3's claim that disclosure is a literacy-based intervention: the authors' whole chain (find, read, understand, remember, use) is a chain of reading tasks, and they list "Illiteracy and Innumeracy" as a cause of failure (pp. 711–713). The paper supports P8 strongly for standard-form disclosure to individuals. It is not about algorithms, platforms or triads, and its evidence is a literature survey, not new measurement.

**Ruling D7 and premise P9.** The expertise section is an unexpected ally of D7: the authors hold that good decisions in these areas come from practice, pattern recognition and "trial and error" (p. 726), not from information. That is close to the author's "we understand THROUGH deciding." The same section explains why disclosees, who face these decisions rarely, never get the practice; that sits with P9's condition that feedback must be rapid and repeated.

**Premise P10.** The "very brief, simple, and easy" signal (p. 743) and the shift "From Information Toward Advice" (p. 746) point toward conventions that re-represent rather than disclose. The authors hedge both. The restaurant case works partly through the discloser: "some warnings are focused on persuading disclosers to change their behavior" (p. 744), and the grades lead "some restaurants to try to get their grades up" (p. 748).

## Caution
A law-review argument that reads the evidence to make a case; the authors choose the examples and say they "survey the empirical literature" rather than review it systematically (p. 652). Many cited findings are from the 1970s–2000s and from US settings. Some statements are rhetorical (the "Chris Consumer" day, pp. 705–709). The paper does not test any alternative it proposes. My copy is an OCR text: quotations above were checked against it, but footnote citations should be checked against a clean copy before being repeated.
