## What it argues
The record of a mediator changing its own rule. "In July of 2005 the Boston School Committee... voted to replace the existing school choice mechanism... with an alternative mechanism that removes the incentives to 'game the system'" (p. 2), after the authors presented "theoretical, historical, and experimental evidence" in October 2003 (p. 2). BPS staff "wanted us to demonstrate this... in the Boston data" (p. 3). The paper shows two things: some families strategised, and others were hurt by not doing so. Of 391 elementary students who ranked two overdemanded schools first and second with only random priority at the first, 38% got their first choice, a third a lower choice, and 29% were unassigned (p. 17); "a majority of all unassigned students submitted a rank order with this property" (p. 18); 115 were unassigned at elementary level and 66 stayed in the system, so "it is likely these students are playing suboptimally" (p. 18). The Superintendent's May 2005 memo: "The most compelling argument for moving to a new algorithm is to enable families to list their true choices of schools without jeopardizing their chances"; the old rule "provides an advantage to families who have the time, resources and knowledge to conduct the necessary research"; "A strategy-proof algorithm levels the playing field by diminishing the harm done to parents who do not strategize or do not strategize well" (p. 24). Other stated benefits: "clear and straightforward advice to parents regarding how to rank schools", efficiency, and credible demand data, because under the old rule "we cannot make assumptions about where families truly wish to enroll" (pp. 24–25). DA was chosen over TTC partly because TTC "is less transparent– and therefore more difficult to explain to parents... which may perpetuate the need or perceived need to 'game the system'" (p. 26). The authors add that "The advantages of strategy proofness would be lost if it is not effectively communicated to the community" (p. 27), and list the September 2005 messages: families "do not have to (in fact, should not) 'strategize'" and "do not have to factor into their decision-making how many other families will choose a particular school" (p. 27). The conclusion: Boston "was like a patient with high blood pressure, a potentially deadly disease that has no easily visible symptoms" (p. 27); parents "resented having to strategize and that caused parents to lose trust" (p. 28).

## Relation to the argument
Question 4's clearest case: a mediator adopted a rule so that users would stop strategising, and said so in those words. Three points for the abstract. First, the "offloading" was explicitly a transfer of work: the memo's list tells families what they no longer need to compute (p. 27), which is the closest any source in the facet comes to the author's framing, though the word used is "strategize", not "offload". Second, the designers insisted the transfer would fail without communication (p. 27), which is why the later field studies (`hassidim2021limits`, `reesjones2018suboptimal`) matter: the communication was done and a residue remained. Third, the choice of DA over TTC on transparency grounds (p. 26) shows the mediator weighing how a rule would be understood, not only what it would do. The equity rationale (sophisticated families gain under a manipulable rule) is the "levelling" argument formalised by Pathak and Sönmez (2008, abstract only). Supports P10 as a historical instance; nothing here measures what happened to strategising after the switch.

## Caution
Working paper; the sections establishing strategic behaviour (§5.1–5.2) and the data (§4) were not read, so sample sizes beyond those quoted are not on this card. The authors cannot observe true preferences and say so (pp. 3, 28); the "unsophisticated" group is identified conservatively. No post-reform behavioural data. The designers were the authors, who also argued for the change.
