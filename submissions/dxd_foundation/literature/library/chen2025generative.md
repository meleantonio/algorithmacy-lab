## What it argues

The authors propose "generative interfaces": instead of replying with a block of text, the model writes a task-specific user interface (HTML, CSS and JavaScript) for each query. The pipeline turns the query into a requirement specification, then a structured representation (an interaction-flow graph plus finite state machines for components), then code, and refines the code over up to five rounds against a rubric the model writes for that query (§2, PDF pp. 3–4).

They compare it (GenUI, built on Claude 3.7) with chat baselines (GPT-4o or Claude 3.7, with Claude's own artifacts stripped out) and with "instructed UI" (Claude 3.7 told to solve the query with a UI). Annotators compare two interfaces on seven perception dimensions plus an overall choice (§3).

The headline result: against the Claude 3.7 chat baseline GenUI wins 84%, ties 4% and loses 12% overall (Table 1, PDF p. 6). Against GPT-4o it wins 69% to 30%. Against instructed UI it wins 75% to 17%. The abstract's "up to a 72% improvement in human preference" matches the 84% win minus the 12% loss in the Claude row; the text never defines it, and §4.2 uses the same arithmetic for "58.0% higher win rate" (75 − 17). So 72 is a margin in percentage points, not a ratio.

Preferences vary with the task. GenUI is preferred in 93.8% of Data Analysis and Visualization comparisons but only 50.0% in Advanced AI/ML Applications (Table 2, PDF p. 6); the text adds that chat remains competitive "in math-heavy contexts". Comments that mention cognitive load favour GenUI 78.5% of the time, while on easy how-to queries chat won because "additional tools impose unnecessary cognitive load" (§4.3, PDF pp. 7–8). In the real-user study GenUI's margin shrank to 50.8% win, 8.2% tie, 41.1% loss (§4.4, PDF p. 8), and 30.3% of participants preferred it in at least 80% of cases against 18.4% for chat. That study used Gemini-2.5-flash to produce both interfaces (App. H).

The limitations name latency "of up to several minutes", HTML-only front ends, and interface generation even when none is needed (PDF p. 9). The ethics section warns that "the polished, tool-like presentation of outputs may also increase user trust and lead to overconfidence" (PDF p. 9).

## Relation to the argument

This is an interface that the intermediary itself writes for each request. For the foundation paper it is an instance of the "structured and non-chat" family in facet H, and a counter-example for the word-separation precedent: spacing became a convention because the form stayed fixed across texts and readers, and GenUI's premise is that the form changes with every query. That reading is mine, not the authors'. It sharpens P7 and P10: the party that produces the content also produces, and can change, the display in which the person reads it. The authors' own ethics note concedes that presentation moves trust independently of correctness.

The evidence is on preference, not decisions. "Task efficiency" and "learnability" are annotator judgements of screenshots and live pages, not measured completion or error rates. The authors also report that Claude 3.7 as an automated judge agreed with human pairwise votes on 69.0%, and that length bias may favour GenUI (App. B). The 100 test queries were themselves generated by Claude 3.7 (§3.1), the same model family that drives GenUI. For P9 this is weak support: a richer artefact is liked, with no evidence it changes what people do.

Facet check: facet H lists it as abstract only and says "preference, not performance." That holds. The full text adds the definition of the 72%, the shrinking margin with real users, and the domain split.

## In conversation with

- `risko2016cognitive`: cited for cognitive offloading as the reason users preferred GenUI on complex tasks (§4.3, PDF pp. 7–8).
- No other source in the bib is cited. The facet H siblings (`suh2023sensecape`, `gero2024supporting`, `amin2026conversations`, `ueno2026viscanvas`) are not discussed. The related-work section names Graphologue (Jiang et al. 2023), OpenAI Canvas and Claude Artifacts as the nearest systems and contrasts them as "predesigned" UIs (PDF p. 9).
- Leads: Tamkin et al. 2024 (Clio, the domain taxonomy); Nielsen et al. 2012 and Hartmann et al. 2008 (the usability dimensions); Findlater and Gajos 2009 (adaptive interfaces and loss of predictability, which the authors cite on p. 9).

## Caution

A v3 preprint dated May 2026; earlier versions and the venue version may differ. The authors built the system and ran the evaluation, with a Claude model as backbone, as query writer, and as the automated judge behind the Table 5 scores and the refinement reward. Annotators were US Prolific workers; agreement was moderate. The study has no measure of whether the content was correct or whether people acted differently. Not usable as evidence that generated interfaces improve decisions, trust calibration or understanding.
