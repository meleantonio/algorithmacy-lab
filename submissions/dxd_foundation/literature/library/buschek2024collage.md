## What it argues
Buschek proposes "Collage" as an intermediate-level design concept, taken from avant-garde literary collage and applied to the interfaces of AI writing tools (Section 1). It has four facets: the interface fragments text across several views; the writer juxtaposes two voices, "diegetic" draft text and "non-diegetic" prompts; the text integrates material from several sources, the AI and its training data among them; and the writer's role moves "from manual writing towards making editorial and compositional decisions" (Section 3.5). He builds on Höök and Löwgren's "strong concepts" and on a distinction taken from Dang et al. for the voices (Sections 1, 3.2).

He uses the concept three ways. As an analytical lens, he scores the 36 designs and sorts them by a "Collage Factor" (the count of yes answers), from low (a single page with some assistance) to high (2D canvases, prompt grids), and extracts patterns of text display and text interaction (Section 4, Table 1, Figure 2). As a constructive lens, he generates design ideas by changing fragmentation, sources and voices (Figure 3), then works up two: keep a visible "seam" and an object for each accepted or rejected suggestion, which "reifies" the writer's editorial choice (Section 5.2), and the opposite, a one-line "focus mode" that hides earlier text (Section 5.3). As a critical lens, he reads Expressionist collage as a symptom of mechanisation and sets three concerns beside AI writing: standardised, "prefabricated" language, loss of writing as a way of thinking, and distraction by "linguistic worlds with an agenda" (Section 6). He closes by urging designers of future tools to "engage not only with new technological possibilities, but also with past writing innovations" (Abstract).

## Relation to the argument
The facet file treats it as the nearest neighbour in method: a design paper that takes a past writing form as a guide for AI interfaces. Reading it whole confirms that and sharpens the difference. It shares the paper's stance in ruling D10, history as a guide and not a mechanism. Buschek says so: the transfer concerns "a form or style", not a historic cause, and he marks a "key difference in scope" between literary collage and his concept (Section 2.2). Nothing in the essay touches word separation, *scriptio continua* or the history of reading. A search of the text finds none of these terms. The essay therefore gives no precedent for the spacing move and cannot support premises P1–P6.

Two points do bear on the argument. First, the fourth facet describes the person's work as deciding among and arranging machine-supplied pieces, which resembles the author's ruling D7 (understanding through deciding and taking in results). Buschek treats this as a gain in authorship control and a loss: the cited studies find lower perceived authorship under an editorial role (Section 3.4). Second, his reification proposal (Section 5.2.1) shows where the draft no longer reveals "this moment of choice" after acceptance, an interface that records the person's decision. That sits closer to P8 and P9 than to the spacing story, and it is a read-and-tune aid of the kind P9 doubts. Buschek offers it untested and names the risk that revisiting choices may distract. The paper should cite him for the method of consulting writing history when designing for AI, and for the claim that the field lacks "strong concepts", not for any claim about coordination through a third party; his setting is one writer and one tool.

Facet check: the facet's reading from the abstract ("adjacent in method, distant in content") holds. The full text adds that his history is literary Expressionism and Manovich's 1999 "avant-garde as software" (with hyperlinking as a parallel), and that he explicitly limits the claim to interface form.

- Leads not in the bib: Höök and Löwgren 2012, "Strong concepts"; Manovich 1999, "Avant-garde as software"; Dang et al. 2023 on diegetic and non-diegetic prompting; Draxler et al. on the AI ghostwriter effect; Arnold et al. 2020, predictive text encourages predictable writing.

## Caution
An essay, not an empirical study. The 36-design audit is explicitly "not a survey" and the criteria are the author's own (Section 4.1). The Expressionism parallel is argued by resemblance. He proposes that fragmentation "might be good or bad for users" and tests nothing (Section 3.1). The arXiv v1 may differ from the DIS version of record.
