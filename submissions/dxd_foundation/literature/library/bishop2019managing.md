# Bishop, S. (2019). Managing visibility on YouTube through algorithmic gossip. *New Media & Society, 21*(11–12), 2589–2606. https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444819854731

**Identifier:** doi:10.1177/1461444819854731 · **Read depth:** full_text (the author-accepted manuscript from King's College London's research portal, 17 pp., accepted 14 May 2019; read whole; the AAM has no journal pagination, so locators are section headings; the version of record was not compared) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** three-year online and offline ethnography (2015–2018) following "upwards of 30" UK beauty vloggers with at least 10,000 subscribers; interviews with 10 British beauty and lifestyle vloggers and 9 industry intermediaries, 30 minutes to 3 hours each (Methodology) · **Parties modeled:** two parties + third party (creators, audiences, YouTube's recommender) · **Facets:** U (also listed under M, abstract only there) · **Read:** 2026-10-15

## What it argues
"Algorithmic gossip" is "communally and socially informed theories and strategies pertaining to recommender algorithms, shared and implemented to engender financial consistency and visibility" (abstract). Platforms "release limited instruction on visibility to maintain a 'competitive advantage', as they fear content creators 'gaming' the algorithm" (Introduction, citing Kitchin). Knowledge circulates through the Internet Creators Guild's "Platform Changelog" of "gathered experiences ... unconfirmed by YouTube", behind membership fees of $75–$500 a year, and through closed Facebook and messaging groups where vloggers ask "are you having a terrible week too?" (Algorithmic gossip as collaborative). Creators crowdsource audits: one asked Instagram followers "Was [this video] in your sub box?"; another's request on Twitter "returned 41 fan images" (Algorithmic gossip, knowledge requests and algorithmic auditing). YouTube's official line, in the Creator Academy video, is "get the audience to like your videos", which the author reads as a "meritocratic framework" that diverts the question of how content is promoted (Algorithmic gossip as subversive). Gossip is productive: it supports "uploading frequently and producing feminised beauty content"; one lifestyle vlogger makes occasional beauty videos that get "5 x more views than anything else I do" (Locating algorithmic gossip as method). Vloggers of colour use it to voice suspected discrimination that they cannot prove.

## Relation to the argument
**P8 / D3.** YouTube's disclosure is a maxim ("make good content"); creators treat it as uninformative and build their own theories. The paper supports the abstract's sentence for the case where disclosure is minimal, and shows what fills the gap: shared, unverified lore.

**P9 — learning by deciding is social.** Vloggers learn the mediator by uploading, watching metrics and comparing notes; the unit that learns is the community, and access to it is gated by status and money. That qualifies the author's picture of an individual learning through action.

**Q3.** Visibility is the route to the audience; the time spent on gossip, auditing and genre-switching is the coordination cost the abstract names, though the author frames it as risk management in cultural production, not coordination.

## Caution
Interpretive ethnography; ten creator interviews; UK beauty vlogging 2015–2018; no measurement of whether gossip-based strategies raised visibility. The author's own method argument (gossip as data) is a claim about research, not about what the algorithm does.
