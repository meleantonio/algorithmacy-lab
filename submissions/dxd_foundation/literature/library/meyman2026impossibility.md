## What it argues
Meyman defends a distinction between observability and enforcement in AI governance. This version narrows the claim sharply: it "replaces the universal two-failure argument of earlier editions, whose timing and reconstruction inferences are withdrawn" (Abstract, p. 2; "Changes from Version 1.4.0," pp. 18–19). The surviving claim is an information-sufficiency result. He stipulates a model R of "ex-ante authorization": a verdict (ALLOW, DENY or ABSTAIN) must exist before release, be recorded in an artifact a third party can recompute, and gate execution (Section 3.2, pp. 5–6). Proposition 1 (Section 4.2, p. 11): if two governing states give the evaluation procedure identical information yet require different permissions, no deterministic procedure using only that information can answer correctly in both, and a randomised one gets at least one of them right with probability at most one half. The worked pair is a payment whose delegation is valid in one state and expired in the other, with the model's behaviour held fixed (Section 4.3, p. 12). Corollary 1 applies this to "observation-only" evaluation (p. 13).

The note then lists what it does not show. It does not show "that observational evidence must be excluded from authorization," does not show "that indistinguishable states are common," and does not show that any architecture suffices (Abstract; Section 4.6, pp. 14–15). Observation "can remain fail-closed by abstaining" but "cannot resolve permission correctly across the declared domain" (Abstract). In the conclusion, observability and enforcement "remain complementary layers" (Section 6, p. 18). Meyman admits the proof form is "an elementary indistinguishability argument" with no claim to novelty (Section 1, p. 3). ABSTAIN "blocks execution pending authorized human override" (Section 2, p. 4), so a person enters only at escalation. Section 4.8 (pp. 15–16) cites Arcuschin et al. (2026) on seven language models that shifted decisions on hiring, loan and admissions tasks by attributes they did not cite as reasons.

## Relation to the argument
The facet file calls this "a formal claim that observability cannot produce ex-ante authorization" and puts it in the same direction as the author's critique of observe-log-audit. Reading version 2.0.0 whole, that overstates it. The universal claim was withdrawn; what remains is conditional on a stipulated model and on a pair of states that the note exhibits, not shows to be common. The facet's "the author's critique of observe-log-audit" is also a mismatch of target: Meyman separates observation from a pre-release verdict and puts the person at ABSTAIN, so his remedy is a deterministic external gate, not a different human role.

The note bears on the Nadella contrast (premise P11 and the exposed-flank list in the argument map) from the Nadella side. An external, deterministic control that decides before release is the structure the map credits the post with ("keeps every control outside the model"); Meyman gives a formal reason for such a gate and says observation alone cannot replace it. That fits the map's statement that the post "shares the premise that the model cannot be read and the remedy of external controls." It does not support the author's claim of opposition, and it adds nothing on who holds the authority, the dimension on which the map says the two differ. It also leaves the people the system acts on out of the model (its parties are the system, the evaluator, a verifier and a human override).

For premise P12 (people monitor poorly), Section 4.8 is secondary evidence at best: it rests on one other paper's result and is used here for the narrow point that a stated justification can omit an influence.

## Caution
A self-published note by an author whose affiliation is FERZ, Inc., the company whose doctrine it supports, unreviewed, in a "research corpus" the author mostly cites himself. The version history shows the earlier universal claims were withdrawn in this edition, so any text quoting the earlier edition's strong claim would be unsafe. The formal content is a one-step indistinguishability argument and the whole practical weight rests on the stipulated model R (Section 3.2 says its applicability "must be established" case by case). It makes no empirical claim of its own. Do not cite it for "oversight fails," for any statement about human reviewers, or for the claim that observation is useless.
