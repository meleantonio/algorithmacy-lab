## What it argues

The experiment shows that teaching people how deferred acceptance computes its outcome does not teach them that honest ranking is safe, while a short description of the property itself does, in part. The five treatments are (pp. 3–5): Traditional DA Mechanics (Trad-DA), the standard algorithm with a step-by-step GUI; Menu DA Mechanics (Menu-DA), the full algorithm restated as a personal menu; Menu SP Property (Menu-SP), the two-step menu statement of strategyproofness "without specifying how one could calculate the mechanism's outcome" (p. 4); Textbook SP Property (Textbook-SP), a plain-language textbook definition; and Null, "close-to-zero-information" (p. 5). No treatment advises participants to rank honestly (p. 5).

Results on understanding (pp. 6, 24–25). Mean scores on the strategyproofness test were 56% in Trad-DA and 58% in Menu-DA, "statistically indistinguishable" from each other and close to Null's 54% (p. 6). Menu-SP scored 71% and Textbook-SP 62% (SE 2% each). Under an "arguably over-conservative" Bonferroni correction, Menu-SP still beats Textbook-SP at p < 0.01 and every other treatment at p < 0.001 (p. 24, fn. 14). Three quarters of Trad-DA participants (76%) could compute the DA outcome with no mistakes on the first attempt (p. 6). "Understanding the full, explicit mechanics of DA does not imply understanding its strategyproofness" (p. 6).

Results on behaviour (pp. 28–30). Rates of straightforward ranking were 56% (Trad-DA), 50% (Menu-DA), 59% (Menu-SP), 53% (Textbook-SP) and 48% (Null), SE 3% each (p. 7, fn. 5; p. 28). In Figure 10 (p. 29; two-sample *t*-tests on the pairs the figure marks) Menu-SP against Trad-DA is p = 0.47, Menu-SP against Menu-DA is p = 0.04, and Menu-SP against Null is p = 0.01. The authors call treatment effects on behaviour "overall small" and say the sample "is slightly under-powered to detect most differences within this range" (pp. 28, 30). Straightforward play "jumps" once understanding passes a threshold the authors put at "roughly 75–80%" (p. 29). Pooled across treatments, participants scoring above 75% ranked straightforwardly at 83%, against 46% below 75% (p. 30). The relation between understanding and play was not part of the pre-registered plan (p. 29). Menu-SP's gain in understanding was weaker on Prolific than at Cornell, which the authors flag as a possible "distributional consequence" (p. 33). The conclusion: "mechanical descriptions may remain too complicated to effectively convey strategyproofness, even in their menu form", while Menu-SP's "simplicity may be the key to success" (p. 37). In related work they add that giving people more information on mechanics can lower straightforward reporting (Danz et al. 2022; Guillen & Hing 2014), and "Menu-DA is less effective at teaching SP relative to Menu-SP—despite merely providing more information" (p. 36).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D3, premises P8 and P10.** The paper is strong support for the draft's split between Target 4 (the full rule) and Target 3 (a one-line property). Explaining the algorithm, even well enough that people can run it, left understanding of the property at the level of telling them nothing (p. 6). A one-line property statement moved it. That is the literacy finding (D3) in a lab: a text people can follow is not the same as knowing how to act.

**What it limits.** The behavioural gain from the property statement is small and, against the traditional description, not significant (p = 0.47, Figure 10). The step-function between understanding and play is post hoc (p. 29). The gain was smaller among remote, less-educated participants (p. 33). The draft's "real but partial effect" is an accurate summary.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 325 contains **one substantive misdescription and one omission**.
(a) "tested whether describing a matching mechanism as a personalized menu, in place of the traditional description of its algorithm, increased understanding". The sentence sets up a two-way contrast, menu against traditional. The experiment had two menu treatments. The menu description *of the algorithm* (Menu-DA) scored 58%, no better than the traditional description (56%) (p. 6). The 71% came from Menu-SP, which states only the strategyproofness property in menu form and gives no way to compute the outcome (pp. 4, 24). The draft's next sentence counts the 58% among "the other descriptions", so it treats a menu description as a non-menu one. A reader will take the 71% to be the effect of menu framing as such, when the paper's finding is that the full menu description failed and only the stripped-down property statement worked (p. 37).
(b) "Understanding of strategy-proofness rose to 71%, against 58% and 56% under the other descriptions." The numbers are right (p. 6), but there were four other descriptions, not two: Textbook-SP 62% and Null 54% are omitted (p. 24).
(c) "N = 542": correct (p. 5).
(d) "Straightforward play reached 59% under the menu description, against 56% under the traditional description, a difference that is not significant, and 48% under a control that conveyed almost nothing." Correct (p. 28; p = 0.47, Figure 10, p. 29). Under the same test Menu-SP against Null is significant (p = 0.01, Figure 10), and so is Menu-SP against Menu-DA (p = 0.04, Figure 10). The draft mentions neither. Note that the behavioural p-values in Figure 10 are not corrected for multiple comparisons.
Line 498: "Convention A rests on experiments in mechanism design that show a real but partial effect (Li, 2017; Gonczarowski et al., 2024)." Supported (pp. 28–30, 37).

## Caution

Working paper, not peer reviewed. A lab setting with three computer players and small prizes. The design detail (§2, pp. 9–22) and the appendices were not read, so wording of the treatments is known only from §1's summary. The behavioural comparisons are underpowered by the authors' own account (p. 30). The paper cannot be cited for a significant behavioural effect of the menu property description over the traditional description, nor for a menu description of the *algorithm* raising understanding.
