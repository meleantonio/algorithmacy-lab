## What it argues

Clark and Chalmers propose "an active externalism, based on the active role of the environment in driving cognitive processes" (§1). Their parity test: when "a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process", that part of the world is part of the cognitive process (§2). The cases are Tetris rotation, pen-and-paper multiplication, Scrabble tiles and the nautical slide rule (§2). They gloss Kirsh and Maglio's timings as physical rotation of "about 100 milliseconds, plus about 200 milliseconds to select the button" against "about 1000 milliseconds" for mental rotation (§2); the existing card for Kirsh and Maglio gives the source's own figures as about 100 ms against 800–1200 ms from a three-player pilot.

The coupled system is "a two-way interaction, creating a coupled system that can be seen as a cognitive system in its own right" (§3). Against the "Naked Mind" objection they concede that "reliable coupling is required", and that most reliable coupling happens inside the brain, but hold that a calculator or Filofax that is "always there when I need them" is coupled enough (§3). The Otto and Inga case (§4) extends the thesis from process to belief: Otto's notebook plays the role of memory, so its entries are his standing beliefs; "Twin Otto", whose notebook says 51st Street, believes the museum is on 51st Street (§4). Section 5 lists the features that make the case clear: the notebook is "a constant in Otto's life"; its information is "directly available without difficulty"; on retrieval "he automatically endorses it"; and it was "consciously endorsed at some point in the past" (§5). By those tests "The Internet is likely to fail on multiple counts, unless I am unusually computer-reliant, facile with the technology, and trusting" (§5). Socially extended cognition needs "a high degree of trust, reliance, and accessibility" (§5). The paper closes on a moral: "interfering with someone's environment will have the same moral significance as interfering with their person" (§5).

## Relation to the argument

**Premise P5 and the abstract's "cognitive offloading."** The paper supplies the strongest philosophical licence for calling the page part of the reader's cognitive system, which is what the abstract's move from spacing to "a representational effect" needs. It supports the Scrabble and slide-rule cases, where someone else built the artefact; the parity test does not ask who made the structure.

**The triad (D9) and custody (D8).** The four features in §5 are the paper's own conditions for an external store to count as part of the mind: constancy, easy access, automatic endorsement, past endorsement. A platform mediator with its own objective fails the fourth and makes the third hazardous, and the authors themselves rule the Internet out. Twin Otto is the philosophical version of the manipulation in `risko2019offloading`: change the store and you change the belief. For the argument this cuts against treating the mediator as the person's extended mind, and it gives the exposure "lowering a cost has costs" a precise form: the more automatic the endorsement, the larger the cost of a store someone else can edit. The closing moral sentence supports the author's framing of platform design as an intervention in the person rather than in a tool.

## Caution

A position paper with no data. The version read is the authors' web text, whose stated pagination (10–23) does not match the Crossref record (7–19); cite by section until a journal copy is checked. The Tetris numbers are second-hand. The paper argues about belief and process, not about learning, so it cannot be cited for the abstract's claim that a representational change let readers "internalize" anything.
