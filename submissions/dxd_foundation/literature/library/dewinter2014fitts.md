## What it argues

The authors defend the 1951 Fitts list, eleven statements on what "men are better at" and what "machines are better at," against sixty years of criticism. They treat it as a theory and score it on six criteria from the philosophy of science (plausibility, explanatory adequacy, interpretability, simplicity, descriptive adequacy, generalisability; §3). Their verdict: "the Fitts list is an adequate approximation that captures the most important regularity of automation" (p. 1). Table 1 (p. 2) reproduces the list from Fitts (1951, p. 10).

The section that matters here is §3.2.1. Dekker and Woods reject function allocation because it rests on a "substitution myth," the "false idea that people and computers have fixed strengths and weaknesses" (p. 5). Dekker (2005) puts it as: "capitalizing on some strength of computers does not replace a human weakness. It creates new human strengths and weaknesses" (p. 5). De Winter and Dodou answer that complementarity theories "do not provide explicit answers as to whether a function should be automated or not" (p. 5), and that the substitution charge is "false" because the list names capabilities machines lack (§3.2.2, p. 6).

In §4 they concede that automation "changes the nature of human work, often in ways unanticipated by designers" (p. 7), which the list does not model. They argue Fitts's report already anticipated the "ironies of automation": it urged "great caution" in assuming that "men can successfully monitor complex automatic machines" and take over on failure (p. 7), warned of skill loss, and held that human tasks "should provide activity" because activity "helps to insure that the human will keep abreast of the situation" (p. 8, quoting Fitts 1951, p. 6).

## Relation to the argument

First, this is the fair statement of the objection that "work moved from reader to artefact" is a function-allocation claim (facet X, objection 10). It also shows what a defender of the list concedes: the task changes character. That agrees with the dropped line "Reading 'no longer required the training'" (`ARGUMENT_MAP.md`, "What the argument no longer claims").

Second, the Fitts passages bear on ruling D7 and premise P12. Activity keeps a person alert, and passive monitoring plus takeover is a risk. That supports the half of D7 that favours acting. It does not support the claim that pilots do not read the situation: Fitts says a person needs "an adequate understanding of the traffic picture" to decide in an emergency (p. 7, quoted).

Third, §4 names skill degradation and mistrust, the "lowering a cost has costs" exposure in the map.

The limit: de Winter and Dodou are partisans of the list. The critique reaches this project only through their quotations of Dekker and Woods and of Dekker (2005); nobody here has read `dekker2002maba`.
