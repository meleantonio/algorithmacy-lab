## What it argues
"Traditionally, strategy-proofness is considered an extreme form of strategic simplicity" (p. 220). The authors ask whether truthful mechanisms induce truthful reporting and answer that "Misrepresentation under DA is prevalent across markets and countries" (p. 223). Evidence assembled (pp. 220–222): in the IPMM, "out of 704 relevant ROLs, 19.5 percent obviously misrepresented the applicant's true preferences"; in the NRMP survey, "Seventeen percent of the respondents reported that their ROL did not represent their true preferences, with about one-third reporting that the reason for their behavior was 'strategic'"; in the NRMP's own 2015 applicant survey, 5 percent of US seniors and 17 percent of independent applicants ranked programs by "likelihood of matching"; in Mexico City, Chen and Pereyra found "at least 20 percent" of a relevant sample contradicting their survey answers; in the lab, Chen and Sönmez found "on average 36 percent" not truthful under DA.

Four explanations (pp. 222–223). *Failing to identify the dominant strategy*: in the 2014 IPMM survey, "18 percent of the 310 respondents thought they could possibly gain from 'behaving strategically'", and "An additional 21 percent of the respondents reported that while they thought that truth-telling was dominant, they did not understand why". *Mistrust*: 3 of 51 verbal explanations in 2015 (5.8 percent); parents in Denver and New Orleans "were told to provide the full number of choices in their true order of preference" but "few parents actually trusted this message. Instead, they commonly pursued strategies that matched their own inaccurate explanations of how the match worked" (quoting Gross, DeArmond and Denice 2015, p. 27). When people do not understand or trust the mechanism, "they may resort to naïve theories" in which "the mechanism rewards higher ranking with increased probability of matching", a theory that "is consistent with the optimal behavior in many decentralized markets" and under the Boston mechanism. *Self-selection*: 18 of 51 said they dropped programs because their chances were low. *Nonclassical utility*: ego utility and social preferences. The conclusion contrasts "a much better grasp of the incentives induced by various designs" with "our limited understanding of the effects of human psychology on behavior in matching markets" and calls "a better understanding of the interaction between users and matching mechanisms" indispensable (p. 223).

## Relation to the argument
A compact statement, from inside market design, that the incentive property of a rule and the person's experience of it come apart. The naïve-theory explanation is the most useful for the abstract: people carry a model learned in decentralised markets (rank what you can get) into the strategy-proof one, which is the literacy-era habit the author says the triad needs to shed. The Denver quotation is evidence for ruling D3: parents were told the rule and disbelieved it. The paper does not say what design would make the dominant strategy visible; it lists interventions as "targeted" explanation and information. Note the difference in denominators between this piece (19.5% of 704) and the Management Science version (19.3% of 672, `hassidim2021limits`).

## Caution
A five-page proceedings piece without new analysis; its numbers are drawn from the authors' other work and from secondary sources. The "18 percent" and "21 percent" rest on one post-match survey of 310 self-selected respondents. It offers explanations, not tests.
