## What it argues
The paper turns incentive compatibility on the mechanism's operator. "Suppose the auctioneer can deviate from the rules provided that no single bidder detects the deviation. A mechanism is credible if it is incentive-compatible for the auctioneer to follow the rules" (abstract, p. 425). In a second-price auction "the auctioneer can profit by exaggerating the second-highest bid", so, as Vickrey noted, "the first-price auction is 'automatically self-policing,' while the second-price auction requires special arrangements that tie the auctioneer's hands" (p. 425). An industry newsletter on display advertising is quoted: "In a second-price auction, raising the price floors after the bids come in allows [online auctioneers] to make extra cash off unsuspecting buyers [...] publishers and buyers won't know for sure who their vendor is ripping off" (p. 426). Results, under symmetric independent private values and winner-only payments: "The first-price auction is the unique credible static mechanism. The ascending auction is the unique credible strategy-proof mechanism" (p. 425). Hence "an auction trilemma. Static, strategy-proof, or credible: An optimal auction can have any two of these properties, but not all three at once" (p. 428). The reason: "revelation mechanisms reveal too much. For a bidder to have a dominant strategy, his payment must depend on the other bidders' types. If the auctioneer knows the entire type profile... then the auctioneer can safely deviate" (p. 428). On practice: "Some Internet advertising auctions must be conducted in milliseconds, so latency precludes the use of multi-round protocols. Strategy-proofness matters when bidders are inexperienced... Credibility matters especially when bidders are anonymous to each other or require that their bids be kept private" (p. 429).

## Relation to the argument
The one source in the facet that models the mediator as an interested party who can cheat, which is the abstract's "algorithmic mediator that binds and optimizes". Its lesson for "strategic offloading" is that the device that relieves bidders of strategy (a strategy-proof direct mechanism) hands the mediator the information it needs to exploit them, unless the rule is dynamic. So in a triad the choice is not between gaming and not gaming but between who carries the strategic exposure. For Question 4 it explains, from theory, why display-ad exchanges moved to first-price (`cox2019simplifying`): a static rule the users can police. For the author's argument this supports looking at the form of the rule (static against ascending) rather than disclosure, and it adds a constraint the abstract does not state: the convention that frees the person must also be one the mediator cannot quietly bend. P12 (the owner of the interface is an interested party) finds a formal companion here.

## Caution
Only the introduction was read; the theorems, their assumptions (regularity, symmetry, discretised types) and the generalisations in §§5–6 are taken from the authors' summary. A theory of auctions with transfers; nothing on matching or on platform recommenders. "Credible" is defined relative to fully private communication, which the authors call "a tractable benchmark" (p. 426).
