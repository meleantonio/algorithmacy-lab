## What it argues
A new assistant professor of media production opens a newsletter by stating its programme. She says research on misinformation "treats the production of information as a black box," and that "What happens inside the black box is not incidental to why information misleads. It is often the whole story." Her example is a newsroom "running at half its former staff, under a ninety-second deadline, using a summarization tool it doesn't fully understand, producing a headline optimized not for accuracy but for the click." She claims that "the way information looks is not neutral. It is the accumulated residue of a thousand small decisions."

She introduces "democratic legibility," defined as "the capacity of citizens to read, interpret, and act upon the information systems that govern democratic life." The term inverts James Scott's legibility, which "flows downward, from institution to citizen: the state looks at you and makes you readable to itself"; she asks "Can citizens look back?" She contrasts media literacy ("Can you analyze this message well?") with legibility ("Can you understand the system producing the conditions under which this message appears, circulates, and gains power?"). She says no one has "developed a reliable methodology for measuring democratic legibility" and calls it an empirical question. The rest sets out the newsletter's name (the frame), its cadence (about every two weeks) and its audience. She offers her own guess that the answer to whether citizens can read democracy "is increasingly no." No data or sources are given.
