## What it argues
Charness, Samek and van de Ven start from the profession's norm: "There is a strong norm against the use of deception in experimental economics" (p. 385), and *Experimental Economics* "does not consider studies that employ deception." The abstract states the consensus: "providing false information is not permitted." What is unsettled is the "gray area" of omission and misleading wording (p. 386). They quote Hey (1998): "there is a world of difference between not telling subjects things and telling them the wrong things" (p. 386).

Researchers rated the seven scenarios (Table 4, p. 401). Using confederates or computers that participants are led to think are people was rated most deceptive (mean 5.33 of 7), then relying on participants misinterpreting instructions (4.78). Unexpected data use, subgroup re-matching and unpaid field participation were least deceptive (about 3.2). Across scenarios, mean appropriateness "if the issue was important and there was no other good way" was 4.65 of 7 (p. 402). "Result 1" reads: "The use of confederates (or bots) and misrepresentation of information are rated as the most deceptive of the scenarios" (p. 403). Europeans found deception more unethical than North Americans (difference 0.56 points, p < .001; p. 399). Students were more lenient (unethical 2.75 against 4.68, p < .001); only 27% knew their lab had a no-deception policy; 25% said they would participate less if deceived (pp. 403–404).

Section 5 lists respondents' alternatives: a larger sample instead of subgroup re-matching; multi-part designs instead of surprise restarts; for confederates or computer players, Bardsley's (2000) "Conditional Information Lottery" or a computer that replays a real earlier subject's decisions (Table 6, pp. 408–409). The authors recommend making the no-deception policy known and argue for "a more nuanced view," while "certainly not arguing for telling outright lies to the subjects" (pp. 410–411).

## Relation to the argument
**The proposed experiment (draft §8; ruling D9, premise P11).** The source matters to design, not to the theory. If the mediator M is a program or a confederate, the norm requires participants to be told that M is a program or a confederate: the bot scenario is the one researchers rated most deceptive. Giving M "a margin of its own" is compatible with the norm if participants are told truthfully that M has an objective, even without the formula; facet P drew the same line. Hiding M's interest while describing M as neutral would be a false statement.

**Ruling D3.** An arm in which the rule is simply not described (the opaque-mediator condition) is an omission. The paper does not support a general rule that omission is judged less deceptive than misstatement: the scenario researchers rated most deceptive (S5, mean 5.33) is itself an omission (not telling participants that confederates or computers are involved, while they are led to believe they face another participant), whereas the omission of unexpected data use (S4) was rated among the least deceptive (Table 1; Table 4, p. 401). An undisclosed rule for a disclosed program is not among the seven scenarios. The paper documents disagreement, not a ruling, so the author should state what participants are and are not told.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 459 cites the paper for "no false statements to participants (Charness, Samek, & van de Ven, 2022)." Supported: the norm and its consensus core, that "providing false information is not permitted" (abstract; pp. 385–386). The paper's own contribution is the gray area beyond that core, so the citation is accurate but thin; it would also support a sentence saying the protocol will disclose that M is a program (Table 1, S5; Table 4; Result 1). Not omitting that M has an objective is a reasonable extension of the same norm, but the survey did not test that scenario.

## Caution
A survey of opinion, not a rule; journals and labs set the binding policy. Respondents self-selected (about half of those contacted). Scenario wording differed between the researcher and student versions (Table 1). The researcher survey was in 2018, before the spread of AI agents in experiments; ratings of "computers that do not operate of their own volition, but instead behave as scripted" (Table 1, S5) may not carry over to AI mediators.
