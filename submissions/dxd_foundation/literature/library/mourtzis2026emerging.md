## What it argues

The review summarizes the history of human–machine interfaces against the industrial revolutions and then the recent engineering literature on sensors, extended reality, and robotics, in order to name challenges and opportunities for Industry 5.0 and the industrial metaverse. The abstract's problem is that interfaces now "are required to provide access to larger amounts and complexity of information, as well as more complex control capabilities."

Section 2 (pp. 2–3) and Figure 1 give the history they use. Industry 1.0 and 2.0: machines were tools; interaction was "factory layout, manual control, and machine maintenance"; interfaces were levers, wheels, then buttons and switches. Industry 3.0: the human's role shifts "from operators to overseers," driven by CNC and graphical user interfaces. Industry 4.0: HMI is "a critical enabler which ensures transparency, safety, and usability"; "visualization dashboards, Augmented Reality (AR), and decision-support tools are designed to assist humans in understanding machine behavior"; humans do "cognitive and supervisory tasks," and "trust and situational awareness" matter. Industry 5.0, still ongoing: the pillars are human-centricity, sustainability, and resilience, and the human's role shifts again, "from supervisor to colleague." They say Industry 5.0 is the first iteration in which the HMI model drives the technology rather than the reverse (p. 3). Extended reality is to provide "adaptive control capabilities, enhanced human-to-machine communication, and immersive visualization, reducing manual effort and cognitive workload" (p. 3).

The method is a Scopus review bounded to 2021–2026 (Section 3, pp. 4–5). Four keyword clusters: collaborative technologies, sensors, augmented and virtual reality, robotics. Table 1 counts the state-of-the-art set as sensors (40 papers), robotics (26), and XR (21), totaling the 87 publications.

Section 5 is their critical assessment. Sensor TRL in the set "is limited to between 3 and 4, with only a single case among forty showing TRL 5" (Section 5.1, p. 8). Table 2 (p. 11) reports average TRL of 3.76 for sensors (range 3–4), 4.57 for robotics (range 2–7), and 4.4 for XR (range 3–7). The collaborative model in Section 5.4 (p. 10) and Figure 5 is the sentence this paper uses: the extended-reality interface "provides the human with the necessary information to perform tasks and make decisions in real-time." The conclusions (p. 11) repeat the role change "from supervisor to collaborator" and the division of labor: sensing lets robots perceive, "XR interfaces provide instructions and support decision-making for humans collaborating with robots," and robots do the precision work. The prospects section (p. 11) notes brain–machine interfaces, invasive and non-invasive, as a further HMI category, first unidirectional and, "since 2025," also discussed as bidirectional.

## Relation to the argument

This is the manufacturing-side review of where interfaces stand in 2026. It supports the claim that the field's history of human–machine interaction is a history of what the human is given to operate or to read: levers, buttons, screens, dashboards, decision-support, then an extended-reality display that still "provides the human with the necessary information." The colleague of Industry 5.0 is still a person receiving instructions and making decisions from that display.

It does not support a claim about triadic platforms, disclosure experiments, or Nadella. The parties are a worker and a machine or robot on a shop floor. The essay's inference, marked as inference: a richer display that reduces "manual effort and cognitive workload" (p. 3) is still a literacy solution if the work that remains is understanding machine behavior and deciding from what the interface shows. The smoking gun would take that work off the person and show that it is gone. This review does not ask for that. Its own next step is more sensing, lighter XR, and collaborative robots (Figure 6, p. 11).

The low TRLs are a limit on citing the review as evidence that these interfaces work in factories. Most of the sensor papers had not left the laboratory.

## Caution

Engineering review of manufacturing interfaces, 2021–2026, Scopus, subject area limited to engineering in the follow-up queries. Inaccessible publications were excluded (Section 3), so the 87 are not the full search. TRL scores are the authors' assignments, not a standard coding by the original papers. No DOI was printed on the PDF. Do not cite Siemens or Hyundai investments, or the sensor-market dollar figures, from this review without opening those sources. Do not treat the industrial metaverse as a finding about platform triads.
