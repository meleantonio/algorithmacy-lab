## What it argues

Ride-hailing platforms, the authors argue, are prone to a failure mode that taxis are not. Under a "first-dispatch protocol, in which an idle driver is immediately dispatched every time a rider requests a trip" (p. 1), high demand depletes idle drivers, pickup times rise, and "Cars are thus sent on a wild goose chase (WGC) to pick up distant customers, wasting drivers' time and reducing earnings" (p. 1). The model's core identity is L = I + tQ + TQ: drivers are idle, driving a rider, or en route (p. 5). Trip supply S(T, L) = (L − I(T))/(t + T) is backward-bending in pickup time (Lemma 1, p. 7), so "there are two ways to supply the same number of trips for a fixed number of rides," one with low and one with high pickup time (p. 8). A WGC is diagnosable: it occurs when slack, "the ratio of idle drivers to en route drivers," falls below the elasticity of pickup time to idle density, estimated "somewhere between −0.25 and −0.5" (pp. 8–9). In a WGC, price cuts collapse the market through three reinforcing effects (pp. 13–14); "WGCs can always be avoided by increasing prices" (p. 12, Proposition 2, p. 14).

Evidence: slack was below 1/2 in 11.4% of half-hour observations and below 1/4 in 1.36% (p. 20); WGCs occur "less than 10% of the time by our most conservative measure" (p. 17); completion rates, rider cancellations, "no driver nearby" messages and ETAs all deteriorate as slack approaches 0.5, while the surge multiplier rises steeply only below 0.3, so "Uber might benefit from reacting more strongly when slack is between 0.5 and 0.3" (p. 21). Calibrated to Manhattan with Uber's take τ = 0.238 (p. 25): "a 30% increase in prices from the optimum only decreases welfare by less than 4%. On the other hand, a 30% decrease in prices from the optimum leads to a 41% decrease in welfare" (p. 26). In a two-market exercise the dynamic price is "only 3% above the static price" in the strong market and "38% below" in the weak one (p. 29); across 168 hourly markets "the optimal static price is at the 92nd percentile of the distribution of optimal dynamic prices" (p. 32; also stated pp. 2 and 37). Alternatives to surge (random denial, a dispatch radius "on the order of twenty minutes," queues, rematching, charging for pickup time) are discussed and set aside on reliability, interface and public-relations grounds (pp. 32–34); "product leaders at Uber would be loath to compromise" the rider's view of an approaching car (p. 33).

## Relation to the argument

**Question 3 (what "irreducible" means operationally).** This is the facet's most concrete case of a triad whose behaviour is not the sum of its dyads. Supply of trips depends on both sides through pickup time; a rider's request removes an idle driver and lengthens every other rider's wait; the labour-supply response feeds back (pp. 13–14). No party in a rider–platform or driver–platform dyad can see or cause a wild goose chase. If the author wants an operational sense of irreducibility that is not Φ, this feedback structure is one.

**Question 4 ("binds and optimizes").** The mediator binds by first-dispatch (it, not the parties, decides the match) and by price; it optimizes (the paper models a welfare maximiser and a revenue maximiser, Proposition 4, p. 16). The authors' reading that surge *lowers* prices overall (p. 2) reverses the usual gaming story: the mediator's dynamic rule is what lets it avoid a permanently high price.

**Where it cuts against the author.** The remedies are prices, radii and queues; no interface change for riders or drivers is proposed, and the one UI feature discussed (seeing the car approach) is treated as a constraint on design, not a lever. The strategic agent here is the platform, not the counterparts.
