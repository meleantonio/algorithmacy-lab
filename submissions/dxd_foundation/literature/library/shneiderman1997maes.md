# Shneiderman, B., & Maes, P. (1997). Direct manipulation vs. interface agents. *Interactions*, *4*(6), 42–61. https://doi.org/10.1145/267505.267514

## What it argues

Shneiderman: the successful systems "all had rapid, incremental, and reversible actions, selection by pointing, and immediate feedback (100-millisecond updates for all actions). I believe that this strategy reduces errors and encourages exploration" (p. 44). His demonstrations (FilmFinder, LifeLines, Visible Human) make the case that "the overview is the most important" and that control gives users responsibility (p. 48). Maes: a software agent is "personalized," "proactive," "long-lived" and "adaptive" (pp. 49–50); it is needed because the environment is "continuously changing" and "the users are becoming more and more naive," so "a person has to delegate" (p. 50). Her Kasbah agents "buy and sell on behalf of users" in a marketplace, tested with 200 people and planned for 15,000 (p. 52). "Agents are not an alternative for direct manipulation … They are actually complementary metaphors" (p. 53). Giving up control is like the car mechanic: "I don't mind giving up some control … as long as the job is done in a more-or-less satisfactory way" (p. 54). The design challenge is "understanding and control" (p. 54).

Shneiderman's concession: "we now see two levels: the user interface level, which users want to be predictable and controllable … and the level below the table, where there may be some interesting algorithms such as collaborative filtering" (p. 55); adaptation is fine "as long as it does not interfere with the user's prediction of behavior" (p. 56). He endorses Maes's slide "Make the user model available to the user" and says "I don't see that being done in most of the work about agents" (p. 58). On time-critical settings he invokes Sheridan's supervisory control: "If they do not know or are uncertain about what the results of their actions are, they will disengage the automatic system," and "In emergency situations, people cannot solve problems. They can only do what's rehearsed and predictable" (p. 59). His closing plea is for studies, "whether they are controlled scientific experiments, usability studies, or simply observations," that "report on real users doing real tasks" (p. 60). Maes closes: "there are real limits to what we can do with visualization and direct manipulation because our computer environments are becoming more and more complex" (p. 61).

## Relation to the argument

**The abstract's Shneiderman citation.** The debate records the moment direct manipulation met adaptive intermediaries, and the terms agreed on both sides: a predictable surface, adaptation below it, the user model open to the user (pp. 55, 58). A platform that optimises matching and pricing is Maes's agent operating on the user's surface for a third party's objective, and Kasbah (p. 52) is a 1997 picture of an agent negotiating with other people's agents. The debate supports the author's premise that a dyadic design tradition did not anticipate the triad, and it shows the tradition's own proposed remedy was disclosure of the model, which the author's D3 classifies as a literacy intervention.

**D7 and the pilots.** Shneiderman's account of operators under uncertainty (p. 59) is the same as in the IUI paper: they disengage. It cuts against "pilots … act and through the action gauge and adjust in the moment" as a description of what people do when the system's response is unpredictable.

**P13.** "Agent promoters might shift some attention to showing users what is happening so that they can monitor and supervise the performance of agents" (p. 61) is the user-side version of the enterprise controls in the Nadella post.

## Caution

An edited transcript of two debates, not an argued paper; claims such as "100-millisecond updates" and "reduces errors" (p. 44) are assertions. Kasbah's 200-person test is mentioned without results (p. 52). Both speakers are advocates of their own products. The "two levels" concession (p. 55) is Shneiderman's, made in debate, and should be quoted as such.
