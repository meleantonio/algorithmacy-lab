## What it argues

Blume, Lai and Lim test, in the lab, a classic claim from theory: a mediator that garbles messages can make communication between two parties with conflicting interests more informative. The intuition is in footnote 1: "The mediator has a role because he is committed to a garbling rule" (p. 1, n. 1). In their game the sender of type s wants to be identified and type t wants to pass as s. With direct talk, only pooling is an equilibrium; with mediation (the mediator passes "t" through and turns "s" into "s" or "t" with equal chance), separation is also an equilibrium and is selected by their level-k analysis (§3, pp. 7–10).

The data follow the prediction in direction. In the last ten rounds, type-t senders separate "75% of the time" under mediated talk and only "12% of the time" under direct talk (p. 14). Receivers respond to message "s/L" with the separating action L 68% of the time under mediation against 13% under direct talk (p. 16). Payoffs rise moderately: in the first ten rounds, senders and receivers earn 89.22 and 90.03 points under mediation against 80.46 and 84.66 under direct talk; in the last ten, 85.75 and 90.44 against 80.87 and 85.62 (p. 20).

Two departures matter. About a fifth of type-t senders keep pooling under mediation (21–25%, p. 14). And about 20% of receivers take an action no belief about the sender could justify, behaving as if there were no mediator: "The receiver behavior we observe under mediation is consistent with about 20% of receivers analyzing the game as if there is no mediation" (p. 17). Receivers fail to reach even the payoff they could guarantee by ignoring messages (p. 20).

The framing of messages made no lasting difference: "The choice of language has no impact on terminal behavior. It does not matter whether players communicate using declaratives or directives, or through a direct mechanism" (p. 2). Initial behaviour differed slightly across direct-talk frames and the difference mostly faded (Appendix D, pp. 39–45); for type-s senders a smaller gap remained in the last ten rounds (90% vs 82%, p = 0.0575, p. 39). ## Relation to the argument

**Triadic mediation (§8.1, Claims 2 and 4).** This is a clean experiment with two humans and a third-party mediator whose rule is announced, and it shows the mediator changing what the two parties can achieve. That supports the draft's premise that a mediator's rule, not only the parties, shapes the outcome. The mediator here has no objective of its own, so it is a benign baseline, not the interested mediator (J_M ≠ J_A, J_B) of Claim 2.

**It complicates Claim 2.** Claim 2 says a representational convention beats textual disclosure. Blume and colleagues varied the representation of the same mechanism (declarative reports, directive recommendations, or a direct mechanism) and found it did not matter once players had experience (p. 2; Appendix D). What mattered was the mechanism itself. That is a contrary data point for any claim that presentation, holding the mechanism fixed, drives terminal behaviour. Their manipulation is message labels, not an interface, so the contrary evidence is limited.

**Custody (Claim 4).** The benefit depends on the mediator's commitment to its garbling rule (p. 1, n. 1). That gives the draft a mechanism-level reason why a committed rule matters. It does not test drift; the rule never changes.

## Caution

I read the February 2019 working paper. The JET version (2023) may have changed numbers, treatments or wording; cite page numbers only after checking the published article. The game is a stylised two-type sender-receiver game with abstract labels, not an allocation market. The mediator is a computer with no objective, so the paper cannot speak to an interested mediator or to custody. Students at one Hong Kong university.
