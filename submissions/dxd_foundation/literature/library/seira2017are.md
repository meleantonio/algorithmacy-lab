# Seira, E., Elizondo, A., & Laguna-Müggenburg, E. (2017). Are information disclosures effective? Evidence from the credit card market. *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 9*(1), 277–307.

**Identifier:** doi:10.1257/pol.20140404 · **Read depth:** full_text (the 31-page journal PDF hosted by J-PAL, February 2017; pagination is the journal's; the online appendix is not in the file and I did not read it) · **Source-tier:** peer-reviewed article · **Evidence basis:** direct_read · **Design and sample:** randomised field trial at one large Mexican bank: 167,190 high-risk credit cards (upper tercile of predicted default), 77,175 messages against 90,015 controls, seven message types, outcomes tracked monthly from September 2010 to June 2011; plus an out-of-sample test at two other banks (about 30,000 clients per arm) · **Parties modeled:** person–firm (cardholder and bank, with the regulator as designer of the disclosure) · **Facets:** H · **Read:** 2026-10-13

## What it argues
Seira, Elizondo and Laguna-Müggenburg test whether mandated disclosures change what indebted cardholders do. The bank mailed envelopes that looked like monthly statements but held one message (p. 279). Two messages copied US disclosure law: a salient personalised interest rate (Rate; 12,900 clients) and the personalised months to pay off the debt at the minimum payment (MTP; 12,825). Four were peer comparisons and one was an explicit warning against overconfidence (pp. 279, 285–286).

The baseline survey shows how little the sample knew: "only 3 percent of clients claimed to know their exact interest rate and 34 percent claimed to know it approximately within 5 percentage points," and 92% said they read their statement carefully (pp. 284–285). The average interest rate was 44% a year (p. 283).

Findings. The Rate message had no effect: "−35 pesos for March and 14 pesos for April. This is a tiny 0.2 percent of mean debt and is not statistically" significant (p. 292). The MTP message did not change debt and raised delinquency among frequent interest payers and high-debt clients by 0.011 points in April, 7.4% of the mean (p. 295). The "high risk" thermometer message cut delinquency by about 8% of its mean, and the "low risk" message raised it by about 7% (pp. 279–280, 296). The warning reduced debt by about 0.7% and left delinquency unchanged (p. 280). Effects, where present, lasted one or two months. A power analysis finds the design could detect 455 pesos of debt (1.6% of mean debt) and 0.006 points of delinquency (4% of the mean) with 80% power (p. 302). At two other banks, with representative clients, direct price comparisons across banks and monthly repeats, effects were "always less than 5 percent of the mean," and none was significant at 10% (p. 304).

Their conclusion: "currently mandated disclosures are likely to have zero effect and ... alternative messages that include peer comparisons and warnings are probably more effective" (p. 305). They suggest direct comparisons and easier switching, pointing to Kling et al. 2012 (pp. 303, 305).

## Relation to the argument
Facet H uses this as the clean case that a standard-format disclosure (a reading task) did nothing where Kling's computed comparison did something. The full text supports the first half exactly and softens the second. The authors themselves state that the Rate message "gave no benchmark for comparing the interest rate to that of other banks" and cite Kling et al. 2012 as showing that comparison frictions matter (p. 303). The benchmark is the missing ingredient, not the reading. Their own non-standard messages also work only a little: "their average effects are still small" (p. 297). So the paper supports ruling D3 ("disclosure doesn't affect our ability to navigate") on this one disclosure type, in this market, and not as a general law. The authors warn they "do not know if the clients did actually read" the message; they estimate the effect of sending, which is what the law mandates (pp. 290–291). That limits any claim about what reading would do.

Fit with the triad: the bank both sets the rate and sends the mandated disclosure; the regulator is the third party. The people who gain nothing from the disclosure are the ones the law meant to protect. This bears on P10 only loosely.

Facet check: facet H quotes the abstract accurately. It does not mention that the MTP message raised delinquency, which cuts against a "disclosure is merely inert" reading.

## Caution
One bank, one high-risk population, a single mailing (the other-bank test varies frequency); effects on welfare are not estimated; reading is not observed. Mexican credit cards at 44% interest differ from most platform settings.
