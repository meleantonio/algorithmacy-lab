## What it argues

The study asks whether older readers depend more than young readers on the spaces between words, and why. Older adults had typical lower acuity (20/30 against 20/17) but similar education and reading hours (p. 6).

Sentence reading times (Table 1a, p. 17), in ms:

| | Normal | Unspaced | Open squares | Closed squares |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Young | 2,308 | 4,151 | 3,351 | 2,772 |
| Older | 2,831 | 5,416 | 6,756 | 4,372 |

Removing spaces lengthened young adults' sentence times by about 80% and older adults' by about 91% (computed from Table 1a). Progressive saccades shortened from 8.0 to 5.7 characters (young) and from 10.2 to 7.0 (older). Comprehension stayed above 95% in every condition for both groups (p. 7).

For both groups, closed squares, a "salient (low spatial-frequency) cue to word boundaries", disrupted least (abstract, p. 2). Young readers were hurt most by unspaced text; older readers were hurt most by open squares, which several said they "often mistook … for letters" (p. 12). The age difference in reading time was 523 ms with normal spacing, 1,265 ms unspaced, and 3,406 ms with open squares (Table 1a). The authors conclude that "older readers can adapt reasonably flexibly to the loss of conventional spaces so long as other coarse-scale cues to word boundaries are available" (pp. 11–12).

Word frequency effects grew when spaces were removed or filled, but the growth was the same for both age groups (pp. 10–11), so "although older adults struggle with the loss of conventional cues to word boundaries, this is not due to additional difficulties in word recognition" (abstract, p. 2). The authors read the age difference as visual (lower acuity and greater crowding) and as a shift to a "more cautious reading strategy" (p. 12).

## Relation to the argument

This bears on P3 (readers used to spaced script lose a third to a half of their speed when spaces go) and on P5 (spacing turns an inferential task into a perceptual one).

For **P3 it supports the size of the effect for young adults and shows it varies with the reader**. An 80% longer reading time means reading at about 56% of normal speed, a loss of about 44%, inside the draft's "roughly half". For readers over 65 the loss is larger. A single figure for "modern readers" hides that spread.

For **P5 it supports the perceptual reading and sharpens it.** What older readers needed was a coarse, salient mark at each boundary, not empty space as such: closed squares restored much of the benefit, and letter-like open squares did not. That is a human-factors point the draft can use. The cue works through low-spatial-frequency salience, so whether a convention helps depends on the reader's visual system as well as on habit.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** McGowan et al. are not cited in DRAFT_v2. They bear on these lines:
- Line 113 ("By carving white channels between character clusters, spacing gives the oculomotor system targets for the next saccade … Deprived of these spatial markers, modern readers lose roughly half their reading rate (Rayner et al., 1998)"), with the same figure repeated at line 17. **Supports for young adults, narrows for the general claim.** Young readers' sentence times rose 80% (rate about 56% of normal); older readers' rose 91% (Table 1a). Closed squares, which are not white channels, recovered most of the loss for the young (2,772 ms, against 2,308 normal and 4,151 unspaced; about 20% slower than normal). So much of the benefit comes from a salient boundary mark, not from whiteness as such.
- Line 111 ("word spacing substituted visual segmentation for that inference"). **Supports** the visual-segmentation reading: the age-specific cost came from visual, not lexical, processing (pp. 10–12).
- Line 119 ("A convention pays once readers have come to depend on it"). **Complicates.** Older readers, with decades more practice in spaced text, were hurt more by its removal, which fits dependence; but the authors attribute the extra cost to visual decline, not to habit (p. 12).

## Caution

Small groups (16 per age group) reading single English sentences in Courier font. The version read is the authors' manuscript; wording and table values may differ slightly from the journal's version, which was not seen, and page numbers here are the manuscript's. Percentages of slowdown are computed from Table 1a means, not reported by the authors. The study concerns modern readers of a spaced script, so it says nothing directly about medieval readers.
