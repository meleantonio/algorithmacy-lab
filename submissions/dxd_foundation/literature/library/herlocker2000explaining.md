## What it argues

The paper asks how a collaborative-filtering recommender should explain its recommendations, and whether explanation raises acceptance and the quality of users' choices. The authors start from trust. Such systems "have yet to be successful" where accepting a recommendation carries risk (p. 1), because they compute from sparse data and are "black boxes, computerized oracles that give advice, but cannot be questioned" (p. 1). They write that "These problems have prevented acceptance of ACF systems in all but the low-risk content domains" (p. 1). That is the authors' diagnosis. No data is offered for it.

They then build explanations "outside-in", from the user's conceptual model of the system (p. 3): a white-box model (profile → similar neighbours → combined ratings, pp. 3–4), a black-box model where the owner may not reveal the process and instead justifies by past performance (p. 5), and "misinformed conceptual models" (p. 5). In that last section they flag a case they set aside: a recommender "claiming to provide personalized recommendations, while pushing high inventory or high margin items" (p. 5).

Experiment 1 (Table 1, p. 6). Against a base case of about 4.5 (explanations 11 and 12, means 4.53 and 4.51), the best performer was a histogram of neighbours' ratings grouped into good, neutral and bad (mean 5.25, N = 76), then "past performance" ("MovieLens has predicted correctly for you 80% of the time in the past", mean 5.19, N = 77), then a neighbour-ratings histogram (5.09) and table (4.97), similarity to other movies rated (4.97) and favourite actor (4.92, SD 1.73). The exact wording of that explanation is given on p. 7. The authors note that the 80% figure was not measured: "this is the only explanation that is not based on actual data"; they "extrapolated this number" from experience of the algorithm's accuracy (p. 7, fn. 2). Four explanations (18–21) scored significantly below the base case, and the authors conclude that "Poorly designed explanations can actually decrease the effectiveness of a recommender system" (p. 7). A richer graph (explanation 16, 4.36) did worse than a simple histogram, and a correlation of 0.4 confused users who read it as weak (p. 7).

Experiment 2 (pp. 8–9). Of 743 mini-surveys, users consulted MovieLens in 315 and it had some effect in 257. On filtering performance: "There was no statistically significant difference between any two experimental groups (based on a one-way ANOVA with α = 0.05)" (p. 8; Figure 5 adds that the data were "of extremely high variance"). On acceptance: 86% of 97 exit-survey respondents wanted their explanation interface added to the site (p. 8). The authors call the performance result "inconclusive" and "confounded primarily by lack of good data", since most decisions were made without consulting MovieLens (p. 9). In conclusion they "were unable to prove or disprove our hypothesis" that explanation improves filtering (p. 10). They also note that explanation design spans a "dimension that varies from persuasive to informational" (p. 10). The two experiments were run in the reverse of the order reported (p. 10).

## Relation to the argument

**Ruling D3 and premise P8.** This is the founding paper of the explanation remedy that the draft opens against, and its own data fit the author's line better than its framing does. What explanation raised was a stated likelihood of going to the movie and a stated wish to keep the feature. What it did not raise, measurably, was the quality of users' decisions (p. 8). The authors' explanation for this (sparse use, uncontrolled variance) is fair, so the paper is no evidence that explanation fails. It is evidence that explanation was adopted for acceptance and trust before anyone had shown it improved the decision. That is P8's claim in its earliest form.

**What it complicates.** The second most persuasive explanation in Experiment 1 was "past performance", a single accuracy figure extrapolated rather than measured (Table 1, p. 6; p. 7, fn. 2), and the paper notes that explanation can be persuasive rather than informational (p. 10). That is a warning the draft can use: an explanation can raise acceptance without showing anything true about the system. The "black box" section also concedes an owner's motive to withhold the process (p. 5), and the "misinformed models" section names a recommender that pushes high-margin items under cover of personalisation (p. 5). These are two-party conflicts (owner against user) that the paper raises and then sets aside.

**Use in DRAFT_v2:** Line 31 cites Herlocker, Konstan and Riedl (2000) for two statements. (a) "When early collaborative filtering algorithms began making recommendations, researchers observed that users resisted suggestions whose origin they could not discern." Partly supported. The paper asserts that black-box opacity "prevented acceptance" in high-risk domains (p. 1), but it reports no observation of users resisting. It argues the point and does not measure it. "Observed" overstates. (b) "The initial remedy was straightforward: attach an explanation showing neighboring user ratings or item similarities." Supported: neighbour-rating histograms and tables and "similarity to other movies rated" are explanations 1, 3, 4 and 5 (Table 1, p. 6), and the histogram was the best performer (pp. 6–7). The next sentences on line 31 (an expectation that showing internals yields accurate mental models, calibrated trust and "better decisions") are not attributed to Herlocker. Note, though, that Herlocker et al. tested "better decisions" directly and found no significant difference (p. 8). The draft could cite that result to support its own case.

## Caution

Volunteer MovieLens users, a hypothetical $7 scenario and one movie ("Titanic", rated for the first author, p. 6 fn. 1) in Experiment 1. Ratings of intention, not choices. Experiment 2's performance measure was underpowered by the authors' own account (p. 9). The page mapping to the ACM proceedings (pp. 241–250) is inferred from the copy's length and was not checked against the ACM PDF. The cited "80%" in the past-performance explanation was not real data (p. 7, fn. 2). The paper cannot be cited for a measured effect of explanation on decisions, or for an observation of users resisting.
