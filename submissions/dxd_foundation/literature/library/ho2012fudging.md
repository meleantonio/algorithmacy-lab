## What it argues

Ho tests "targeted transparency's poster child" (p. 582). Fung, Graham and Weil rate restaurant grading "highly effective", and Ben-Shahar and Schneider "point to restaurant grading as a salutary exception and as the prototype for promising regulatory alternatives" (p. 582). Ho finds that its merits "rest on remarkably fragile empirical grounds" (p. 586), and that "jurisdictions fudge more than nudge" (abstract, p. 574).

Findings, jurisdiction by jurisdiction:
- **San Diego: inflation.** "While 703 restaurants received a score of 90, only two received a score of 89"; of 8,941 graded restaurants only eight had a B (p. 611). Of sixty-two graded restaurants that scored below 90, the median time to scoring above 90 was seven days; county law lets a restaurant pay ($142 an hour) for a reinspection within thirty days, and Ho argues such strategic cleanups are much less likely to bring general health benefits (p. 612). Scores do carry some information: about a quarter of the variation in one cycle's score is predicted by the last (p. 614).
- **New York: noise.** Grading created a cliff at the A threshold: before grading 1,424 inspections scored 13 and 1,784 scored 14; after, 3,923 scored 13 and 1,416 scored 14 (p. 632). Prior scores predict "less than 2% of score variation" (p. 635). An A-range restaurant has a 37 percent chance of an A at its next initial inspection, a C-range restaurant 20 percent (p. 637). Ho attributes the noise in part to New York's complex score sheet (Part V, pp. 638–643).
- **No detectable health effect.** Restaurant-related 311 complaints and Google searches for "food poisoning" did not change with grading (pp. 643–646): "we find no evidence based on these indicators of positive health effects" (p. 646).
- **Perverse resource shift.** Compliance inspections fell from 8–15 percent of all inspections to under 5 percent, while grade reinspections rose; 58 percent of reinspections were of restaurants initially in the B range (p. 647).
- **Elsewhere.** North Carolina, South Carolina and Louisville give A grades to 99, 97 and 94 percent of restaurants, with threshold spikes; Louisville's spike moved when its A cut-off moved (Appendix D, p. 671).

Ho also reopens the Los Angeles evidence: "There are, however, reasons to question the findings on foodborne illness", citing small counts, a decline that may have begun earlier, and a comparison region that diverges on non-food illnesses (p. 598); and grading in Los Angeles came with more inspectors, more closures and a new state food-temperature law (pp. 599–601).

His lessons are for design, not abolition. "[C]ognitive limitations impede not just users of information, but suppliers as well" (p. 587). He proposes releasing full microdata, simpler score sheets, random inspection timing, higher grade thresholds and statistical adjustment for inspectors (Part VII.A). "[N]udges cannot compensate for underlying problems in regulatory design" (p. 655). He still calls targeted transparency "one of the most promising regulatory approaches of this generation" (p. 656).

## Relation to the argument

**Line 164 and line 500.** The paper's example of a disclosure that works is the case Ho dismantles outside Los Angeles. His evidence does not say simple signals never work. It says that a simple signal is only as good as the measurement behind it, that the discloser and the inspector both target the threshold, and that in New York the grade carried almost no information about future sanitation. That cuts against the paper's theoretical claim (line 500) that working disclosures succeed "insofar as they function as simple perceptual signals": simplicity of the display did not secure validity.

**Ruling D3 and premise P10.** Ho's argument has an algorithmacy-friendly reading. The fault lies in how the information is produced (the inspection), not in how it is read. The paper's §5 concern with the validity of feedback (premise P9) has a parallel here: a grade that does not track the underlying state is a cue that cannot be learned from.

**Premise P12.** In New York the regulated party gained a "grade pending" card that it could post until a hearing, and B and C cards were posted only briefly (pp. 624, 629). The convention bent toward the discloser, the opposite of the bending the paper looks for.

## Caution

Observational; Ho states that grade reform is never randomly assigned and is usually bundled with other changes (pp. 599–601), and his New York health indicators (311 calls, search volume) are proxies, not hospitalisations. The author testified at a New York City Council hearing on grading (p. 586, n. 68). Law-review format: very long footnotes carry much of the evidence. New York's system has changed since 2012.
