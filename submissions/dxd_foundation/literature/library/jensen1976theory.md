## What it argues
The authors combine agency theory, property rights and finance to explain why a firm's ownership structure takes the form it does. Their definition (§1.4, p. 308): "We define an agency relationship as a contract under which one or more persons (the principal(s)) engage another person (the agent) to perform some service on their behalf which involves delegating some decision making authority to the agent." The core claim follows: "If both parties to the relationship are utility maximizers there is good reason to believe that the agent will not always act in the best interests of the principal." The principal can limit the divergence with incentives and "monitoring costs"; the agent may pay "bonding costs" to guarantee good conduct. Neither is free, so a "residual loss" remains. Agency costs are the sum of monitoring expenditure by the principal, bonding expenditure by the agent and the residual loss (p. 308).

Section 2 uses a manager who sells part of his firm to outsiders. When he owned all of it he bore the full cost of perquisites; once he owns a fraction a, each dollar costs him only a times a dollar, so he consumes more of them and the firm's value falls (pp. 312–317). The authors also say that this is likely not the largest source of conflict: as his claim shrinks his incentive to seek out profitable ventures falls too (p. 313). Buyers anticipate this, so the owner-manager bears the cost through the price he gets (pp. 312–313). Auditing, budget limits and incentive pay are the monitoring tools (§2.4, p. 322), and audited accounts and limits on his authority are bonding (p. 324). Section 3 asks why diffuse equity is common given these costs and finds no good existing answer. Section 4 turns to debt: with little of his own money in, the owner-manager "will have a strong incentive to engage in activities (investments) which promise very high payoffs if successful even if they have a very low probability of success" (p. 334). The conclusion (p. 357) calls agency costs "as real as any other costs" whose level depends on law and "human ingenuity in devising contracts."

§1.5 (pp. 310–311) defines the firm as "a nexus for a set of contracting relationships," and §1.4 (p. 309) notes agency costs "arise in any situation involving cooperative effort ... by two or more people even though there is no clear cut principal-agent relationship."
