## What it argues
The surveyed paradigm is stated at the start: AI systems "augment or assist human decision makers—by providing a prediction or recommendation for a decision task, which humans can choose to follow or ignore and make their own decision" (§1, p. 1). The survey organises studies by decision task (Table 1), AI assistance element (Table 3) and evaluation metric (Tables 4–5).

Tasks (§3): recidivism, medical diagnosis, income and loan prediction, education, leisure, artificial tasks; "Although many decision tasks require domain expertise, experts are seldom the subjects of study" (§3.3, p. 9); most studies use "AI for discovery" tasks where ground truth comes from external processes (§3.2). Assistance elements (§4.2) are the prediction, information about the prediction (uncertainty, local feature importance, rules, examples, counterfactuals, natural-language rationales), information about the model (accuracy, global importance, documentation, training data), and "other AI system elements affecting user agency or experience". "The typical paradigm of human-AI decision making is to have models providing predictions, then users can choose to follow or ignore" (§4.2.4, p. 20). The minority alternatives listed there are workflow interventions: users "making their own predictions before being shown the model output", training phases, outcome feedback, varying response time (Buçinca et al.'s cognitive forcing with a 30-second wait), and levels of user agency, including feedback to the model, guiding the outcome before or after the decision, interactive explanations and choosing inputs; "Typical decision-support AI systems work in a closed loop without the possibilities for guidance from end users" (§4.2.4, p. 20). The authors name "A focus on AI explanations" as a trend and "Focus on decision trials only instead of the holistic experience with AI" as a gap, since discrete trials "lack ... ecological validity" (§4.3, pp. 21–22). Models were mostly shallow or Wizard-of-Oz (Table 2).

Metrics (§5): accuracy of the joint human–AI decision against a baseline is the most common; understanding is measured by forward simulation; trust by self-report and reliance by agreement, switching, over- and under-reliance; the authors stress that trust and reliance "are in fact two different constructs" (§5.2, p. 27) and that "results from subjective and objective metrics do not always align" (§5.3, p. 28). The field "prioritizes optimizing decision outcomes rather than the experience of human decision makers" (§5.3, p. 28). The closing call asks the field to consider "what matters for different stakeholders instead of just the decision-makers (e.g., people whose life will be impacted by the decision)" (§6, p. 30).

## Relation to the argument
For facet question 1 this is the HCI census of what the person gets: a prediction and things to read about it (explanations, uncertainty, accuracy). That supports the abstract's second sentence for the HCI side and supports D3 (`../../SOURCE.md`): explanation is the field's dominant element, and it is a literacy-side intervention. The survey also shows where the abstract's alternatives already exist as a minority: predict-before-seeing, interactive explanation, user control of inputs and outcomes are "epistemic action" in the abstract's sense, inside the dyad, and the authors judge them under-studied.

On the dyad the paper supports the abstract fully: every surveyed study pairs one decision maker with one AI, and the people decided about are named only as missing stakeholders. No study has two counterparts coordinating through the system. The survey's complaint that trials lack ecological validity and that experts are rarely the subjects is a limit on everything the facet cites from this field.
