## What it argues

Deferred acceptance (DA) is strategy-proof for the proposing side, yet field and lab evidence shows many participants misreport. Ashlagi and Gonczarowski ask whether DA's stable outcome can be implemented in a way that makes truth-telling obvious. They use Li's (2017) notion of obvious strategy-proofness (OSP): a mechanism is OSP if the worst outcome from acting truthfully at a decision node is no worse than the best outcome from lying there. That property can be checked without contingent reasoning, and it belongs to a mechanism, not to the underlying rule (pp. 5–6; footnote 6, p. 3).

Results. If the proposed-to side's preferences are acyclical, the men-optimal stable matching can be implemented by an OSP mechanism (Theorem 2, p. 9). For general preferences it cannot: with at least three agents on each side there is a profile of the other side's preferences, namely three women with cyclical preferences over three men, for which no stable matching rule can be implemented by an OSP mechanism (Theorem 3, p. 10; proved for 3 and 3 in Lemma 1, pp. 10–11). The profile needs only three women and three men inside a larger market, and for large random markets "with high probability no implementation of any stable mechanism is obviously strategy-proof for all men" (introduction, p. 3; Corollary 1, p. 12). The result extends to markets where both sides are strategic (Corollary 2, p. 13). The abstract puts it as "no mechanism that implements a stable matching is obviously strategy-proof for any side of the market" (p. 1).

The discussion draws the lesson: strategic mistakes seen in practice "may not be avoidable by better explaining the mechanism", which "highlights the importance of gaining the trust of the agents", who must act as advised even when it is hard to verify and be "assured that the social planner will not deviate from the prescribed procedure after preferences are elicited" (p. 14). Sequential implementations of DA are not OSP either, though experiments show they get more sincere behaviour; the authors suggest they ease cognitive load by letting participants decide in smaller steps with feedback (p. 14). The "next best thing" may be a mechanism that gives students "a 'feeling' similar to that of OSP mechanisms by not relinquishing control... and by being able to constantly witness that the mechanism is run as promised" (p. 15).

## Relation to the argument

**Premise P8 and its weak point.** The map's weak point for P8 is that matching clearinghouses have no objective of their own and "the removed task relocates". Ashlagi adds a second qualification, formal and not empirical: for the mechanism the field actually uses (stable, DA-type), the removal of the strategic task can be proved but cannot be made obvious, in Li's sense, by any redesign of how the mechanism is run. A rule can remove the task from the person's best-reply problem and still leave the person unable to see that it has. That sharpens the wording "removes a strategic task and shows that it is gone" in the conclusion of the map: for stable matching, the "shows" part has a proved limit.

**Ruling D3.** The paper bears on the author's claim that disclosure is a literacy intervention and not an algorithmacy one. Its own finding is that explanation cannot, for general stable mechanisms, make strategy-proofness obvious. That supports the narrow point that explaining a rule is limited. It does not test disclosure on people, and the OSP standard is a theory of a cognitively limited proof, not an experiment on readers.

**Ruling D8 and premise P10.** The discussion's call for trust that the planner will not deviate after preferences are elicited states, in an economist's terms, the problem of who holds a convention. The planner here is the only party that can break it, and the paper offers no mechanism for holding the planner to account beyond "witnessing" that it is run as promised.

**Facet check:** the facet quotes the abstract correctly and reads the bearing as "formal and not cognitive". The full text shows the claim is about the existence of a bad preference profile, not about every market, and that the paper's own suggestion for cognitive relief is sequential mechanisms, which it says are not OSP either.

## Caution

Pure theory in a stylised one-to-one market with ordinal preferences. OSP is a standard of cognitive simplicity taken from one theory, and a failure of OSP is not shown to cause the mistakes in the field. The arXiv v5 may differ from the published article in wording and pagination.
