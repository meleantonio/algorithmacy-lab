## What it argues

The article explains and assesses the algorithmic-management chapter of the *proposed* Platform Work Directive (Arts. 6–10 of the proposal). Its central point is scope: unlike the employment-status presumption, these provisions cover "all platform workers, including genuinely self-employed persons", except for information and consultation (Art. 9) and health and safety (Art. 7(1)) (abstract, p. 29; p. 37).

The chapter has four layers, which the authors present as a progression beyond information:
- *Information* (Art. 6). Platforms must disclose the use of automated monitoring and decision systems, the categories of decisions, "the main parameters that such systems analyse and their relative weights", and the grounds for account restriction, suspension or termination (p. 38).
- *Monitoring of impact* (Art. 7). "The introduction of mere information rights would not be enough to effectively counter the information and bargaining power asymmetries that exist between people performing platform work and platforms" (p. 39), so platforms must monitor and evaluate the effects of individual decisions.
- *Explanation and human review* (Art. 8), "arguably the most innovative aspect of the PWD" (p. 39). It covers decisions "taken or supported" by automated systems, so "the residual human validation (known as rubber-stamping in the tech jargon) cannot be used to deny that these decisions fall within the remit of ADMS" (p. 39). It "shifts the burden of ex-post legibility to platforms" (p. 40), names a contact person, requires a written statement for account restriction, suspension or termination, and requires a reply to a review request "within one week" (pp. 40–41).
- *Collective channels* (Art. 9). Representatives must be informed and consulted, with a paid expert where a platform has over 500 workers in a Member State; this does not reach the genuinely self-employed (p. 41).

The authors are mixed. They argue that monitoring and decision-making "are neither logically nor chronologically separated" (p. 38), that the chapter adds another piece to a "patchy jigsaw" of GDPR, P2B Regulation and AI Act, and that "it is questionable whether a narrowly construed chapter on algorithmic management within the PWD could succeed in this endeavour" (p. 42). Yet the binding text means platforms' "alibis centring on insidious 'black box' rhetoric ... are no longer credible" (p. 42). They contrast the proposed AI Act, under which workplace systems would face only "an ex-ante internal self-evaluation by the provider" (p. 44). They warn that "the current wording of the PWD is likely to change substantially" (p. 41).

## Relation to the argument

**Premise P12 and ruling D8.** The source supports the paper's §7.3 move from disclosure to structural intervention, and it shows the step was made at the proposal stage: the drafters added impact monitoring, human review, contact persons and collective consultation because information alone was judged insufficient (p. 39). It also complicates the paper's picture of who holds the remedy: the authors locate the stronger remedy in representatives and strategic litigation, and in the right to review, not in any convention at the interface.

**Ruling D3.** Art. 6 is a Target 2/4 disclosure (parameters and weights); Art. 8 is an individual explanation after a decision. The authors expect these to help workers "understand and possibly challenge algorithmically made decisions" (p. 42) and to support reclassification litigation (p. 33). That is a defence of disclosure as evidence for contest, not for navigation, which fits the paper's ruling that disclosure serves a different purpose from steering.

## Caution

Written in mid-2022 on the Commission's proposal; the adopted Directive (EU) 2024/2831 renumbered and changed the chapter, so no article number here can be cited for the final law. A legal commentary with no empirical evidence on whether these rights change outcomes. Its "client" appears only as a party to whom monitoring may be outsourced, so it does not analyse the two-sided triad the paper studies.
