## What it argues
The paper replaces the one-dimensional levels of automation with a two-dimensional frame: "The new goal is to seek high levels of human control AND high levels of automation" (p. 1). Sheridan and Verplank's ten levels are "widely cited, but mind-limiting" (Table 1 caption, p. 2); the levels model and the SAE driving levels both suggest "that increases in automation must come at the cost of lowering human control" (p. 1). Critics of autonomy are listed: ironies (Bainbridge), deadly myths (Bradshaw et al.), conundrums (Endsley); "A common point is that humans have to spend more effort monitoring autonomous computers because they are unsure of what it will do, often leading to inferior performance" (p. 3).

Reliable, Safe and Trustworthy (RST) systems rest on three supports. Reliability comes from technical practices including "Audit trails and analysis tools to review failures & near misses" and benchmark tests; "Reliability is advanced by studying past performances by way of detailed audit trails, often called flight data recorders" (pp. 3–4). Safety comes from management cultures of reporting and review (p. 4). Trust comes from independent oversight structures: professional bodies, regulators, certifiers, auditors and insurers (p. 4). The paper rejects the teammate metaphor: "Computers are not teammates, collaborators, or co-active partners, as Johnson et al. (2014) suggest; computers should support human activities in ways that reduce workload, raise performance, and ensure safety" (p. 5). The framework's quadrants place rapid-action autonomy (airbags, pacemakers) at high automation and low control, human mastery (bicycles, piano) at high control and low automation, and names two dangers, excessive automation (Boeing 737 MAX MCAS, where "its existence was not described in the user manual"; Tesla Autopilot's name) and excessive human control, met by interlocks (pp. 7–10). Recommender systems appear once: their mistakes are "usually less serious" but "malicious actors can manipulate these systems", and "Thoughtful design that improves user control could increase consumer satisfaction" (pp. 5–6).

The "Prometheus Principles" for interfaces are: "consistent interfaces to allow users to form, express, and revise intent", "continuous visual display of the objects and actions of interest", "rapid, incremental, and reversible actions", "informative feedback to acknowledge each user action", progress indicators and completion reports (p. 13). The examples (thermostat, elevator, camera) give operators "a clear understanding of the machine state and their choices" (p. 15). Open problems named: "the de-skilling effects that undermine human skills that may be needed when automation fails, and the difficulty of remaining vigilant when user actions become less frequent" (p. 16).

## Relation to the argument
This is the 2020 statement of human-centred AI from the author of the abstract's Shneiderman (1983) citation, and it shows that the direct-manipulation lineage and the read-to-check lineage are the same author's two answers. The Prometheus Principles (continuous display, rapid reversible actions, immediate feedback) are the abstract's "ecological feedback immediacy" in design-guideline form, so the abstract's third sentence has a current source in the HCI canon. At the same time the paper's reliability and trust supports are audit trails, flight data recorders and independent review (pp. 3–4), which is the same structure as Nadella's "human readable evidence" (`nadella2026models`, P15), stated by an HCI authority four years before. That supports the abstract's second sentence: when the field reaches for assurance it reaches for a record someone reads.

On the dyad the paper cuts against the abstract in an unusual way: it keeps the operator–tool pair on purpose, rejecting the teammate framing (p. 5), and it treats the social third parties as oversight structures around the pair, not as a party inside the interaction. The one platform case, recommenders, is a single user and a system plus "malicious actors"; two counterparts coordinating through the recommender are not in view. For D7 and P9, "rapid, incremental, and reversible actions" with "informative feedback" is the author's own condition for learning by doing, confined to a device the user controls.
