# Facet Y — Strategic offloading: strategy-proof and obviously strategy-proof mechanisms

## What the facet covers

The abstract's last sentence lists "strategic offloading (Li, 2017)" as one of three means by which a design change might let people "decide within irreducible triads". The facet tests that phrase. It asks what Li's obviously strategy-proof (OSP) mechanisms do and how well the lab evidence holds up (Q1); whether people stop strategising once a rule is strategy-proof, and what presentation changes the residue (Q2); whether anyone before the author has framed a strategy-proof rule as moving strategic work from the person to the mechanism, and whether the phrase "strategic offloading" has a prior printed meaning (Q3); and what happened when real mediators adopted strategy-proof or simpler rules: Boston's schools, eBay's proxy bidding, Google's ad auctions (Q4).

Seventeen new cards were written from full or partial reads, and three existing cards (`li2017obviously`, `gonczarowski2023strategyproofness`, `reesjones2018experimental`) carry an "Also used by facet Y" section. Full texts are saved under `dxd_foundation_local/pdfs/Y/`.

## Findings by question

### Q1. Li (2017): the definition, the rationale, the lab evidence, and the follow-ups

**The definition and rationale.** The existing card (`li2017obviously`) has the definition from the working paper. Li's own 2024 restatement adds the piece the author needs: an OSP mechanism is one in which "truth-telling can be seen to be dominant from a certain kind of partial description" that "omits how the outcome depends on unobserved opponent moves" (`li2024designing`, p. 8). The rationale is cognitive and about trust: "Participants have to see for themselves that it is incentive-compatible; the mechanism has to be simple. Simplicity bypasses the need for participants to trust the mechanism designer" (p. 3). Li now distinguishes three strategic burdens, contingent reasoning about others, planning one's own future moves, and reasoning about others' beliefs, and OSP addresses only the first; chess has an obviously dominant strategy that is not simple (pp. 4, 9). He concedes two caveats in footnotes: the ascending-clock advantage may be presentation (fn. 5) and the 93% against 64% serial-dictatorship gap may be loss aversion (fn. 8).

**The lab evidence and its replication.** Breitmoser and Schweighofer-Kodritsch replicated Li's 2P–AC comparison with his materials (66–72 subjects per format) and split the move into three steps. Of the reduction in deviations from truthful bidding after round three, about 49% came from showing an ascending clock after sealed bids, 12% (not significant) from live bidding on the clock, which is the step that creates OSP, and 39% from live drop-out information; "Theoretical obviousness therefore fails our stronger test" (`breitmoser2022obviousness`, pp. 3–4). Overbidding above €1 developed "almost exclusively in 2P" (about 40% of bids against under 20% elsewhere, p. 9). Their conclusion: "obviousness is really a matter of what one gets to see... not how one formally gets to decide" (p. 18), and "running two practice runs may be considered a substitute for implementing an OSP mechanism in our case" (fn. 17). They did not replicate the serial-dictatorship pair.

**Follow-ups.** *Pycia and Troyan* (existing card `pycia2023theory`): OSP is weaker than it looks; strong OSP requires no foresight. *Bó and Hakimov*: with TTC and SD, truthful play was Direct 35% / PAO 55% (cyclic TTC), Direct 56% / PAO 67% / OSP 77% (acyclic TTC), Direct 72% / PAO 75% / OSP 98% (SD) (`bo2024pickanobject`, Table 3, p. 31); within OSP TTC, 93% truthful when the dominant path only required clinching (N = 836) and 56% when it required passing (N = 620), "not significantly different from truthful rates under direct TTC (p=0.94)" (Table 4, p. 33). *Ashlagi and Gonczarowski* (existing card): stable matching is not OSP-implementable in general. *Zhang and Levin* 2017 (AER P&P 107(5), 235–239): abstract only; it proposes "Obvious Preference" and "Obvious Nash Equilibrium" as axiomatic foundations (Crossref record; AEA PDF restricted). *Katuščák and Kittsteiner* 2025 (Management Science 71(9)): abstract only; a new description of TTC raised truth-telling (no open copy). *Troyan and Morrill* 2020 (JET 185, 104970): abstract only; defines obvious manipulations as the mirror of obvious dominance (no open copy). *Gonczarowski, Heffetz and Thomas* (existing card): menu descriptions, theory; the behavioural companion is `gonczarowski2024describing`.

**Net.** The OSP label has not survived as the active ingredient. What repeatedly moves truth-telling is the shape of the person's decision (pick an object; clinch) and what the person sees (a clock, rivals dropping out, a step-by-step rejection). That is closer to the abstract's "representational effect" and "feedback immediacy" than to a property of the incentive structure.

### Q2. Misreporting under strategy-proof rules, and what presentation reduces it

**Lab.** Chen and Sönmez: truthful revelation was BOS 0.139 / GS 0.722 / TTC 0.500 (designed environment) and 0.278 / 0.556 / 0.431 (random); "roughly 80% of the participants under Boston, 53% under TTC and 36% under Gale–Shapley misrepresent" (`chen2006school`, Table 4 and p. 217). Hakimov and Kübler's survey: the BOS < DA result "was replicated in almost all subsequent studies"; information about others lowers truth-telling even under DA and TTC, so incomplete-information rates are "an upper bound" (66.7% in DA in Pais and Pintér); 85% truthful under TTC fell to 31% once subjects were told others might misreport (Guillen and Hakimov 2017); reversed mechanisms show some truth-telling is a default rather than understanding (Guillen and Veszteg) (`hakimov2021experiments`, pp. 444–449). Learning raises DA truth-telling (65 → 77% over 20 rounds; 38% → 56%), and longer lists lower it (pp. 449–451). Rees-Jones and Skowronek (existing card): 23.3% of 1,714 recent NRMP applicants submitted a suboptimal list in an incentivised replica.

**Field.** Israel: of 672 relevant lists, 130 (19.3%) ranked an unfunded contract above or instead of the funded one, after advice "on multiple occasions", a FAQ, a lecture video and a pop-up warning; 3 to 10 were costly (more than $5,000 average); 18% of 2014 respondents still believed there was a strategic advantage and another 21% believed there was none but could not say why; misrepresentation fell with ability and desirability (`hassidim2021limits`, pp. 11–21). Hungary: 11% of 528,172 eligible lists (27% of relevant lists) contained a dominated choice, 12.3–18.7% of those costly at about $6,600 each, in a system that publishes cut-offs so "applicants can verify that their assignment is indeed the option they ranked highest"; dominated choices were more frequent where cheaper (selective programs, +3.4 points per SD of cut-off), which the authors read as ruling out cognitive limits "as a sole determinant" (`shorrer2023dominated`, pp. 2–4, 8–13). NRMP survey: 17% non-truthful, 5% avowedly strategic, after tutorials and a website citing the Nobel prize (`reesjones2018suboptimal`, pp. 8–11). The P&P review adds Denver and New Orleans parents who "were told to provide the full number of choices in their true order of preference" but "few parents actually trusted this message" (`hassidim2017mechanism`, p. 222).

**What presentation does.** Three kinds of change have measured effects. (a) *Form of the decision*: dynamic DA raised truth-telling over static DA in Bó and Hakimov (significantly) and Klijn et al. (55% against 47%, not significant); the survey attributes the gain to "the feedback it provides after every step... and the possibility to re-strategize" (`hakimov2021experiments`, p. 468); posting tentative cut-offs after each step raised truthful behaviour further (p. 469); continuous feedback on the would-be allocation raised stable outcomes from 83% to "almost 99% of markets" (Stephenson 2016, p. 469). (b) *Advice*: effects range from −20 points (third-party advice under TTC, even when correct) to +13 (an instructor's advice) and +19 (advice to be truthful under DA), with "the disclosure of the mechanism reduc[ing] the rate of truth-telling among a subsample" in one field setting (pp. 453–454); Masuda et al. (existing card) found a net +23.8 points from announcing strategy-proofness in a Vickrey auction. (c) *Disclosure of mechanics*: in a truthful belief-elicitation rule, showing the exact payoff table doubled false reports (41.5% against 20.6% with no quantitative information; a calculator helped less; per-period payoff feedback raised false reports from 21.7% to 34.2% within ten periods; a verbal description preserved truth-telling at 24.5%) (`danz2022belief`, Table 2, pp. 12–17). The Gonczarowski et al. companion (existing card) found a short statement of the property helped where teaching the algorithm did not.

**Net.** Strategy-proofness removes most but not all strategising, and the residue is systematic (weaker applicants, higher competition, longer lists, more information about others). Removing it further has worked through the shape and sequence of the decision and through feedback, inconsistently through advice, and in one clean test disclosure of the mechanics made it worse.

### Q3. Has anyone framed a strategy-proof rule as offloading strategy from the person to the mechanism? Does "strategic offloading" have a prior printed use?

**The phrase.** "Strategic offloading" is an established term in cognitive psychology. It is the title phrase of Gilbert (2015), four online experiments (N = 1,196) in which people chose to externalise delayed intentions by moving cues in the environment, "strategic" meaning adaptive to memory load and distraction (`gilbert2015strategic`, pp. 1, 5). Later uses keep that sense: "strategic reminder setting" (Engeler and Gilbert 2020, PLOS ONE 15(10); abstract only), "Strategic offloading: How the value of to-be-remembered information influences offloading decision-making" (Murphy 2023, Applied Cognitive Psychology 37(4); abstract only), and Gilbert et al. (2020, JEP: General 149(3); abstract only). In every case the agent who offloads is the person, and what is offloaded is a memory or monitoring demand, not a game-theoretic burden. Risko and Gilbert (2016), already carded, is the review of this line. No source in economics found this round uses the phrase.

**Li's own framing.** Li does not use "offload". His 2017 paper speaks of agents who "cannot reason contingently" and of mechanisms whose dominance is "obvious"; his 2024 survey speaks of "simplicity", of "the cognitive cost of participation, which is just as real as other costs", and of simplicity that "bypasses the need for participants to trust the mechanism designer" and "can level the playing field" (`li2024designing`, p. 3). The nearest statement to the author's framing in the facet is Vickrey's, quoted by Li: in a second-price auction "Each bidder can confine his efforts and attention to an appraisal of the value the article would have in his own hands, at a considerable saving in mental strain" (p. 4). Bó and Hakimov state the behavioural contrast as "Wait until you can pick your best feasible object" against "Pick your best feasible object and wait to see if you can keep it" (`bo2024pickanobject`, p. 3). Rees-Jones and Shorrer write that "making strategy-proofness easy to see was a clear design goal even in the earliest stages" (`reesjones2023behavioral`, p. 47), and the Boston superintendent's memo lists what families "do not have to factor into their decision-making" (`abdulkadiroglu2006changing`, p. 27). Rees-Jones puts the design's promise as letting students "entirely avoid devoting time or effort into figuring out how they should misrepresent their preferences" (`reesjones2018suboptimal`, pp. 2–3).

**Net.** The idea that a strategy-proof rule takes strategic work off the person is present throughout market design, in the vocabulary of simplicity, mental strain, levelling and "not having to strategize". The label "strategic offloading" is the author's, and it collides with a prior printed term that means something else. The abstract's citation "(Li, 2017)" attaches a phrase to a source that does not use it; it should cite Li for the mechanism and either coin the term as the author's own or cite the offloading literature and mark the transfer of sense.

### Q4. Mediators that adopted strategy-proof or simpler rules, and what happened to strategising

**Boston schools, 2005.** The School Committee voted unanimously to replace the Boston mechanism with student-proposing DA after the designers showed that families strategised and that others were hurt by not doing so: of 391 elementary students who ranked two overdemanded schools first and second with only random priority at the first, 29% were unassigned, and "a majority of all unassigned students" had such lists (`abdulkadiroglu2006changing`, pp. 17–18). The superintendent's stated reason: "to enable families to list their true choices of schools without jeopardizing their chances"; the old rule gave "an advantage to families who have the time, resources and knowledge to conduct the necessary research" (p. 24). DA was preferred to TTC partly because TTC "is less transparent... which may perpetuate the need or perceived need to 'game the system'" (p. 26). The designers warned that "The advantages of strategy proofness would be lost if it is not effectively communicated" (p. 27). Under the manipulable rule the strategic work had been pushed onto parents by the system's own advisers: Minneapolis told parents to make "the first choice a true favorite and the other two 'realistic,' that is, strategic choices" (`chen2006school`, p. 204). No post-reform behavioural data were found this round; the field residue under DA elsewhere is in Q2.

**eBay proxy bidding.** eBay runs a second-price rule with proxy bidding and told users to "bid the absolute maximum they are willing to pay for an item right from the start and let the proxy bidding system work for them" (`ockenfels2006late`, fn. 1, p. 298). Users sniped anyway: 13% of last bids on eBay came in the last five minutes against 1% on Amazon, whose soft close removes the advantage; half of eBay auctions against 3% of Amazon auctions (both categories pooled) had their last bid in the final five minutes; experience raised late bidding on eBay (p = 0.008) and lowered it on Amazon (p = 0.059) (Table 1, p. 313; p. 315). The device meant to offload strategy did not remove it; a change to the closing rule did, measurably.

**Google ad auctions.** The generalized second-price auction, which carried over 98% of Google's 2004 revenue, "generally does not have an equilibrium in dominant strategies, and truth-telling is not an equilibrium" (`edelman2007internet`, abstract), while Google's advertising implied otherwise (Li 2017, §1; `li2024designing`, p. 3). In 2019 Google Ad Manager moved display inventory to "a unified first price auction", stating that layered auctions had "driven our publishers and app developers to implement increasingly complicated ad monetization strategies" and that under the new rule a bid "will not... be able to set the price for another buyer"; it added that buyers and sellers "will need to... adjust how they bid" (`cox2019simplifying`). Akbarpour and Li explain the direction: a strategy-proof direct mechanism "reveal[s] too much" to an auctioneer who can cheat, so "The first-price auction is the unique credible static mechanism. The ascending auction is the unique credible strategy-proof mechanism" (`akbarpour2020credible`, pp. 425, 428). The move was toward a rule users can police, not toward relief from strategy.

**Net.** Of three mediators, one (Boston) adopted a strategy-proof rule to stop users strategising and said so; one (eBay) offered an offloading device whose users learned to game timing instead, until a rule change elsewhere (Amazon) removed the incentive; one (Google) moved away from the strategy-proof family for the mediator's own credibility and told users to re-strategise. The mediator's interests decided the rule in all three.

## What this means for the abstract's sentence

The sentence: *"I argue we should look for a similar 'smoking gun' design change that would free algorithmacy from literacy—enabling people to decide within irreducible triads through epistemic action (Kirsh & Maglio, 1994), strategic offloading (Li, 2017), and ecological feedback immediacy (Shneiderman, 1983; Kahneman & Klein, 2009)."* Facet Y bears on the middle term and, through it, on the other two.

| Claim in or behind the sentence | Verdict | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A design change in the mediator's rule can relieve people of strategic work (the sense of "strategic offloading") | **Supported in a narrower form** | Strategy-proof rules cut manipulation from about 80% to 36–53% in the first lab test (`chen2006school`) and Boston adopted DA for exactly this reason (`abdulkadiroglu2006changing`). But 11–23% of people keep strategising in the field after advice and tutorials (`hassidim2021limits`, `shorrer2023dominated`, `reesjones2018suboptimal`, `reesjones2018experimental`), the residue is systematic, and in part calculated rather than confused (`shorrer2023dominated`). The task is removed for the person only when the person can see it is gone. |
| Li (2017) is the source for that move | **Supported for the mechanism, cuts against for the phrase** | Li supplies OSP and the trust-bypassing rationale (`li2024designing`, p. 3). He does not use "offloading"; "strategic offloading" has a prior printed meaning in cognitive psychology, the person's own choice to externalise a memory demand (`gilbert2015strategic`; Murphy 2023; Engeler and Gilbert 2020). The citation as written attaches the author's label to a source that does not carry it. |
| OSP is the design change that does the freeing | **Cuts against** | With Li's own materials, 49% of the gain came from showing a clock after the fact and 39% from seeing rivals drop out; the OSP step contributed 12%, not significant (`breitmoser2022obviousness`). Within one OSP mechanism truth-telling was 93% when the person only had to clinch and 56% when the dominant path needed foresight, the latter no better than the direct mechanism (`bo2024pickanobject`, Table 4). Li concedes both points (`li2024designing`, fnn. 5, 8). What frees the person is the shape of the decision and what is shown, which is the sentence's first and third terms, not an incentive property. |
| The three terms (epistemic action, offloading, feedback immediacy) are distinct means | **Narrower: they overlap in the evidence** | The gains attributed to dynamic mechanisms come "through the feedback it provides after every step... and the possibility to re-strategize" (`hakimov2021experiments`, p. 468); continuous feedback on the would-be allocation raised stable outcomes to about 99% of markets (p. 469); Breitmoser's clock presentation is a representational effect. In the sources, "offloading" works by the other two. |
| Disclosure of the rule does not free people (ruling D3, implied by contrast) | **Supported, with one qualification** | Full disclosure of a truthful rule's payoffs doubled false reports (`danz2022belief`); describing the mechanism lowered truth-telling in one field study and correct third-party advice lowered it in one lab study (`hakimov2021experiments`, pp. 453–454); Denver parents disbelieved the instruction (`hassidim2017mechanism`). Qualification: advice from a trusted source raised truth-telling by 13–24 points (Guillen and Hakimov 2018; `masuda2022net`), and a short statement of the property helped where the algorithm did not (`gonczarowski2024describing`). "Which disclosure, from whom" is the live question, not "whether". |
| The design change is one the mediator will make | **Cuts against, for an interested mediator** | Every strategy-proof or OSP result above comes from a clearinghouse or experimenter with no stake. Where the mediator has a stake, a strategy-proof direct rule hands it the information to cheat (`akbarpour2020credible`); eBay kept the hard close under which its experienced users snipe (`ockenfels2006late`); Google left the strategy-proof family to restore its own credibility and told users to re-strategise (`cox2019simplifying`). The sentence's "irreducible triads" have a mediator that "binds and optimizes", and the facet found no study of such a mediator adopting a rule that frees its counterparties. That gap stands from round 4 (argument map, open problem 1). |

Two recommendations follow for the draft. First, rename or re-source the middle term: either "strategic simplicity (Li, 2017; Li, 2024)" or keep "offloading" and cite Risko and Gilbert (2016) and Gilbert (2015) for the term while saying the sense is transferred. Second, when the paper reaches for the "smoking gun", the evidence points at the pick-from-a-menu form and step-by-step feedback (Bó and Hakimov; Stephenson; Breitmoser) rather than at OSP; the abstract's own first and third terms are where the measured effects sit.
