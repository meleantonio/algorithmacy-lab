# P — Triad experiments: what the section 8 experiment must build on or answer

*Facet P, 2026-10-13. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

*Audited 2026-10-13 (AUDIT_11.md); WRONG and OVERSTATED rows corrected in place.*

**What this facet covers.** The experiment in section 8 of the author's outline: two human participants coordinate an exchange through an algorithmic mediator that has its own objective, under four conditions (opaque baseline; full textual disclosure; a strategy-proof presentation; continuous real-time feedback), with behavioural outcomes, and with integrated information (Φ) used to gauge whether the triad is irreducible to two person–mediator pairs. I looked for (Q1) experiments with two or more humans and an algorithmic or institutional intermediary; (Q2) any experiment that varied what participants were told about a mediator's rule and measured coordination or surplus; (Q3) measures of irreducibility applied to groups, and what they can and cannot show; (Q4) the standards the design must meet (preregistration, power at the triad level, software, deception); (Q5) how coordination and market experiments define their outcome measures.

**The question the synthesis answers.** What prior work does the section 8 experiment have to build on, where does that work cut against the design, and what should the outline add?

**Lab position I worked from** (`../../../../CLAUDE.md`, "Dissertation guiding structure"): the lab uses Φ to explore whether a coordination form is irreducible, computed on small Boolean models; it has measured no human participants. I do not call Φ decorative or unnecessary, and I do not claim it is validated for human triads. Where I say what is untested, I mean untested in the sources I opened.

## (b) Entries

### B1. Experiments with an intermediary or mediator between humans

**casella2025fragility** — Casella, A., Friedman, E., & Perez Archila, M. (2025). On the fragility of mediation: Theory and experimental evidence. *Journal of the European Economic Association, 24*, 801–850. https://doi.org/10.1093/jeea/jvaf049. *Verification:* Crossref. *Read status:* **read in part** by the auditor from the authors' own working-paper PDF (version of 2 November 2024, linked from Evan Friedman's web page; the JEEA version was not opened, the OUP page returned HTTP 403): abstract, §1, §2 model, §4 experimental design. Page numbers not recorded; the JEEA abstract is worded slightly differently from the working paper's.
- *Design (abstract).* "an experiment where two subjects, uncertain about each other's strength, negotiate how to share a resource. The subjects send cheap talk messages to one another (under direct communication) or to the computer mediator (under mediated communication), before expressing demands or receiving the mediator's non-binding recommendation." The mediator is a "theoretically optimal mediation algorithm".
- *Result (abstract).* "While messages to the mediator are more sincere, we find that peaceful resolution is not more frequent."
- *Design (working paper, §3 and §4).* Pairs of subjects, randomly rematched each round; 12 subjects per session, six sessions per parametrisation (q = 1/2 and q = 1/3), so 144 subjects in 12 sessions; each subject plays 20 rounds of unmediated communication (UC) and 20 rounds of computer mediation (CM), in counterbalanced order. The mediator's objective is "to maximize the probability of agreement" (§2); that is the same as maximising ex ante efficiency. Subjects were told how the mediator works: "The mediator's program relevant to the parametrization used in the session was projected on the lab screen during instructions and remained on the screen throughout all rounds of the treatment" (§4), and the paper calls it "a commonly known algorithm" (§1). The manipulation is mediation against direct communication, not disclosure against non-disclosure.
- *Bearing.* This is the nearest design to section 8 that I found, and it is a two-human, algorithm-mediated experiment with a behavioural outcome (agreement). It has no disclosure manipulation: the mediator's rule is shown in full in every mediated round. The mediator's objective is the pair's agreement, not an objective of its own. It cuts against any expectation that putting a well-designed algorithm in the middle will by itself raise coordination. Its theory section also says why: the optimal mediator works by obfuscating each party's report, and the result is fragile to any insincerity.

**duffy2016coordination** — Duffy, J., Lai, E. K., & Lim, W. (2017). Coordination via correlation: an experimental study. *Economic Theory, 64*, 265–304. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00199-016-0998-8 (Crossref lists the volume as 2016/2017). **Abstract only.** Subjects in Battle of the Sexes with a correlation device; "subjects had an easier time coordinating on the efficient correlated equilibrium with a direct rather than an indirect device", where a direct device phrases messages as actions. *Bearing:* a mediator that recommends actions is an old design in experimental economics, and *how the recommendation is phrased* changed coordination. That supports a presentation arm; it does not involve disclosure of the device's rule.

**cason2006recommended** — Cason, T. N., & Sharma, T. (2007). Recommended play and correlated equilibria: an experimental study. *Economic Theory, 33*, 11–27. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00199-006-0155-x. **Not opened; title and authors verified at Crossref.** Listed because the title names the design (a mediator recommending play to two humans). I make no claim about its results.

**tessler2024ai** — Tessler, M. H., Bakker, M. A., Jarrett, D., et al. (2024). AI can help humans find common ground in democratic deliberation. *Science, 386*, eadq2852. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adq2852. **Abstract only.** "we trained an artificial intelligence (AI) to mediate human deliberation ... Participants (N = 5734) preferred AI-generated statements to those written by human mediators". *Bearing:* an AI mediator, many humans, behavioural outcome (preference, updated views). The mediator is built to find common ground, so its objective is again the group's. Size reference only: N = 5,734 is an online deliberation study, not a triad design.

**park2026mediating** — Park, H. J. (2026). Mediating under the shadow of AI: public reactions to procedural and substantive roles of AI in court-annexed mediation. *Computer Law & Security Review, 61*, 106323. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clsr.2026.106323. **Abstract only.** Web experiment, "1000 participants"; "global (rule-level) explanations improved accuracy and acceptance only when paired with procedural AI, whereas local (case-specific) explanations backfired when combined with human-authored proposals." *Bearing:* a rare case that varies a *rule-level* explanation of an AI in a mediation setting and measures acceptance. The participants are single respondents reviewing a scenario, not two parties interacting, and the outcome is acceptance and satisfaction, not surplus. It also shows the effect of explanation depends on the role the AI plays, which the section 8 conditions do not currently vary.

**normann2023human** — Normann, H.-T., & Sternberg, M. (2023). Human-algorithm interaction: Algorithmic pricing in hybrid laboratory markets. *European Economic Review, 152*, 104347. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2022.104347. *Verification:* Crossref. *Read status:* **read in part** from the Max Planck discussion paper 2021/11 (version of April 2022): abstract, §2–§4; results tables not read.
- *Design.* Groups of three form a market. In "Human" treatments all three are human; in "Algorithm" treatments "there are two human players and one algorithm", which is "an n-player generalization of tit-for-tat" (abstract). A second factor tells participants whether an algorithm is present ("Certain") or only that "with a probability of 50%, one of the three subjects' decisions is taken by an algorithm" ("Uncertain") (§2). 309 participants, 16 sessions, four per treatment, 2019–2020, Düsseldorf and Bonn (§4).
- *Result (abstract).* "When one of three firms in a market is an algorithm, we observe significantly higher prices compared to human-only markets ... (Un)certainty about the actual presence of an algorithm does not significantly affect collusion, although humans seem to perceive algorithms as more disruptive."
- *Bearing.* Two humans plus an algorithm in a group of three, with disclosure of presence manipulated and a behavioural outcome. But the algorithm is a rival that plays for a third firm, not a mediator between the two humans, and what varies is *whether* an algorithm is there, not what its rule is. For section 8 it shows that, in pairwise tests, telling people whether an algorithm is present produced no statistically significant difference in cooperation (Result 2; four sessions per treatment). The paper's ordered-alternative test across the four treatments was significant (Result 3), and the algorithm's presence itself raised cooperation in the Uncertain treatments (Result 1). In the Algorithm treatments the third firm's human seat is inactive and is paid the algorithm's payoff (§2), so the 309 participants include inactive seats. The algorithm follows a fixed, simple rule (proportional tit-for-tat), so "its own objective" is just a fixed strategy. The EER abstract matches the discussion-paper abstract on the disclosure result.

**ishowooloko2019behavioural** — Ishowo-Oloko, F., Bonnefon, J.-F., Soroye, Z., Crandall, J., Rahwan, I., & Rahwan, T. (2019). Behavioural evidence for a transparency–efficiency tradeoff in human–machine cooperation. *Nature Machine Intelligence, 1*, 517–521. https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-019-0113-5. *Verification:* Crossref. *Read status:* **read in part** (accepted manuscript; abstract, design, results, discussion; supplementary notes not read).
- *Design.* A repeated prisoner's dilemma, "at least 50 rounds", against a human or a reinforcement-learning bot (S++); 698 MTurk participants, in a 2×2 of true nature by stated nature: "170 were accurately informed that their partner was human, and 180 were inaccurately informed that their partner was a bot", and likewise for bots (PDF p. 3).
- *Result.* "bots do better than humans at inducing cooperation, but that disclosing their true nature negates this superior efficiency" (abstract). "The magnitude of this effect was about 10 percentage points" (PDF p. 7). A debiasing message to 190 participants, "Data suggest that people are better off if they treat the bot as if it were a human", left behaviour "essentially the same" (PDF p. 8).
- *Bearing.* The clearest experimental case in which disclosing *what the counterpart is* lowered the surplus of the pair. It is a dyad (one human, one bot), and the participants were told false things in two cells, which the no-deception norm of experimental economics would not allow (see Q4). It supports the position that disclosure is not a free good, and it complicates any reading that more disclosure helps.

**grossklags2006software** — Grossklags, J., & Schmidt, C. (2006). Software agents and market (in)efficiency: a human trader experiment. *IEEE Trans. Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, Part C, 36*, 56–67. https://doi.org/10.1109/tsmcc.2005.860575. **Abstract only.** "common knowledge about the presence of software agents triggers more efficient market prices when the programmed strategy was employed, whereas an effect of the information condition on behavioral variables could not be observed. When controlling for information on software agents' participation, the introduction of software agents results in lower market efficiency". *Bearing:* disclosure of presence and an efficiency outcome in a double auction; the result depends on the agent's programmed strategy. Humans trade with each other and with agents; the agents are not mediators.

**jacobleal2024algorithmic** — Jacob-Leal, S., & Hanaki, N. (2024). Algorithmic trading, what if it is just an illusion? *J. Behavioral and Experimental Economics, 112*, 102240. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socec.2024.102240. **Abstract only.** No algorithm is actually present; "the information about the presence of AT, employing MM strategy, is sufficient to alter subjects trading behavior". *Bearing:* what participants are *told* about an algorithm's presence and type changes behaviour even when nothing is there. This is relevant to an opaque baseline: the story participants form about the mediator is itself a treatment.

**li2024how** — Li, L., Zheng, et al. (2024). How the introduction of machine traders and disclosure of their presence affect prediction accuracy. *Int. J. Electronic Commerce, 28*, 569–600. https://doi.org/10.1080/10864415.2024.2401948. **Abstract only.** "simply disclosing the presence of machines can have a detrimental impact on prediction performance, as it may lead to a reduction in human deliberation efforts". (Crossref lists two authors, Li and Zheng; the abstract was confirmed at Semantic Scholar.)

**asparouhova2024humans** — Asparouhova, E., Bossaerts, P., Cai, et al. (2024). Humans in charge of trading robots: the first experiment. *Review of Finance, 28*, 1215–1244. https://doi.org/10.1093/rof/rfae007. **Abstract only.** Participants deploy robots themselves (delegation); "Participants earn more provided they combine robot and manual trading." Counter-delegation design, held in spirit by facet H.

**dong2025coordination** — Dong, Y., Gao, et al. (2025). Coordination in a two-sided market entry game with endogenous capacity. *Experimental Economics, 28*, 576–599. https://doi.org/10.1017/eec.2024.2. **Abstract only.** A laboratory game "operated by an intermediary monopoly platform, serving two sides"; "coordination failure and disequilibrium occurred" in one treatment. *Bearing:* the only laboratory two-sided platform game that surfaced in queries 6 and 10. The platform is a payoff structure, not an agent with an objective.

**erlei2020impact** — Erlei, A., Nekdem, F., Meub, L., et al. (2020). Impact of algorithmic decision making on human behavior: Evidence from ultimatum bargaining. *HCOMP 2020, 8*, 43–52. https://doi.org/10.1609/hcomp.v8i1.7462. **Read in part** by the auditor (AAAI open-access PDF, 10 pp.: abstract, design, Table 1, results on proposers and responders). Authors: Erlei, Nekdem, Meub, Anand, Gadiraju. "1178 participants"; users of a decision-support system make better decisions, but "People who negotiate with a user who has a decision support system, but cannot use one themselves, react to its introduction by demanding a better deal for themselves, thereby decreasing overall cooperation levels"; "Explanations increase the number of unique system inquiries, but users appear less willing to follow the system's recommendation." *Design:* a one-shot ultimatum game on Mechanical Turk between a proposer and a responder; the decision-support system (DSS) is trained on earlier bargaining data and recommends the offer that maximises the proposer's gain (the recommended offer is an even split); treatments vary whether the proposer is told that the responder knows of the DSS, whether the proposer gets an explanation of the DSS, and whether the responder is told that the proposer can use the DSS and gets the same explanation (Table 1). *Bearing:* two humans, an algorithm on one side, a surplus-type outcome, and both a presence-disclosure and an explanation manipulation, so it is a two-human experiment with a disclosure manipulation, but not a mediated one. Result for responders: learning that the proposer has a DSS led about 40% to raise their minimum offer; an explanation of the DSS reduced the upward revision (t(208) = 2.84, p = 0.005) and the share calling the DSS unfair, but responders still asked for more than before and rejection rates rose (20% to 24.7%, p = 0.058). The algorithm advises one party; it is not between them.

**vonschenk2023social** — von Schenk, A., Klockmann, V., & Köbis, N. (2023/2025). Social preferences toward humans and machines: A systematic experiment on the role of machine payoffs. *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 20*, 165–181. https://doi.org/10.1177/17456916231194949. **Abstract only.** "more than 160 studies ... have reported on how people cooperate with machines"; "the implementation of the machine payoffs and the information participants receive about them differ drastically across these studies"; with N = 1,198, "the extent to which humans cooperate with machines depends on the implementation and information about the machine's earnings". *Bearing:* what the participants are told about what the machine *gets* changes how they treat it. For a mediator with "its own objective", this is the most direct warning: how the mediator's objective is described is a treatment, and the field has no standard for it.

### B2. Experiments that varied what participants were told about a rule or property

**masuda2022net** — Masuda, T., Mikami, R., Sakai, T., Serizawa, S., & Wakayama, T. (2022). The net effect of advice on strategy-proof mechanisms: an experiment for the Vickrey auction. *Experimental Economics, 25*, 902–941. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10683-021-09736-5. *Verification:* Crossref. *Read status:* **read in part** (open PDF: abstract, §1, §2.2, §3.5, Tables 1 and 10).
- *Design.* "Three bidders compete for two units"; between-subjects 2×2 of auction rule (Vickrey; pay-your-bid) by advice (yes/no); 11 sessions at Osaka, 21 or 24 subjects each, z-Tree; Table 1 gives 63 (21 groups), 48 (16), 69 (23) and 72 (24) subjects (groups) for VA, VN, PA, PN (§2.2). The advice, read aloud and on paper, was: "You can maximize your earnings by bidding your valuations as they are, regardless of what others bid." (§2.2, p. 908). The authors added "a careful note" asking subjects to consider "whether this advice is true or false" so that advice that is false in the pay-your-bid auction involves no deception (§1 footnote 5; §2.2).
- *Results.* "although the rate of sincere bidding is 20.6% without advice, it increases to 46.9% once subjects are advised regarding the strategy-proofness of the Vickrey auction" (§1.1, p. 904). Efficiency "is taken from Kagel and Levin (2009)": the efficiency ratio is the sum of the two winning units' valuations over the sum of the two highest of the six valuations (§3.5, p. 918). "Providing advice improves efficiency in the Vickrey auction, particularly in the former 13 periods" (Result 5, p = 0.020, §3.5).
- *Bearing.* This is the nearest answer to Q2, and it goes against the strong form of the author's ruling D3. A one-sentence true statement about a property of the rule (a dominant strategy exists) changed what people did and raised efficiency modestly in a three-party auction. The efficiency gain is small and the measure sits near the ceiling: 0.977 with advice against 0.967 without across all periods (Table 6; p = 0.020, Result 5), significant in periods 1–13 (p = 0.039) and not in periods 14–25 (p = 0.143); the net effect against the pay-your-bid control is significant only at the 10% level (p = 0.051, Result 6). A published Correction (Exp. Econ. 26:249–250) says an incorrect version of the electronic supplementary material was uploaded during proofing; it concerns the supplement, not the article text. Limits: the "mediator" is an auction rule with no objective of its own, the statement is about a property and not the rule's text, the advice is the experimenter's, and the sample is Japanese undergraduates. Also: the net effect is measured against a pay-your-bid control to separate understanding from demand effects, which is the control logic the section 8 arms lack.

**guillen2018effectiveness** — Guillén, P., & Hakimov, R. (2018). The effectiveness of top-down advice in strategy-proof mechanisms: A field experiment. *European Economic Review, 101*, 505–511. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroecorev.2017.10.020. **Abstract only** (Consensus record; no open copy found). Three information treatments for a Top Trading Cycles allocation: a description of the mechanism, a description of strategy-proofness, both. "We find a significant and positive effect of describing the strategy-proofness on truth-telling rates. On the other hand, describing the matching mechanism has a negative effect." *Bearing:* the same intermediary, two different disclosures, opposite effects. This qualifies "full textual disclosure" as a single arm: a description of the rule and a description of its guarantee are different treatments.

**fiala2017transparency** — Fiala, L., & Suetens, S. (2017). Transparency and cooperation in repeated dilemma games: a meta study. *Experimental Economics, 20*, 755–771. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10683-017-9517-4. **Abstract only** (open PDF in hand, body unread). 71 voluntary-contribution studies and 18 oligopoly studies. "transparency about what everyone in a group earns reduces contributions to the public good, as well as the degree of collusion in oligopoly markets. In contrast, transparency about choices tends to lead to an increase in contributions and collusion." *Bearing:* the direction of a transparency effect depends on *what* is made transparent; "disclosure" is not one variable. Only the humans' own information is varied there, not a mediator's rule.

**vonschenk2023social**, **ishowooloko2019behavioural**, **normann2023human**, **li2024how**, **grossklags2006software**, **jacobleal2024algorithmic**, **erlei2020impact**, **park2026mediating**: see B1; each varies *presence, identity, payoffs or explanation* of a machine. None varies the text of a mediator's rule for two parties.

**Data-disclosure field experiments (platform context).** **tadelis2015information** — Tadelis, S., & Zettelmeyer, F. (2015). Information disclosure as a matching mechanism: theory and evidence from a field experiment. *American Economic Review, 105*, 886–905. https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20110753. **Abstract only.** Randomly disclosing quality information in wholesale auto auctions; "both good and bad news ... increase competition and revenues". **huang2022managing** — Huang, N., Burtch, G., He, et al. (2022). Managing congestion in a matching market via demand information disclosure. *Information Systems Research, 33*, 1196–1220. https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2022.1148. **Abstract only.** A randomized field experiment on a dating platform; disclosure of peers' demand "particularly effective at improving matching efficiency when presented in tandem with a textual message-framing cue". *Bearing:* a platform's *disclosure of data* has moved matching efficiency in two field experiments; both disclose what the market looks like, not how the platform decides.

### B3. Integrated information, synergy and irreducibility measures

**engel2018integrated** — Engel, D., & Malone, T. W. (2018). Integrated information as a metric for group interaction. *PLOS ONE, 13*(10), e0205335. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0205335. *Verification:* Crossref. *Read status:* **read in full** (19 pp., open access).
- *What it does.* Three applications of an empirical Φ to groups. Study 1: 68 groups of four, each completing a battery of tasks (face-to-face or text chat, §Methods p. 10); the state vector records which members are speaking in 200 ms steps for face-to-face groups; for chat, each line is one time step and "the group member who chatted is encoded as 1 (for active)" (p. 10–11). The measure is Barrett and Seth's "empirical phi" (FE), with a minimum information bipartition (Eq. 2). Result: "phi was significantly correlated with the measured collective intelligence of the groups (r = 0.370, p = 0.003)" (p. 5). Study 2: Wikipedia editors per article, phi higher for higher-quality articles (F = 3.6847, p = 0.0053; p. 6). Study 3: internet traffic over six years.
- *What the authors say about the measure.* "Phi was originally developed by neuroscientists as a measure of consciousness in brains, but it captures, in a single mathematical quantity, two properties that are important in many other kinds of groups as well: differentiated information and integration" (abstract). "there is still considerable debate among researchers about whether phi actually measures consciousness" (p. 2). "in order to apply the theoretical definitions of phi, a complete model of the rules governing state transitions in the system is needed. Since such models are rarely available for observational data, however, we used here two alternative versions of phi suggested by Barrett and Seth [23]" (p. 3). "we assume, but do not test for, stationarity of the time series of state vectors" (p. 11). "it is certainly possible that other metrics would have predictive power similar to that of phi and be simpler to compute" (p. 9). They describe phi as "a potential metric of effective group collaboration" and say it "at least sometimes, predicts group performance" (abstract).
- *Bearing.* This is the only application of Φ-type measures to human groups that I found with a behavioural outcome, and the authors present it as a predictor of group performance, not as a test of irreducibility. The state variable is *who is active*; the mediator in a section 8 triad has to be coded as a third unit with its own state, which the paper does not do. The correlation is across 68 groups, observational, with phi for the face-to-face groups computed at a time lag (2 s) chosen from the peak of average phi (Fig. 1); the chat groups used a lag of one message. They did not compare it to a pair-factorisation baseline.

**albantakis2023integrated** — Albantakis, L., Barbosa, L., Findlay, G., et al. (2023). Integrated information theory (IIT) 4.0. *PLOS Computational Biology, 19*, e1011465. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011465. *Read status:* **read in part** (PDF pp. 1–10: abstract, the substrate and TPM passages, the definition of irreducibility). IIT's formalism "assume[s] a complete description of the substrate, which means that we can determine the conditional probabilities in (2) for every system state", with the interventional form "do(u)", so "U must correspond to a causal network" and "T^U is a transition probability matrix" (PDF p. 8). "The TPM ... forms the starting point of IIT's analysis" (same). The next paragraph adds: "In practice, a causal model will be neither complete nor atomic (capturing the smallest units that can be observed and manipulated), but will capture the relevant features of what we are trying to explain and predict" (same). "Irreducibility is measured by integrated information (φ) over the substrate's minimum partition" (Postulates, PDF p. 5). "In the 'Results and discussion' section, the IIT formalism will be applied to extremely simple, simulated networks, rather than causal models of actual substrates" (PDF p. 8). *Bearing:* the full measure is defined on a causal model with interventional probabilities. A recorded behavioural time series gives observational statistics only; going from one to the other needs a modelling step the sources describe as an approximation or proxy. The lab's Boolean models, in which the transition rule is specified, are the setting the theory is written for.

**barrett2026integrated** — Barrett, A. B., Milinkovic, B., Mediano, P. A. M., Rosas, F. E., Bor, D., Barnett, L., & Seth, A. K. (2026). Integrated information theory: the good, the bad and the misunderstood. arXiv:2604.11482v1. *Read status:* **read in part** (abstract; §5.1.1, §5.1.2, §5.2). A **preprint** by authors who work on and are sympathetic to IIT, so these are admissions from inside, not an outside attack.
- Abstract: "Φ is not well-defined for real physical systems, and has not been computed on any real physical system. Fourth, so far only proxies for IIT measures have been computed, and not approximations."
- §5.1.1 (p. 11): the algorithm "is therefore not well-defined whenever there is at least one non-Markovian graining."
- §5.1.2 (p. 12): "The toy model systems on which the IIT algorithm has been applied are composed of a finite set of binary units that interact with each other and update their states in discrete time, according to a memoryless (Markovian) dynamics." State variables on continuous domains are also out of reach ("The IIT algorithm cannot be applied when state variables are taken to be continuous").
- §5.2 (p. 13): "it would be useful if researchers were clear that empirical work uses proxy Φ measures, and not Φ itself, nor approximations to Φ."
- *Bearing.* The sentence describing the toy systems (finite binary units, discrete time, memoryless dynamics) also describes the lab's Boolean models. The paper says nothing about groups of people; its examples are brains and physical substrates. My own inference, not the paper's: whether human behaviour is Markov at a coarse-graining a researcher would choose is a separate question the paper does not address. The paper's "caveat is needed when interpreting the empirical behaviour of any proxy measure; and validation with models is important" (§5.2) is a recommendation the lab's Boolean corpus is positioned to meet: it can serve as the validation set for whatever proxy is chosen for human data.

**mediano2019measuring** — Mediano, P. A. M., Seth, A. K., & Barrett, A. B. (2019). Measuring integrated information: comparison of candidate measures in theory and simulation. *Entropy, 21*(1), 17. https://doi.org/10.3390/e21010017. *Read status:* **read in part** (arXiv:1806.09373v1: abstract, intro, parts of §2). Six candidate measures were run on eight-node networks with Gaussian linear autoregressive dynamics: "We find a striking diversity in the behaviour of these measures – no two measures show consistent agreement across all analyses. Further, only a subset of the measures appear to genuinely reflect some form of dynamical complexity" (abstract). *Bearing:* "the value of Φ" is not one number: the choice of measure changes the answer, so section 8 must name the measure and the coding before data are collected.

**barrett2011practical** — Barrett, A. B., & Seth, A. K. (2011). Practical measures of integrated information for time-series data. *PLOS Computational Biology, 7*, e1001052. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1001052. **Read in part** (abstract, definitions of W_E and W_AR). "WDM is defined only for discrete Markov systems, which are unusual in biology; as a result, WDM can rarely be measured in practice. Here, we describe two new measures, WE and WAR, that overcome these limitations" (abstract); the same abstract adds that "our findings pose challenges for theories that ascribe physical meaning to the measured quantities." **williams2010nonnegative** — Williams, P. L., & Beer, R. D. (2010). Nonnegative decomposition of multivariate information. arXiv:1004.2515. **Read in part** (abstract, introduction). The partial information decomposition splits the information that sources carry about a target into redundant, unique and synergistic atoms: the abstract says interaction information "suffers from the problem that it is sometimes negative" and that "the negativity of interaction information can be explained by its confounding of redundancy and synergy". *Bearing:* PID needs a designated target. In a mediator triad one could take the next state of one person as the target and the other person and the mediator as sources. It asks a different question from Φ's partition test, and its redundancy measure is one of several competing definitions (the literature I saw lists many; I did not read it).

**rosas2019quantifying** — Rosas, F. E., Mediano, P. A. M., Gastpar, M., & Jensen, H. J. (2019). It "introduces the O-information as a metric capable of characterising synergy- and redundancy-dominated systems" with no source/target split; the demonstration is on Baroque music scores. *Bearing:* the only non-neural application in the abstract is music; I found no application to human group behaviour (queries 22–23).

**gehlen2024bias** — Gehlen, J., Li, J., Hourican, C., et al. (2024). Bias in O-information estimation. *Entropy, 26*, 837. https://doi.org/10.3390/e26100837. **Abstract read** (Europe PMC XML; body not read). "The main finding is that the O-information of independent systems is severely biased towards synergy if the sample size is smaller than the number of jointly possible observations. **rebbin2025inferring** — Rebbin, D., Down, K. J. A., Varley, T. F., Ince, R. A. A., & Canales-Johnson, A. (2025, v2 Aug 2026). **mediano2022strength** — Mediano, P. A. M., Rosas, F. E., Bor, D., et al. (2022). **hanson2021formalizing** — Hanson, J. R., & Walker, S. I. (2021). **charness2022what** — Charness, G., Samek, A., & van de Ven, J. (2022). ## (c) Synthesis by question

### Q1. Existing experiments with two humans and an intermediary

I found no experiment with all of the section 8 elements: two human parties, a mediating algorithm with an objective of its own, a disclosure manipulation, and a surplus or coordination outcome. The sources fall into five groups, each with a different missing element.

1. **Mediators aligned with the parties.** Casella, Friedman and Perez Archila (two humans, a computer mediator that maximises the probability of agreement, a non-binding recommendation, the mediator's rule shown on screen; peaceful resolution not more frequent; read in part from the working paper), Duffy et al. and Cason and Sharma (correlation devices), Tessler et al. (an AI mediator built for common ground, N = 5,734). The mediator's goal is the parties' agreement or welfare.
2. **Algorithms as a third player or rival.** Normann and Sternberg (309 participants, groups of three, an algorithm as the third firm), Grossklags and Schmidt, Jacob-Leal and Hanaki, Li et al., Asparouhova et al.
3. **Algorithms on one party's side.** Erlei et al., Hohenstein et al. (held), Zhu et al. (held), Terrucha et al. (held). Two or more humans with an AI assistant for each or one; the assistant serves a party.
4. **Machine as partner.** Ishowo-Oloko et al. and the review literature behind von Schenk et al. ("more than 160 studies"), March and Chugunova and Sele (not read).
5. **Platforms.** Dong et al. is a laboratory platform game with no agent. Tadelis and Zettelmeyer and Huang et al. are field experiments with disclosed data. Almost all of the platform-fee literature that surfaced (query 10) is theory.

What each study's intermediary optimised: a pricing rule that mimics tit-for-tat (Normann), a bot's repeated-game policy (Ishowo-Oloko), the pair's agreement (Casella), consensus statements (Tessler). None gave the mediator a payoff that differs from the parties' payoffs. **That is the section 8 design's distinctive feature, and it has no precedent among the sources I read.** The outline should say so with the qualifier that March and Chugunova and Sele were not read. Erlei et al.'s advisor does maximise one party's gain, but it advises that party and does not sit between the two.

N and the unit of analysis: Normann and Sternberg, 309 participants and 16 sessions (§4); Masuda, 11 sessions of 21 or 24; Ishowo-Oloko, 698 (dyads); Hohenstein, 219 pairs in Study 1 (held); Zhu, 81 groups (held); Engel and Malone, 68 groups. Triad-level N in lab studies is therefore tens of groups per cell at best.

### Q2. Has anyone varied what participants are told about a mediator's rule?

**No study I opened or saw in an abstract manipulates the text of an intermediary's rule for two parties and measures joint surplus.** The searches are logged (queries 9, 11, 12, 33, 32). What exists is nearby:

- **Presence or identity.** Normann and Sternberg: no significant pairwise effect on collusion (a significant ordered-alternative test across the four treatments). Ishowo-Oloko: disclosure of the bot's identity lowered cooperation by about 10 percentage points. Grossklags and Schmidt (abstract): common knowledge of the agents' presence went with more efficient prices under the programmed strategy, while without that information the agents lowered efficiency. Li et al.: disclosure harmful. Jacob-Leal and Hanaki: belief about presence alone moved trading. Mixed signs; no single direction.
- **A property of the rule.** Masuda et al.: a true one-sentence statement of strategy-proofness raised sincere bidding from 20.6% to 46.9% (Vickrey with against without advice) and raised efficiency by about one percentage point, 0.967 to 0.977 (p = 0.020; the net effect against the pay-your-bid control p = 0.051). Guillén and Hakimov (abstract): describing strategy-proofness helped; describing the mechanism hurt.
- **Explanation of an AI.** Erlei et al.: explanations raised the number of distinct queries but lowered use of the advice for proposers; for responders an explanation reduced, but did not remove, the upward revision of minimum offers. Park (abstract): rule-level and case-level explanations had opposite effects depending on the AI's role.
- **Payoffs of the machine.** von Schenk et al. (abstract): information on the machine's payoffs changed cooperation.
- **Data, not rule.** Tadelis and Zettelmeyer; Huang et al.

So the thing section 8 proposes has not been done, but three results complicate it: what is disclosed matters more than whether anything is (Guillén and Hakimov; Fiala and Suetens), disclosure of identity can cost surplus (Ishowo-Oloko), and in at least one case a short true statement about the rule changed behaviour and, by about one percentage point, efficiency (Masuda). These bear directly on the four arms. "Full textual disclosure" bundles at least three things: that an algorithm is present, what it optimises, and how it decides.

### Q3. Irreducibility measures on groups

**Φ on human groups.** Engel and Malone is the one application I found. It uses a proxy (empirical Φ with a minimum information bipartition), a state coding of who is active, a lag chosen to maximise Φ, and assumes stationarity that it does not test. Its result is a correlation with collective intelligence over 68 groups. Its measure is a minimum-information-bipartition quantity, which the authors describe as information "generated by the system as a whole that is more than just the sum of its parts", but they use it as a predictor of performance and do not test a group's interaction against a reducibility null or a pair-factorisation baseline; it has no mediator.

**PID and O-information on human data.** I found none (queries 22–23). The applications I saw are in neuroscience, physiology and Baroque music (Rosas et al.). The estimation literature warns that for three discrete variables small samples inflate synergy (Gehlen et al.) and that interpretation of synergy outruns the evidence for mechanism (Rebbin et al.).

**What the sources say about the measure.** Albantakis et al. define irreducibility against a transition probability matrix under interventions. Barrett et al. (2026, preprint, from inside the IIT community) say in their abstract that Φ "is not well-defined for real physical systems, and has not been computed on any real physical system" and that "only proxies for IIT measures have been computed, and not approximations", and in §5.1.2 that the toy systems used are "finite ... binary units" with "memoryless (Markovian) dynamics". The paper discusses brains and physical substrates and does not address groups of people, so what its statement implies for a human triad is untested rather than answered. Mediano et al. 2019 find that candidate measures disagree. Barrett and Seth designed their empirical measures for stationary time series. Weak-IIT writing (Mediano et al. 2022, abstract only) offers a pragmatic framing.

**What would be needed to compute any of these on a three-party task (agent's reading of the sources).**
1. A coding of each unit's state (for example, a message sent or not; a choice among a few options; the mediator's output) into a small discrete alphabet, fixed before data collection, with the mediator's state *logged* (the experimenter has this; Engel and Malone's field data did not).
2. A time-step definition and a lag. Engel and Malone picked the lag that maximised Φ (Fig. 1), which should not be copied for a confirmatory test.
3. Enough observations per triad for the joint state space (with three binary units, 8 joint states; with ternary, 27). The O-information bias result says the bias towards synergy is severe when the sample is smaller than the number of jointly possible observations (Gehlen et al., abstract); the abstract does not say what sample size removes it, so null simulations are required.
4. A stationarity check, which Engel and Malone did not run.
5. A named measure, because measures disagree (Mediano et al. 2019).
6. A stated partition of interest: person–mediator pairs against the whole triad, and a comparison to the null of independent pairs. That is a narrower question than "minimum information partition" asks, and the outline should say which one it means.

**What the number can and cannot show.** From the sources: a proxy Φ on a coded time series measures differentiated, integrated dynamics under that coding (Engel and Malone's own description). It has predicted performance in one set of groups. It is not Φ in the IIT 4.0 sense, because that needs interventional transition probabilities (Albantakis et al.; Barrett et al.). By itself it does not show that a condition helps the people in the triad (the same point as T_adjudication §9.2, D9). Two cautions are my inference, not in the sources: activity rate could confound Φ across conditions (the continuous-feedback arm may simply produce more events), and a Φ difference across the four arms would be a description of the recorded dynamics, not a verdict on what the participants understood. The lab's own position (Φ as a principled lens, not a claim of necessity) is compatible with using a pair-factorisation comparison on the same data as a cross-check; I note that as a suggestion for the author and not as a statement of the lab's view.

### Q4. Standards

- **Preregistration.** Both economics (Olken, abstract-level) and HCI (Cockburn et al. 2018, abstract-level) have an explicit literature. [unverified: see AUDIT_11] I cannot report their specifics beyond the abstracts.
- **Unit of analysis and power.** The triad (or session) is the unit. Typical group counts in the sources are 16–24 groups per cell (Masuda, Table 1; Li 2017 held: 9 groups of four per treatment) and 68 groups in Engel and Malone. Bellemare et al. offer simulation-based power but, in what I read, cluster at the individual level. A triad design needs a simulation written for the study, using pilot estimates of between-triad variance.
- **Software.** oTree's live pages allow continuous-time play; Empirica supports artificial players and a bot API. Neither source describes a mediator role. The mediator would be code, and its internal state would have to be logged by the study's own code.
- **Deception.** Under the norm in Charness et al., false statements are deception and omissions mostly are not; scripted computers presented as people are rated among the most deceptive. The opaque arm must therefore say truthfully that an algorithm mediates. Masuda's way of handling advice that is false in a control cell (an explicit "consider whether this is true or false" note) is a model for any control that shows a wrong description.

### Q5. Outcome definitions

- **Efficiency or surplus.** Masuda: realised value over the maximum, from Kagel and Levin (2009), per game, averaged (§3.5); a "binary efficiency" variant (efficient allocation reached or not) also reported. Gode and Sunder: allocative efficiency of a double auction (abstract). Engel and Malone: group "collective intelligence", a first-factor score (p. 10).
- **Cooperation and collusion.** Normann and Sternberg: cooperation rates and prices; Ishowo-Oloko: proportion cooperating per round, with profit correlating with cooperation (r = .52 with humans, .58 with bots, PDF p. 7); Fiala and Suetens: contributions and collusion.
- **Misreport or deviation.** Masuda: frequency of "sincere bidding"; Guillén and Hakimov: truth-telling rate; Rees-Jones (held): 23% misrepresent.
- **Agreement.** Casella et al.: whether the pair reaches peaceful resolution.
- **Convergence time.** Not defined in any source I opened. Devetag (2003, *Experimental Economics 6*, 53–73, https://doi.org/10.1023/a:1024252725591; abstract only, bib key `devetag2003coordination`) mentions "the frequency and speed of convergence" to the payoff-dominant equilibrium; I did not open the paper. [unverified: see AUDIT_11] Section 8 will need its own definition (for example, the first period after which the pair stays within a tolerance of the efficient outcome) and should state it in the preregistration.
- **Mixed signs.** The measures can point different ways in one study (Ishowo-Oloko: identity disclosure lowers cooperation; Normann: no effect on prices). The outline should choose a primary outcome (joint surplus) and list the others as secondary.

### Claims in the paper's position that these sources contradict or qualify

1. **"This experiment tests a configuration nobody has tested."** Qualified. Two-human experiments with a computer mediator exist (Casella et al.; the correlation-device literature). What has no precedent in what I read is a mediator whose objective differs from the parties'.
2. **D3, "disclosure of the algorithm doesn't affect our ability to navigate it."** Qualified by Masuda et al. (a true statement of a property raised sincere bidding from 20.6% to 46.9% and raised efficiency by about one percentage point, p = 0.020), by Guillén and Hakimov (abstract; direction depends on what is disclosed), and by Ishowo-Oloko (disclosure changes outcomes, downward). It is supported by Normann and Sternberg (no significant pairwise effect of knowing about the algorithm) and Hohenstein (exposure alone had no effect, held). The evidence does not say disclosure is inert; it says the effect depends on what is disclosed and how.
3. **"Full textual disclosure" as one condition.** The sources treat presence, identity, a property of the rule, the rule's text, and the mediator's payoffs as different treatments with different signs (Q2). One arm bundles them.
4. **"Continuous real-time feedback" as an improvement.** Friedman and Oprea found about 90% mutual cooperation in continuous time against less than half as much in eight-subperiod controls; Leng et al. found continuous and discrete time not significantly different, with information on all members' efforts raising effort in continuous time only (full against limited feedback within continuous time was not significant). The arm confounds time structure with information unless a discrete-time arm with the same information is added (my suggestion).
5. **"A strategy-proof presentation" as one thing.** In the one field experiment (Guillén and Hakimov, abstract), describing the mechanism lowered truth-telling and describing strategy-proofness raised it.
6. **Φ gauges the irreducibility of any triad (D9).** Qualified. Φ in the IIT 4.0 sense needs a causal model with interventional probabilities (Albantakis et al.). The only application to human groups used a proxy, as a predictor of performance, without a mediator and without a test of reducibility (Engel and Malone). Authors who work on IIT write that Φ "has not been computed on any real physical system" and that only proxies have (Barrett et al. 2026, preprint; the paper concerns brains and physical substrates, not groups). Candidate measures disagree (Mediano et al. 2019). This does not show Φ is wrong for the lab's Boolean models, where the transition rule is specified and the systems are the discrete, memoryless kind the theory is written for. It shows that for human data the claim is untested.
7. **Behavioural outcomes at the triad level.** Group counts in comparable studies are small (16–24 per cell in Masuda; 68 in total in Engel and Malone), and I found no power routine for triad clustering.

### What the outline should add (section 8, with links to 4 and 9)

Each item gives the claim, the two or three sources, and their read status.

1. **Section 8, positioning.** Claim: the experiment has near neighbours, but none gives the mediator an objective of its own or varies disclosure of its rule. Sources: Casella et al. 2025 (read in part, working paper; its mediator maximises agreement and its rule is shown to subjects); Normann and Sternberg 2023 (read in part); Masuda et al. 2022 (read in part); Erlei et al. 2020 (read in part; advisor on one side, with a presence and explanation manipulation).
2. **Section 8, split the disclosure arm.** Claim: presence, identity, the mediator's objective, a property of the rule, and the rule's text are different treatments with different signs. Offer at least a "property" arm (a true one-line statement of what the mediator guarantees) next to the "full text" arm, because the property statement is the one with a documented effect on behaviour (Masuda: sincere bidding 20.6% to 46.9%; efficiency up by about one percentage point, 0.967 to 0.977). Sources: Masuda et al. 2022 (read in part); Guillén and Hakimov 2018 (abstract only); Ishowo-Oloko et al. 2019 (read in part).
3. **Section 8, state and describe the mediator's objective.** Claim: how a machine's payoffs are described changes how people treat it, and the field has no standard. Sources: von Schenk et al. 2023/2025 (abstract only); Jacob-Leal and Hanaki 2024 (abstract only).
4. **Section 8, controls for the continuous-feedback arm.** Claim: continuous time and information shown may be different variables; the two abstracts disagree on whether continuity alone raises coordination, and the within-continuous-time contrast between full and limited feedback was not significant in Leng et al. Sources: Friedman and Oprea 2012 (abstract only); Leng et al. 2018 (abstract only).
5. **Section 8, Φ plan.** Claim: name the measure, the state coding (including the mediator's logged state), the lag and the partition before data collection; say it is a proxy for human data; use the lab's Boolean models as the validation set; plan sample size against the joint state space with null simulations. Sources: Engel and Malone 2018 (read in full); Albantakis et al. 2023 (read in part); Barrett et al. 2026 preprint (read in part); Gehlen et al. 2024 (abstract only); Mediano et al. 2019 (read in part).
6. **Section 8, deception and preregistration.** Claim: an opaque arm is permitted if participants are told an algorithm mediates and only the rule is withheld; controls that present a false description need a Masuda-style note. Preregister the primary outcome (joint surplus) and the definition of convergence. Sources: Charness et al. 2022 (read in part); Masuda et al. 2022 (read in part); Olken 2015 and Cockburn et al. 2018 (abstract-level).
7. **Section 8, outcome definitions.** Claim: define joint efficiency as in Kagel and Levin (via Masuda), the misreport rate against the mediator's best response, and an agreement or convergence criterion fixed in advance. Sources: Masuda et al. 2022 (read in part); Casella et al. 2025 (read in part, working paper); Gode and Sunder 1993 (abstract only).
8. **Section 8, power.** Claim: the unit is the triad (or the session under re-matching); write a simulation-based power analysis. Sources: Bellemare et al. 2016 (abstract and partial); Engel and Malone 2018 (read in full) for a group count benchmark.
9. **Section 4.** Claim: the disclosure literature in settings with an algorithm is mixed in direction; section 4 should not assert that disclosure fails to change behaviour. No source I read manipulates disclosure for a mediator between two humans. Sources: Masuda et al.; Ishowo-Oloko et al.; Normann and Sternberg.
10. **Section 9 (limits).** Claim: Φ for human triads is untested; no PID or O-information application to human groups was found; the experiment cannot show that a convention helps the people in it from Φ alone. Sources: Barrett et al. 2026 preprint; Engel and Malone 2018; Rebbin et al. 2025 (abstract only).
