# M — Acting on algorithms: what the literatures on strategic response, probing, resistance and intent say

*Facet M, 2026-10-13. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

*Audited 2026-10-13 (AUDIT_08.md); WRONG and OVERSTATED rows corrected in place.*

**What this facet covers.** Outline section 5 ("from literacy to algorithmacy"). The paper argues that people come to understand an algorithmic third party by acting through it and taking in the results, and that the conditions for learning this way are often missing. Section 5 asserts that platforms introduce noise and delay "to protect information asymmetries"; the project had found no source showing intent. The facet covers five literatures the first two rounds did not: (Q1) machine-learning models of people who adapt to a decision rule (strategic classification, performative prediction, algorithmic recourse); (Q2) empirical studies of people probing and gaming algorithms; (Q3) resistance and collective sensemaking; (Q4) whether platforms keep rules opaque, noisy or shifting on purpose; (Q5) the counter-literature in which people learn markets and platforms well by experience.

**The question the synthesis answers.** What do these literatures assume, find and claim about a person who acts on an algorithm in order to understand or beat it; where do they support, qualify or contradict the paper's position (ruling D7 in `SOURCE.md`, and the Part 1 line 66 claim that an adaptive intermediary disrupts rapid, unequivocal feedback); and what can honestly be said about intent?

## (b) Entries

### Q1. Models of people who adapt to a decision rule

**hardt2016strategic** — Hardt, M., Megiddo, N., Papadimitriou, C., & Wootters, M. (2016). Strategic classification. In *Proceedings of the 2016 ACM Conference on Innovations in Theoretical Computer Science* (ITCS '16), 111–122. https://doi.org/10.1145/2840728.2840730
- *Verification:* Crossref match. Text read from arXiv:1506.06980 v2 (22 Nov 2015), which may differ from the ITCS version.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (arXiv PDF pp. 1–3: abstract, introduction, Definition 1.1; rest not read). Theory, with experiments on real data.
- *What it argues:* Classification is a sequential game between "Jury" (publishes a classifier) and "Contestant" (changes his input at a cost).
- *Assumption about the person:* Definition 1.1, p. 3: "Contestant (who knows c, h, D, and f), produces a function ∆". The person knows the rule in full, and the cost function is "known to both players" (p. 3).
- *Quotations:* p. 2: "Indeed, concerns of gaming and manipulation are often used as a reason for keeping classification mechanisms secret, which is a major concern in credit scoring". Same page: "Secrecy is not a robust solution to the problem; information about a classifier may leak, and it is often possible for an outsider to learn such information from classification outcomes." Abstract: "Knowing information about the classifier, such individuals may manipulate their attributes in order to obtain a better classification outcome."
- *Bearing:* **Qualifies.** It states the anti-gaming rationale for secrecy in the field's founding paper (supports Q4's finding on stated reasons), and it concedes that outsiders can learn a rule "from classification outcomes", which is the paper's learning-by-acting route. Its person is a perfect best-responder who already knows the rule, so it models neither the learning nor the cost of not knowing.

**perdomo2020performative** — Perdomo, J. C., Zrnic, T., Mendler-Dünner, C., & Hardt, M. (2020). Performative prediction. Preprint, arXiv:2002.06673 v4 (2 Mar 2021). https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06673 (no DOI; ICML 2020 venue not checked)
- *Verification:* arXiv record opened.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (PDF pp. 1–2, 4, 7, 13: abstract, introduction, related work, Definition 3.1, §5.1).
- *What it argues:* A prediction used to decide can change the thing predicted. The paper gives conditions under which repeated retraining converges to a "performatively stable" model.
- *Assumption about the person:* None about knowledge or cost. p. 4: "Unlike many works in this area, our results do not depend on a specific cost function for changing individual features. Rather, we rely on an assumption about the sensitivity of the data-generating distribution to changes in the model parameters." Strategic classification is "a two-player game between an institution which deploys a classifier and agents who selectively adapt their features in order to improve their outcomes" (§5.1, PDF p. 13). Adaptation happens "only after the bank has deployed their classifier".
- *Quotations:* p. 2: retraining is "often considered an undesired — yet necessary — cat and mouse game of chasing a moving target".
- *Bearing:* **Supports the paper's "adaptive intermediary" premise in the decision-maker's frame.** The model builder, not the person, is the learner; the person's side is a fixed response map. It names the loop (rule changes, people respond, rule is retrained) that makes the target move for the person too, but it does not model what a person can learn from it.

**milli2019social** — Milli, S., Miller, J., Dragan, A. D., & Hardt, M. (2019). The social cost of strategic classification. In *Proceedings of the Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency* (FAT* '19), 230–239. https://doi.org/10.1145/3287560.3287576
- *Verification:* Crossref match. Text from arXiv:1808.08460 v2.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (PDF pp. 1–2: abstract, introduction, contributions, model).
- *What it argues:* Making a classifier robust to gaming raises the institution's utility at the expense of the people classified, and the burden falls disproportionately on disadvantaged groups.
- *Assumption about the person:* "The institution publishes a classifier f"; individuals best-respond, though the authors note "it is unlikely individuals actually play best-response solutions" (p. 2).
- *Quotations:* Abstract: "any increase in institutional utility necessarily leads to a corresponding increase in social burden." p. 1: "honest individuals worthy of a positive classification outcome may face a higher bar for success."
- *Bearing:* **Qualifies the paper on who bears the cost.** In this model the cost of defensive opacity or conservatism falls on honest people, not on gamers. It does not address noise or delay, and it assumes a published rule.

**ustun2019actionable** — Ustun, B., Spangher, A., & Liu, Y. (2019). Actionable recourse in linear classification. In *Proceedings of the Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency* (FAT* '19), 10–19. https://doi.org/10.1145/3287560.3287566
- *Verification:* Crossref match. Text from the extended arXiv version (arXiv:1809.06514 v2, Nov 2019).
- *Read status:* **read in part** (PDF pp. 1–2, 16: abstract, introduction, related work, discussion of informing consumers).
- *What it argues:* A model should give each person the ability to change its decision by altering actionable inputs ("recourse"). The authors give tools to test this for linear classifiers.
- *Assumption about the person:* The model is fixed and known to the evaluator; the person acts on actionable features. The authors state that "recourse" is not "transparency" or "explainability" (p. 2).
- *Quotations:* p. 1: "The lack of recourse is often mentioned in calls for increased transparency and explainability in algorithmic decision-making … Yet, transparency and explainability do not provide meaningful protection with regards to recourse." p. 16: "In practice, the potential drawbacks of gaming have not stopped the development of laws and tools to empower consumers with actionable information." p. 16 also: "disclosing the action would benefit both parties" where an action also improves the true outcome.
- *Bearing:* **Supports D3 in a specific form.** Disclosure of the rule does not by itself give a person the ability to change the outcome; what matters is whether the actions open to them flip it. It also shows a mainstream ML position that tells people what to do (an action list) rather than how the model works, which resembles the paper's "specify intent" competency.

**karimi2022survey** — Karimi, A.-H., Barthe, G., Schölkopf, B., & Valera, I. (2022). A survey of algorithmic recourse: Contrastive explanations and consequential recommendations. *ACM Computing Surveys, 55*(5), 1–29. https://doi.org/10.1145/3527848
- *Verification:* Crossref match. Text from arXiv:2010.04050 v2 (1 Mar 2021).
- *Read status:* **read in part** (PDF pp. 1–2, 8: abstract, introduction, §2 definition, opening of §5, §5.1).
- *What it argues:* A unified survey of recourse, separating contrastive explanations (why) from consequential recommendations (what to do).
- *Quotations:* PDF p. 2: "recourse can be achieved by an affected individual if they can understand … and accordingly act … to alleviate an unfavorable situation, thus exercising temporallyextended agency" (text as extracted; the hyphenation is the extraction's). PDF p. 8: "There are, however, a number of implicit assumptions made in existing setups, e.g., that the world dynamics are known and do not change, the predictive (supervised) model is fixed, and that changes only arise due to the actions of the individual seeking recourse. Moreover, in the multi-agent settings considered (with e.g., bank and loan seeker), agents are assumed to act truthfully with no gaming or false reporting of features".
- *Bearing:* **Supports and supplies a vocabulary.** The field's own definition joins "understand" and "act", and its authors list the assumption that the model is fixed as the first thing the field has not relaxed. A drifting, adaptive mediator is outside the recourse literature's settings, which is the paper's case.

**ghalme2021strategic** — Ghalme, G., Nair, V., Eilat, I., Talgam-Cohen, I., & Rosenfeld, N. (2021). Strategic classification in the dark. Preprint, arXiv:2102.11592 v3 (June 2021). https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11592 (no DOI; venue not checked)
- *Verification:* arXiv record opened.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (PDF pp. 1–3: abstract, introduction, results overview; proofs and experiments not read).
- *What it argues:* Relaxes the assumption that the classifier is known. Users learn an estimate of the rule and game that. The authors define a "price of opacity" and show it can be large.
- *Quotations:* Abstract: "Under the assumption that the classifier is known, rational agents respond to it by manipulating their features. However, in many real-life scenarios of high-stake classification (e.g., credit scoring), the classifier is not revealed to the agents, which leads agents to attempt to learn the classifier and game it too." p. 1: "Currently known results for strategic classification rely on a key assumption: that users have full knowledge of the classifier and respond accordingly". p. 2: "This partial knowledge is a result of a second, smaller-scale learning process, this time of the users themselves, based on information aggregated by media platforms or by their own social network." p. 2: "rather than preventing gaming, opacity leads users to attempt to learn the classifier and game it too." p. 3: the loans experiment shows an opaque policy "can disproportionately harm users having few social connections".
- *Bearing:* **Supports the paper's premise and qualifies D3.** It models users inferring a hidden rule from other people's observed outcomes (m samples labelled by the rule), which is learning by observing, not by acting, and finds that the route is imperfect and that the error can cost qualified users; the price of opacity it defines is the firm's loss of accuracy. In its model the transparent policy is the recommended one where the price of opacity is positive (abstract). That cuts against a flat "disclosure does not affect our ability to navigate" (D3); the model's disclosure is a full, stationary rule, which is not the platform case.

**bechavod2022information** — Bechavod, Y., Podimata, C., Wu, Z. S., & Ziani, J. (2022). Information discrepancy in strategic learning. Preprint, arXiv:2103.01028 v4 (10 Feb 2022). https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01028 (no DOI; venue not checked)
- *Verification:* arXiv record opened.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (PDF pp. 1–3: abstract, introduction, contributions).
- *What it argues:* People infer a hidden rule from peers, so groups hold different information. In equilibrium, a rule optimised for overall improvement can make some groups worse.
- *Quotations:* p. 2: "Models in the strategic learning literature, however, typically make a full transparency assumption—that is, the agents fully observe the deployed scoring or decision rule". p. 2: "agents learn by observing others within their communities." Abstract: "the true quality of some of the groups can actually deteriorate."
- *Bearing:* **Qualifies the paper (Q3).** What a person learns by acting depends on whom they can observe, and the distribution of that peer information can produce unequal outcomes under one rule. Theory plus experiments on credit datasets; no human subjects.

**hardt2023algorithmic** — Hardt, M., Mazumdar, E., Mendler-Dünner, C., & Zrnic, T. (2023). Algorithmic collective action in machine learning. Preprint, arXiv:2302.04262 v3 (7 Aug 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04262 (no DOI; venue not checked)
- *Verification:* arXiv record opened.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (PDF p. 1: abstract and opening of the introduction).
- *What it argues:* A collective that pools participants' data and instructs them how to modify it can steer a platform's learning algorithm. Theory, plus more than two thousand BERT-style training runs on resumes from a gig platform.
- *Quotation:* Abstract: "algorithmic collectives of exceedingly small fractional size can exert significant control over a platform's learning algorithm."
- *Bearing:* **Qualifies (Q3).** It is the reverse of the paper's direction: people act on the algorithm, as a group, to change it, rather than to understand it. The result is from a simulated collective on one task, not an observed worker action.

### Q2. People probing and gaming algorithms

**petre2019gaming** — Petre, C., Duffy, B. E., & Hund, E. (2019). "Gaming the system": Platform paternalism and the politics of algorithmic visibility. *Social Media + Society, 5*(4). https://doi.org/10.1177/2056305119879995
- *Verification:* Crossref match.
- *Read status:* **abstract only** (Crossref abstract, paraphrased; full text behind a bot wall). Qualitative: textual analysis of 105 news articles and platform user guidelines (Google, Facebook, Instagram), per the abstract and the search summary.
- *What it reports (abstract, paraphrase):* Platforms call tactics such as search-engine optimisation and automated engagement boosting "gaming the system". The line between illegitimate manipulation and legitimate strategy is blurry and mostly serves the platforms' interests; the moral framing presents platforms as neutral guardians. The authors call this "platform paternalism".
- *Bearing:* **Qualifies Q4, from the creators' side.** "Gaming" is a platform's label, not a neutral behaviour. If the paper treats gaming as the user's attempt to understand, this source says the platform decides which probing is allowed. It is evidence of a moral framing, not of intent to keep asymmetries.

**bishop2019managing** — Bishop, S. (2019). Managing visibility on YouTube through algorithmic gossip. *New Media & Society, 21*(11–12), 2589–2606. https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444819854731
- *Verification:* Crossref match.
- *Read status:* **abstract only** (Crossref abstract, truncated by me at about 900 characters). Qualitative: ethnography of beauty vloggers and industry stakeholders.
- *What it reports (abstract):* Defines "algorithmic gossip" as "communally and socially informed theories and strategies pertaining to recommender algorithms, shared and implemented to engender financial consistency and visibility". Community talk "informs and supports practices such as uploading frequently and producing feminised beauty content".
- *Bearing:* **Qualifies (Q3).** Knowledge of the algorithm is socially produced; the abstract does not say whether the gossip is correct or tested. No measured outcome.

**lee2015working** — Lee, M. K., Kusbit, D., Metsky, E., & Dabbish, L. (2015). Working with machines: The impact of algorithmic and data-driven management on human workers. In *Proceedings of CHI '15*, 1603–1612. https://doi.org/10.1145/2702123.2702548
- *Verification:* Crossref match. Author copy at `minlee.ischool.utexas.edu`.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (findings on assignment, surge pricing and online sensemaking; discussion; limitations; methods skimmed). **Qualitative**: 21 Uber and Lyft driver interviews, 12 passenger interviews, 128 forum posts (Methods).
- *Quotations:* Findings, assignment: a driver who knew that "Lyft's assignment algorithms take into consideration how long drivers have been online" turned his app off and on at traffic signals to avoid distant requests; "this information was not publicly made available to all drivers" and drivers without it "attributed the distant assignment to the error of the assignment system, or drivers with higher ratings getting priority. These drivers could not create workaround strategies". Findings, surge: "More than half of interviewed drivers, however, were not influenced by surge pricing information … Surge pricing changed too rapidly and unexpectedly to utilize the information in a strategic way to boost their incomes. Surge areas were on and off, sometimes by the second". Discussion: "online driver forums became a primary avenue for the driver socialization and system sensemaking … On the other hand, sensemaking activities around assignment algorithms and surge pricing seemed less successful in terms of informational usefulness." Discussion: social sensemaking "followed the form of 'fragmented social sensemaking' where there were many active contributors but no central authority figure … This type of sensemaking was effective for discussing rating improvement strategies where there were no right or wrong answers … On the other hand, fragmented social sensemaking fell short on subject matters where only an authority figure had the right information."
- *Bearing:* **Qualifies D7 and Part 1 line 66 in one study.** Drivers learned the parts that return stable, checkable feedback (ratings, how to behave) and did not learn the parts that flicker (surge) or are hidden (assignment). Shared knowledge helped in the first case and failed in the second. This is the closest card-level evidence for "learning by acting works where feedback is stable and fails where it is fast-changing or hidden". It does not measure anything, and the drivers had a mean tenure of 149 days (Methods).

**chen2015peeking** — Chen, L., Mislove, A., & Wilson, C. (2015). Peeking beneath the hood of Uber. In *Proceedings of the 2015 Internet Measurement Conference* (IMC '15), 495–508. https://doi.org/10.1145/2815675.2815681
- *Verification:* Crossref match. Author copy at `ccs.neu.edu/home/amislove/publications/Uber-IMC.pdf`.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (PDF pp. 1–2 abstract and introduction; §5 on surge update timing and jitter, PDF pp. 8–9; discussion/conclusion pp. 12–13). **Measured**: four weeks of data from 43 emulated client apps in San Francisco and Manhattan.
- *Quotations:* PDF p. 1: "Uber is a black-box: they do not provide data about supply or demand, and prices are set dynamically by an opaque 'surge pricing' algorithm." PDF p. 1: "Surge prices are extremely noisy: the majority of surges last less than five minutes." PDF p. 2: "Prices update every 5 minutes", and since April 2015 prices served to users "do not always match the prices returned by the Uber API"; "We reported this finding to Uber, and their engineers confirmed that this behavior was caused by a bug in their system." Same page: "the vague, changing aspects of the algorithm impacts drivers' ability to predict fares." PDF p. 9: "In August 2015, we contacted Uber … they quickly determined that jitter was being caused by a consistency bug in their system. The manifestation of this bug was that random customers could receive stale surge multipliers." PDF p. 13: "the existence of this 'jitter' bug serves as a cautionary tale: as the complexity of algorithmic systems increases, they are more likely to exhibit unintended behaviors."
- *Bearing:* **Contradicts "noise is introduced to protect asymmetries" in the one case where noise was measured and traced.** The noise the authors measured (jitter, 20–30 seconds, stale multipliers shown to one client at a time) was reported by Uber's engineers to be a consistency bug. The 5-minute update clock is a design choice; the authors report that prices correlate with supply, demand and wait time over the previous interval (p. 2) but give no reason for the clock and no motive either way. They also report that jitter almost always lowered the price riders saw (PDF p. 9). It also supplies the measured delay and noise facts that section 5 needs (surges mostly last under five minutes; "surge prices cannot be forecast in advance"). The caveat is that Uber's statement is Uber's own account to the researchers, not an independent finding.

### Q3. Resistance and collective sensemaking

**kellogg2020algorithms** — Kellogg, K. C., Valentine, M. A., & Christin, A. (2020). Algorithms at work: The new contested terrain of control. *Academy of Management Annals, 14*(1), 366–410. https://doi.org/10.5465/annals.2018.0174
- *Verification:* Crossref match. NSF Public Access Repository copy with the journal's running head and pagination. Journal page = PDF page + 365.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (pp. 372, 384, 387–388, 391–392: the opacity passages, the "Algorithmic opacity" insight, "Algoactivism"). A review, not original data.
- *What it argues:* A labour-process review of algorithmic control in six mechanisms ("6 Rs"); workers resist through "algoactivism".
- *Quotations:* p. 372: "algorithms can be opaque, for three main reasons: intentional secrecy, required technical literacy, and machine-learning opacity (Burrell, 2016)." pp. 387–388: "employers using algorithmic rewarding often keep their algorithms secret to discourage manipulation and ratings inflation, which gives workers limited transparency into why work is rejected or why they are guaranteed pay at sometimes and not others." p. 384: "In this view, information asymmetries are not random: instead, they are deliberately created by employers to constrain workers' choices and control workers' ability to contest the distribution of surplus value (e.g., Felstiner, 2011; Howcroft & Bergvall-Kåreborn, 2019)." p. 391: workers "may reverse engineer the algorithm that produced the rating to be able to prioritize the activities that seem to impact the score (Jharver et al., 2018; Lix & Valentine, 2019; Rahman, 2017)"; Airbnb hosts "participated in online forums, read the company's technical documentation, and monitored competitors' profiles and ratings". p. 392: in online communities "workers have been shown to help each other learn new systems and practices, anticipate or avoid disciplinary processes, regain access when locked out of platforms".

## (c) Synthesis

### Q1. What the machine-learning models assume about the person, and what they say about transparency, gaming and cost

Strategic classification starts from a person who knows the rule. In Hardt et al. (2016, arXiv v2 p. 3, Definition 1.1, which the authors call an "idealized" full-information game) the Contestant "knows c, h, D, and f" (in their statistical game, Definition 1.4, the Contestant "knows c and f"), and Ghalme et al. (2021, p. 1) say the literature "rel[ies] on a key assumption: that users have full knowledge of the classifier and respond accordingly". Performative prediction (Perdomo et al. 2020) drops the person's cost and knowledge from the model and keeps only how much the population's distribution moves when the model moves; the learner it studies is the institution, retraining. Recourse work (Ustun et al. 2019; Karimi et al. 2022) takes the opposite stance on the person: it asks whether an action exists that flips the decision, assumes a fixed model, and lists "the predictive (supervised) model is fixed" among the "implicit assumptions made in existing setups" (Karimi et al., arXiv v2 p. 8; that arXiv text carries a different title from the Computing Surveys article, and I did not open the journal text). Its section 5 then relaxes other assumptions (non-deterministic recourse, non-supervised models, groups instead of individuals); in the pages I checked it does not relax the fixed-model one. The two papers that relax the full-knowledge assumption (Ghalme et al.; Bechavod et al. 2022) make the person infer the rule from other people's observed outcomes (Ghalme et al.: m samples labelled by the rule, from media or the person's network; Bechavod et al.: a peer dataset of unmodified samples), not from the results of the person's own acts. That is a formal model of learning by observing others, adjacent to the paper's "act and take in the results" route and not the same. Their result is that this route works imperfectly and unequally: a small error in the person's estimate "can potentially be enlarged" by strategic response into a larger region of misclassified qualified people (Ghalme et al., p. 2), and groups with different peer information can end up with different outcomes under one rule, some of them worse off (Bechavod et al., abstract, which also reports that in many natural cases improvement can be guaranteed for all groups). The "price of opacity" Ghalme et al. define is the firm's loss of accuracy; they give a sufficient condition for it to be positive (Theorem 2), and separately show that qualified users in the enlarged region lose (Theorem 3).

On transparency, the field is split. The founding paper records the anti-gaming reason for secrecy and doubts that secrecy is robust (Hardt et al., p. 2). Ghalme et al. give a sufficient condition for a positive price of opacity (the firm's accuracy loss) and call transparency "the recommended policy" where it holds. Ustun et al. say that transparency and explanation "do not provide meaningful protection with regards to recourse" (p. 1), because even simple transparent models can lack recourse (immutable features, drastic required changes); they also say simple transparent models "allow users to check the feasibility of recourse" (p. 2) and that consumers should be given actionable information (p. 16). These are different claims, and the last supports the author's D3 in a form the paper can use: disclosure of how a rule works is not the same as an ability to change its verdict. On cost, Milli et al. (2019) show that when a decision-maker makes a published rule more cautious about gaming, honest people worthy of a positive outcome "may face a higher bar for success" (p. 1) and that the burden can fall disproportionately on disadvantaged groups. Their model has no hidden rule. None of these sources models noise, delay or rule drift. They model one rule, deployed once or retrained, and a person whose response is cheap to compute. They are theory and simulation on credit-like datasets, with no human subjects in the papers I read.

### Q2. Probing and gaming: measured versus qualitative

Almost everything on how people probe is qualitative. Lee et al. (2015, 21 driver interviews), Bishop (2019, ethnography), Petre et al. (2019, text analysis), Cameron and Rahman (2022, ethnography), Rahman (2019, one platform), Choi et al. (2023, 14 interviews) and Heiland (2023, 35 interviews plus the author's own paired trials) describe what people do and believe. The measured items are fewer: Chen et al. (2015) measured the platform's behaviour, not the user's; Cook et al. (2021) and Jiang and Sinchaisri (2025) measured experience effects; Cen et al. (2024) measured the effect of a belief about the algorithm on behaviour [unverified: see AUDIT_08]. The pattern across the qualitative studies is consistent. Drivers and freelancers do experiment (Rahman, PDF p. 128: "obsessing over their scores and trying various methods to take apart and understand the algorithm"). What they learn is what returns steady, checkable feedback: how to raise a rating (Lee et al.). What they do not learn is what flickers or is hidden: assignment and surge (Lee et al.), the weighting of a score (Rahman). Chen et al. put numbers on the rider-side signal (they emulated the Client app, not the Partner app that drivers use): "the majority of surges last less than five minutes" (p. 1), 90% of jitter events last 20–30 seconds (p. 9), and surge prices "cannot be forecast in advance" (p. 2). Lee et al. describe surge as of September 2014 and Chen et al. date the jitter to April 2015, so Chen's jitter bug cannot explain what Lee's drivers reported. Where couriers did correct a false theory, it took a controlled contrast or a manager's testimony (Heiland, from facet I), not repetition.

"Gaming" is itself contested terrain. Petre et al. read the word as a platform's moral label, finding the line between illegitimate manipulation and legitimate strategy "nebulous and largely reflective of" platforms' "material interests" (abstract only; the practices studied are search-engine optimisation, reciprocal linking and automated engagement-boosting, not users' attempts to understand a rule). Bishop's "algorithmic gossip" names the opposite flank: communally and socially informed theories that the author calls "algorithmic expertise" and a "valuable resource" (abstract only; the abstract does not say whether the theories are tested). None of the three measured outcomes for the people involved.

### Q3. Resistance and shared knowledge

Shared knowledge changes what one person can learn by acting in three ways, each with a source. First, it supplies what acting cannot: Lee et al. report that one driver (P2) who knew a Lyft assignment rule (the pickup radius grows with wait time) used it, and that drivers without it blamed assignment errors or higher-rated drivers; the source of P2's knowledge is not stated, and the authors did not check the rule. Forums were effective for rating strategies and failed "on subject matters where only an authority figure had the right information". Second, it is unequal: Bechavod et al. and Ghalme et al. show in models that the quality of a person's peer information produces unequal outcomes, and Ghalme et al.'s loans experiment finds that an opaque policy "can disproportionately harm users having few social connections". Third, it is not always right: Bishop and Kellogg et al. describe communal theories and reverse engineering, with no test of accuracy in the parts I read. Kellogg et al. (p. 392) list what forums do: workers "help each other learn new systems and practices". Cameron and Rahman add that workers' everyday interactions with customers can "influence and manipulate algorithms in ways that platforms cannot always observe", and Hardt et al. (2023) show in theory and in experiments on resume data that a very small fraction of participants can steer a learner. For the paper, the unit that learns is often the community, not the individual who acts, and an individual's act is also a contribution to what the platform learns about the community.

### Q4. Intent: what can and cannot be said

What can be said, with sources: (1) One primary platform statement gives an anti-gaming reason for an undisclosed rule: StarWorks' help page, as Rahman quotes it (2019, PDF p. 100; I did not see the page itself). Scholars repeat the reason without independent platform evidence: Hardt et al. (2016, p. 2) call it a reason "often used" and cite Citron and Pasquale (2014); Kellogg et al. (2020, pp. 382, 387) state it as a fact, and on p. 382 support it with the same Rahman (2019) and Dourish (2016) sources; Bambauer and Zarsky (2018, pp. 15, 45) analyse it and add that firms treat algorithms as trade secrets "for multiple reasons". (2) The stated reason is to protect the measure. Rahman reports it as the platform's aim in his own voice but adds a second aim in the same sentence (the platform "hid its algorithm to prevent gaming of the system and maintain a stratified body of freelancers whose ratings were controlled solely by the platform", PDF p. 128); he says he has no "further insight into StarWorks' motives" and offers two possible reasons that concern platform interests (clients pay and matter more than freelancers; classifying people "from within" without their knowing how, PDF pp. 128–129); and he calls strategic opaqueness a source of "competitive advantage" (PDF p. 47). Cameron and Rahman give competitive advantage as the reason platforms keep algorithms secret, and Petre et al. read the gaming boundary as reflecting platforms' material interests (abstract only). The sources therefore offer several reasons (anti-gaming, trade secrecy, differentiating workers, client priority) and test none. Rahman's conclusion that "too much transparency contributes to gaming behavior" (PDF p. 147), with its remedy of "intermittent, limited disclosure", is his judgement; in the passages I read I found no comparison with a transparent platform. (3) Bambauer and Zarsky describe what designers can do against gaming: introduce "more randomness so that the incentives and ability to game the algorithm are reduced" and change the model often so that weights are "less known or predictable" (p. 14); reduce transparency so that it is harder to "ping" the system (p. 15). They add that some of these are "innate features of machine learning algorithms anyway" (p. 15) and, in footnote 55, that noise is also used against overfitting. This is analysis of options, not evidence that a named platform does it, and the randomness they mean is in the model, not in the feedback shown to users. (4) One ethnography reports a platform that withheld criteria, gave vague information and changed a feedback section without notice, and interprets this as deliberately manufacturing uncertainty (Rahman, PDF p. 144; the unannounced change was to the client's feedback section). (5) Economic theory gives conditions under which hiding and obfuscation raise profits (Ellison and Ellison; Gabaix and Laibson) and a conceptual vocabulary that separates intentional from inadvertent friction (Sunstein). (6) A labour-process view holds that asymmetry is "deliberately created by employers" (Kellogg et al., p. 384, citing two works I did not open).

What cannot be said: that any platform introduces noise or delay in order to protect an information asymmetry. No source I opened shows that. The one case where noise was measured and the platform answered, Uber's jitter, was a bug according to Uber's engineers, as the authors report it (Chen et al., pp. 2, 9, 13; that is Uber's own account), and jitter "almost always reduces prices for passengers" (p. 9). The 5-minute surge clock is the platform's design: Chen et al. report that prices update every 5 minutes and correlate with supply, demand and wait time over the previous interval (p. 2), but give no reason for the clock and no motive either way. No source I opened treats delay as a design choice. The sources on deliberate hiding concern withholding of the rule and criteria, not noise or delay as such; the difference matters because a hidden rule can in principle be learned by acting, while a noisy or delayed signal cannot be. Intent also has an evidential problem the sources share: it is inferred from conduct and stated reasons, since the platform's internal reasoning is not available (Cameron and Rahman say as much about their data). Economic theory and the sludge literature say intent is plausible and sometimes demonstrated in other markets; they do not show it here. The defensible wording for section 5 is that platforms keep rules undisclosed, and often changing, for stated anti-gaming reasons, and that some analysts read that as deliberate control; that the effect on a person is the same whatever the intent (Sunstein's point that "inadvertent or clueless" sludge can be as harmful); and that noise and delay in the signals are documented (by driver reports in Lee et al., by measurement in Chen et al.), while the one cause traced, the jitter, was a software bug; the cause of the 5-minute clock and of the flicker Lee's drivers reported is not given in any source I opened.

### Q5. Counter-literature

There is a counter-literature, and it names its conditions. Experience eliminates the endowment effect in repeated, legible markets (List 2003, abstract only). Experienced traders sit closer to equilibrium in laboratory markets (Anderson and Sunder 1995, abstract only). On platforms, Uber drivers with more than 2,500 lifetime trips earn about 14% more per hour than drivers with fewer than 100 (Cook et al. 2021; NBER Working Paper 24732 per the project card, not the journal text), experienced delivery workers improve and depart from the recommendations (Jiang and Sinchaisri 2025, abstract only) [unverified: see AUDIT_08], and new eBay sellers do worse than experienced ones (Góes et al. 2013, abstract only). The conditions the sources name or imply are: a fixed, public pay formula with no tenure term (Cook et al., from the project card), repetition, and feedback that says clearly what happened. Three results go the other way. Learning curves can be inflated by attrition (Seru et al. 2010, abstract only, on individual investors); Cook et al. test this by restricting the curve to drivers with at least 1,000 trips or six months and report the same shape (project card), so Seru's caution is a question to put to Cook et al. and to Jiang and Sinchaisri, not a finding about them. Retail forex traders show "no evidence ... learn to trade better" though they "learn about their innate abilities" and cut back after a bad day (Hayley and Marsh 2016, abstract only), and Taiwanese day traders' aggregate performance is negative while the unprofitable ones quit (Barber et al. 2019, abstract only); neither abstract mentions the speed of feedback. Ben-David et al. (2018; read in part) document evidence "consistent with" retail forex traders attributing random success to skill and raising risk, in an environment they describe as "very noisy" with "immediate feedback" (working paper, p. 3). This sharpens the paper's conditional. Fast feedback is necessary but not sufficient; the signal also has to carry information about the person's own action.

### Claims in the paper's position that these sources contradict or qualify

1. **"Platforms introduce noise and delay to protect information asymmetries" (outline §5).** Not supported. The one primary platform statement gives protection against gaming (StarWorks, via Rahman 2019, PDF p. 100); the scholars repeat it (Kellogg et al., pp. 382, 387; Hardt et al., p. 2), while Rahman (PDF pp. 47, 128–129) and Cameron and Rahman also give competitive advantage, stratification and platform interest. The only measured noise was a bug (Chen et al., pp. 2, 9). The deliberate-hiding sources concern the rule and its weights, not noise or delay.
2. **"The platform keeps the rule hidden to keep an advantage over the user."** Qualified. The advantage reading is an interpretation (Kellogg et al., p. 384, "In this view"; Rahman's inference of "manufacture uncertainty" and his suggested platform-interest reasons, PDF pp. 129, 144); the platform's stated reason is anti-gaming, and Rahman concludes that full transparency would invite gaming. Neither reading is tested.
3. **"Disclosure does not affect our ability to navigate" (D3, as the outline will state it).** Qualified by Ghalme et al. and Bechavod et al., whose models make rule disclosure matter. Supported in a narrower form by Ustun et al.: disclosing how the rule works does not guarantee an ability to change its verdict (they also say simple transparent models help users check recourse). Part 1's own evidence (facet H) is that disclosure raises awareness and has not been shown to change behaviour; these sources do not settle that on real platforms.
4. **"We understand through deciding and quickly ingesting the results" (D7).** Qualified a second time. Lee et al. and Rahman show people did act and experiment and still learned only the stable, checkable parts; Ben-David et al. find evidence consistent with fast, noisy feedback feeding overconfidence in forex traders. The recourse field defines recourse as the ability to "understand … and accordingly act" (Karimi et al., p. 2), joining the two, not replacing one with the other.
5. **"Platform users learn by experience" (the learning-curve reading of Cook et al. and Jiang and Sinchaisri).** Qualified by Seru et al. (exit can inflate apparent learning; Cook et al. test for it and report the same curve) and by Barber et al. and Hayley and Marsh (no learning to trade better in the trading populations they study).
6. **"Gaming is the user's way of learning the system."** Qualified by Petre et al.: the word is the platform's moral label for practices such as SEO and engagement-boosting, and the boundary is "nebulous and largely reflective of" platforms' material interests (abstract only).
7. **Treating the person as the unit that learns.** Qualified by Lee et al., Bechavod et al. and Hardt et al. (2023): knowledge is pooled and unequal, and a collective act can change the algorithm.
8. **Part 1 line 66 ("systematically disrupts both requirements").** Still too strong, now from a second angle: the measured noise (Chen et al.) is intermittent (90% of jitter events last 20–30 seconds within a 5-minute clock) and traced to a bug according to Uber's engineers, while the stable parts of the work are learned (Lee et al.).
