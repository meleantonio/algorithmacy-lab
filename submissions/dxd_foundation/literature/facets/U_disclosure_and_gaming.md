## (c) Findings per question

### Q1. The state of the evidence on mandated disclosure, including platform regulation

1. **The general critique stands, and nothing read this round overturns it.** Ben-Shahar and Schneider's three-step failure (disclosees "do not read … do not understand … do not use", `benshahar2011failure`, p. 665) and Loewenstein, Sunstein and Golman's finding that disclosure "may have little effect on recipients but large effects on providers" (`loewenstein2014disclosure`, p. 398) are the baseline. Bubb's 2015 review of the 2014 book (not accessible; see (e)) argues, per its abstract, that the critique targets deliberate decisions and that regulators should test which disclosures work. Sunstein's *Too Much Information* (2020) is a book and was not opened.

2. **Platform regulation copies the form and limits the content.** P2B Art. 5 requires "the main parameters determining ranking and the reasons for the relative importance" (`eu2019p2b`, Art. 5(1)); the Commission's Guidelines say the description "aims to improve predictability and help users improve the presentation of their goods and services" (`eu2020ranking`, pts 5, 15), that weights and algorithms need not be disclosed (pt 46), that "an excess of information can mean that, in effect, no meaningful information is provided" (pt 25), and that the point is to let businesses decide "how to invest in 'ranking strategies'", including SEO and paid ranking (pt 94). DSA Art. 27 and PWD Art. 9 use the same "main parameters … relative importance" formula (`eu2022dsa`, `eu2024platform`; `sax2025from` quotes Art. 27 in full, p. 7).

3. **What platforms then wrote.** Sovrano, Lognoul and Bacchelli's three legal experts rated six platforms' ranking documentation between 1.228 (Yahoo) and 3.544 (Bing) on a 1–5 scale where 2 is "very poorly answered" (`sovrano2024empirical`, Table 2); the Commission's own 2023 review found the basic information "potentially insufficient" and a 2022 Observatory study found over half of business users saw no improvement (§2). Only 55 of 134 lay raters read the documentation for more than five minutes (§6.2). For the DSA, Consensus surfaced three studies of the Transparency Database (Groesch et al. 2025; Leijten 2026; Kaushal et al. 2024, card exists) that find structure without usable scrutiny; none was opened this round beyond the existing Kaushal card.

4. **Disclosure as a product.** Sax and Wang document platforms selling what the mandates are meant to give: myFICO credit reports at $19.95–$59.85 showing the five score components and weights, and Tinder's announced $20–50 Concierge (`sax2025from`, §§3.1–3.2). Their argument is that such a service "introduces an incentive for maintaining (or even introducing new) levels of opaqueness" (§4.1).

### Q2. Does disclosing the rule reduce, raise or leave unchanged the strategising?

5. **The founding models assume disclosure and ask the designer to survive it.** Hardt et al. define gaming, state that "concerns of gaming and manipulation are often used as a reason for keeping classification mechanisms secret", and reply that "Secrecy is not a robust solution" because outsiders learn the rule "from classification outcomes" (`hardt2016strategic`, p. 2); their Contestant "knows c, h, D, and f" (p. 3). Kleinberg and Raghavan add that secrecy "can create inequalities between insiders … and outsiders" and show that whenever any monotone rule can induce a desired effort profile, a linear one can (`kleinberg2020how`, pp. 2, 8). Perdomo et al. show the rule is itself a response to the population (`perdomo2020performative`, §5): the loop runs whether or not the rule is disclosed.

6. **Disclosure raises strategising where measured.** Björkegren et al.: shown the rule, participants moved "closer to the direction incentivized by the algorithm", at a cost of about 23% of predictive performance for a standard rule (`bjorkegren2020manipulation`, pp. 5, 29). Tasnim et al.: truthful ranking fell from 80.14% with no probabilities to 57.45% and 40.43% when probabilities were shown; aggressive manipulation rose from 5.67% to 39.01% when it was shown to succeed 90% of the time (`tasnim2025modeling`, Table 2, p. 2474; McNemar χ² = 14.69, p = 0.00013). Eslami et al.: told that Yelp filtered their reviews, six of fifteen said they would "write for the algorithm", seven that they would use Yelp less or leave (`eslami2019user`, p. 11). Lee et al.: the disclosed general assignment rule "helped drivers create their work strategies" (`lee2015working`, p. 1607).

7. **But the models disagree on what the extra strategising does.** Wang et al. find "a broad set of conditions under which making the algorithm transparent is beneficial for the firm", because gaming the disclosed cheap feature forces people onto the costly causal one, while "the agents are not always better off" (`wang2023algorithmic`, p. 4; Theorems 1–2). Cofone and Strandburg argue that gaming requires weak proxies, modifiable features, cost-effective modification and no real improvement, and that "blanket claims that disclosure will lead to gaming are over-blown" (`cofone2019strategic`, pp. 623, 662–663); Canadian banks' disclosed mortgage metrics are not gamed because the features are income and assets (pp. 655–656). Ghalme et al. show that hiding the rule does not stop gaming; it makes people game a guessed rule at a "price of opacity" (`ghalme2021strategic`).

8. **Platforms give the anti-gaming reason for opacity in their own words.** Yelp's blog (via `eslami2019user`, p. 2), Upwork/StarWorks' help page ("Doing so would make it easier for some users to artificially boost their scores", `rahman2019invisible`, p. 89), Airbnb (via its own researchers: "there needs to be some degree of uncertainty … or otherwise, some hosts would inevitably game the system", `jhaver2018algorithmic`, Discussion), Google's "Unnatural links" notice and Facebook's "engagement bait" rule (`petre2019gaming`, Findings), and Lee et al.'s forecast that companies "may also desire a degree of user ignorance" (`lee2015working`, p. 1610). The Guidelines license this: fraud mechanisms "cannot be revealed in detail" (`eu2020ranking`, pt 73).

9. **Partial disclosure produces gaming by guesswork.** Upwork workers' "20/1 rule" and avoidance of "gray area words" (`bucher2020pacifying`, Empirical findings); Rahman's "superstitious reactivity" (`rahman2019invisible`, p. v); the Airbnb host who changed his title daily because "lots of blogs" said to (`jhaver2018algorithmic`, P-15); YouTube creators' "algorithmic gossip" behind $75–$500 guild fees (`bishop2019managing`); Yelp users' engagement and format folk theories, n = 66 and 37 (`eslami2019user`, pp. 8–9). Disclosure of inputs without the calculation leaves the strategic task in place and adds uncertainty about it.

10. **"Gaming" is the platform's word.** Petre, Duffy and Hund find the line between manipulation and legitimate strategy "nebulous and largely reflective of their material interests", and record Facebook advising calls-to-action and then demoting them as "engagement bait" in the same year (`petre2019gaming`, abstract; The Work of Authenticity Metaphors). Bambauer and Zarsky use the word neutrally and note it "has acquired negative connotations" (`bambauer2018algorithm`, p. 10).

### Q3. Gaming the mediator as a coordination cost borne to reach the other party

11. **Sellers to buyers.** Luca and Zervas: about 16% of 316,415 Boston Yelp reviews were filtered as suspicious; a restaurant's positive fraud rises after a 1-star review (an extra 0.01 filtered 5-star reviews a month, about 10% of the mean) and negative fraud rises with same-type independent competition (about 3%); businesses caught soliciting fake reviews had 2.6 stars and 18 reviews against 3.5 and 86 (`luca2016fake`, pp. 16, 19, 23). Curchod et al.: 41 of 77 high-rated eBay sellers described "working around the algorithm" to prevent buyer negatives, in a relation the paper calls triadic (`curchod2020working`). Bucher et al.: Upwork freelancers forgo proposals and underprice work to keep a score that lets them reach clients, "extra work" in a "triadic relationship between algorithm, client and worker" (`bucher2020pacifying`).

12. **Hosts to guests, creators to audiences.** Jhaver et al.'s "double negotiation": hosts appeal to guests and to algorithms at once, and the two conflict when declining short bookings costs acceptance rate (`jhaver2018algorithmic`). Bishop's vloggers switch to beauty content that gets "5 x more views" and crowdsource audits from fans (`bishop2019managing`). Petre et al.'s producers use SEO, reciprocal linking and pods to be seen (`petre2019gaming`).

13. **Drivers to riders.** Lee et al.'s driver who toggled the app at traffic lights to avoid distant requests (`lee2015working`, p. 1607); Rosenblat and Stark's blind acceptance and surge-chasing (`rosenblat2016algorithmic`); Garg and Nazerzadeh's proof that multiplicative surge makes rejecting short surge trips and long non-surge trips optimal, and their RideAustin estimate that additive surge raises the value of short surge trips by about $15 per hour (`garg2022driver`, p. 22). No source in this round documented drivers logging off en masse to trigger surge with data; that claim appears in press coverage of Möhlmann and Zalmanson's 2017 study (abstract only; see (e)).

14. **The cost named as such.** Bambauer and Zarsky call it "third-order inefficiencies—the costs of the system itself", including "the persistent worry and vigilance" (`bambauer2018algorithm`, p. 32); Bucher et al. call it "extra work"; Jhaver et al. call it "algorithmic anxiety". None frames it as a *coordination* cost between the two parties; that framing is the author's, and the sources support it only by description.

### Q4. Counter-evidence: where disclosure helped

15. **Trust, not behaviour.** Kizilcec: a procedural explanation of an adjusted peer grade removed the trust gap between learners whose expectations were violated and met (3.72 against 3.70), where no explanation (3.02 against 4.18, d = 1.01) and full outcome data (2.77 against 3.89, d = 1.08) did not; n = 103, self-report (`kizilcec2016how`, p. 2392). Rader, Cotter and Cho 2018 (abstract only) report that explanations of News Feed raised awareness and judgements of control but not of correctness.

16. **Welfare and improvement, in models.** Wang et al.'s region C2, where transparency Pareto-dominates opacity (`wang2023algorithmic`, p. 32); Cofone and Strandburg's borrower given "a tutorial in good financial behaviour" (`cofone2019strategic`, p. 651); Kleinberg and Raghavan's linear rule that steers effort to improvement (`kleinberg2020how`, Theorem 3).

17. **Policy use.** Tasnim et al. read their own data as a case for disclosing *risk*: parents shifted to aggressive strategies only at a 90% stated success chance, while simulations put real gains at 1–2% of students, so "making risk legible" could deter manipulation (`tasnim2025modeling`, p. 2477). Booking's documentation scored best on "how users can improve the ranking" (`sovrano2024empirical`, Q15), which is disclosure doing what the regulator intended.

18. **Design, not disclosure.** The two documented cases of a change that removed a strategic task are rule changes: Uber's additive surge (`garg2022driver`) and compelled pooling in US insurance and credit-card law, which "renders gaming efforts … unnecessary" (`bambauer2018algorithm`, p. 47). Garg and Nazerzadeh's footnote 7 (p. 9) has Uber disclosing destinations only after the rule was made incentive compatible: "Incentive compatible pricing is an important stepping stone to showing this information."

## (d) What this means for the abstract's sentence

The sentence makes three claims. Verdicts use the facet's three grades.

| Claim | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mandated disclosure (Ben-Shahar & Schneider, 2011) is the relevant intervention on platforms | **Supported** | P2B Art. 5, DSA Art. 27 and PWD Art. 9 are mandated disclosures in the authors' sense (a lawmaker requires a discloser to give a disclosee a standard description). The Guidelines bound the text to "main parameters" without weights (`eu2020ranking`, pts 6, 46). Measured compliance is poor (`sovrano2024empirical`). Citing Ben-Shahar and Schneider is apt but their evidence is pre-platform and dyadic; the platform step rests on the cards above. |
| It "does not spare them from having to game the mediator" | **Supported in a narrower form** | Where disclosure was manipulated, strategising rose or was redirected, never removed (`bjorkegren2020manipulation`, `tasnim2025modeling`, `eslami2019user`, `lee2015working`). The regulator says disclosure is *for* deciding "how to invest in 'ranking strategies'" (`eu2020ranking`, pt 94). Two qualifications: (i) Cofone and Strandburg show that for strong, costly proxies disclosure neither invites nor requires gaming, so the claim holds for ranking rules built on cheap, mutable signals (reviews, engagement, response time), which is the platform case; (ii) "game" is the platform's moral label (`petre2019gaming`), so the sentence should either use it knowingly or say "strategise". |
| The gaming is done "to coordinate with the other person" | **Supported by description, not by a test** | Every platform study reads the strategic work as aimed at reaching buyers, guests, clients, riders or audiences through the mediator (`luca2016fake`, `curchod2020working`, `bucher2020pacifying`, `jhaver2018algorithmic`, `bishop2019managing`, `garg2022driver`). None isolates a coordination cost from a competition or reputation cost, and none measures what the other party loses. The only quantitative link between gaming and reaching the counterpart is Luca and Zervas's incentive evidence, which the authors frame as fraud under competition. |

**What cuts against.** `wang2023algorithmic` (disclosure can make everyone better off in one region of the model, by redirecting gaming); `cofone2019strategic` (gaming is the exception, not the rule, for well-built proxies); `tasnim2025modeling` as its authors read it (disclosing risk may deter); `kizilcec2016how` (the right amount of disclosure restores trust). None of these shows a disclosure that spared people a strategic task in a triad with a behavioural outcome. That gap, named in `ARGUMENT_MAP.md` P8, remains after this round.

**What the facet adds to the argument.** The strongest new support is the regulator's own statement of purpose (`eu2020ranking`, pts 13, 94); the strongest new design evidence for the author's "smoking gun" search is a rule change that removed a strategic task without disclosing anything (`garg2022driver`, with `bambauer2018algorithm`, Part III.C, as the legal analogue); and the strongest new caution is that two of the three field studies of disclosure (Björkegren; Tasnim) are not triads, and the third (Eslami 2019) measures intentions.
