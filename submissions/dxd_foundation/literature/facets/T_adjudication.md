## 1. The history: H-cost against H-cause

**Verdict: H-cost holds in a narrow form. H-cause has no support.**

| Evidence | Says | Depth | Facet |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Saenger 1997, pp. x, 11 | His book "is not a general history of literacy". He does list "the greater diffusion of literacy throughout all social strata of the population" among the advantages modern readers see in ease of reading (p. 11); that is a modern value the ancients did not share, not a finding about the Middle Ages | Confirmed | A |

| Scribner and Cole 1981 | The Vai script "runs across the page without division into words or phrases" and "is customarily learned in adulthood" without extended study. It shows an unspaced script can be learned informally; it is one case | Part; pages unconfirmed | C |

| Winskel et al. 2009; Rayner et al. 1998 | Removing spaces from English slows reading by a third to a half | Part; abstract | D |
| Shen et al. 2012; Blythe et al. 2012; Li et al. 2021 | Spaces help second-language learners and beginners most; other child studies find no gain | Abstract to part | D |
| Hangul; Guido; Pacioli; Hindu-Arabic numerals | Each "one move" story dissolves into a bundle plus schools plus demand. Hangul stayed marginal for four centuries | Part | F |

Three points follow.

1. **Spacing lowered a cost, most for learners.** That is the whole of what the experimental and
   historical record supports. Insular scribes separated words for readers who were learning Latin as a
   foreign language; the Continent adopted separation three centuries later for scholars reading hard
   texts. Nobody designed it for a populace.
2. **Demand may have come first.** Romans used word dividers and gave them up. Saenger and Johnson both
   explain that choice socially: an elite that did not want reading made easier. The move became
   attractive when the readers changed.
3. **No source ties spacing to how many people could read.** Saenger says his book is not a history of
   literacy, the historians of literacy ignore spacing, and unspaced scripts have supported both fluent reading and informal learning.

**Recommended ruling.** Keep the Part 2 ruling for this paper: the history is a design precedent. Retire
"ignited literacy". The defensible sentence is of the form "word separation lowered a cost of reading,
most for readers still learning the language; it did not by itself raise the number of readers."

**The author's sentence at `draft.md:263`.** "…it changed the interface so that reading no longer
required the training" is stronger than the sources. Hutchins and Zhang and Norman describe a task
transformed, and the scribe took on the work the reader shed. "Populace" has no support. I flag it; the
wording is the author's to change.

## 2. Word spacing is a legibility remedy, and the ruling rejects legibility

**Verdict: the contrast can be drawn, with a narrower ruling and a different test.**

- **Two senses of one word.** Parkes's "grammar of legibility" is graphic legibility, a perceptual aid.
  The 30 September ruling rejects legibility as interpretability: asking a person to read an opaque
  system more skilfully. Human factors treats the first as its best work (ecological interface design;
  Erickson and Kellogg's sign and window). A blanket "not legibility" will read as rejecting the field.
- **"Moves work to the artefact" does not separate the paper from Nadella.** A reference monitor does
  exactly that (facet G, C7). Facet E's cut is better: does the change make the person's task perceptual
  at a load they can carry in real time, or leave it analytical and add volume?
- **Two further tests**, both the facet agents' inferences and mine, not findings from a source: what
  task the person keeps after the artefact has done its share, and whom the move serves. Spacing left the
  reader a task people do well and served the reader. "Observe everything" leaves a person reading
  evidence and deciding when to halt, which Bainbridge (1983, p. 776) calls "an impossible task" when
  the machine decides faster than a human can follow, and it serves the deploying organisation.
- **Absorbing a task has costs.** Risko and Gilbert name complacency and skill decay; Buçinca et al.
  found the more demanding design worked best; Bainbridge's own remedy is deliberate inefficiency.

**Recommended ruling.** Restate the first ruling for this paper as "algorithmacy is not a larger reading
load on the person", and let the paper use perceptual re-representation as its vocabulary (Zhang and
Norman's representational effect; Hutchins's task transformation, from his 1995 article "How a cockpit
remembers its speeds").

## 4. One move or a complex

**Verdict: a complex of conventions.**

- Saenger links word order and word separation (p. 17; wording confirmed, page to check);
  spacing arrived with punctuation, construction notes, capitals, and later tables and running heads.
- Every parallel case in facet F is a bundle plus teaching institutions plus demand.
- In facet H the measured candidates sort by what they do with the work:

| What the intervention does | Examples | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Removes a task by fixing the mediator's rule | Strategy-proof matching; withholding cues; simultaneous reveal | Lab and field experiments on matching; removal is incomplete (23% still misreport, Rees-Jones and Skowronek) and depends on presentation (36% against 7% off the dominant outcome, Li 2017). The platform study of withheld cues (Mejia and Parker) says it cannot tell whether the party gained |
| Hands over a pre-computed comparison | Kling et al.: 28% against 17% switching | One randomised field experiment |
| Hands the person something to read or tune | Envelopes, seams, model cards, feed controls | Mostly satisfaction; several nulls |
| Hands the task to the person's own agent | Zhu et al. 2026 | One preprint, no mediator; the delegate's advantage does not survive correction for multiple tests (p = .033, then .10) |
| Removes exposure | Chronological feed | Two field experiments, behaviour only |

- The strongest candidates work because the participant does **not** read or rewrite the mediator. That
  collides with the engagement layer as worded ("reading and rewriting the shared mediator").
- The removed task moves elsewhere (search and stopping in Chilean school choice; Arteaga et al.).

**Recommended ruling.** Write "a convention" or "a complex of conventions", never "the move". Offer
mediator rules that remove a strategic task, and make the removal visible, as the best-evidenced
candidate, and present the missing experiment as the paper's research agenda (facet H, (iii) 1).

## 5. Problems the research added

1. **Who builds it.** Scribes shared the reader's interest; the platform that owns the interface does
   not share its users' (Part 1, line 60: users "whose objectives conflict with the platform's bottom line"). "Redesign the interface" hands the remedy to the
   interested party. The convention has to be one the owner cannot withdraw alone: a public standard, the
   person's own agent, a regulated rule, or exit. Facet X ranks this the most damaging objection.
2. **No trained elite.** `CLAIM.md` says coordination through an algorithm is "in the condition of
   unspaced script: only the trained can do it". Part 1 gives no evidence that anyone can do it reliably
   (lines 66, 138, 156). The analogy needs "a task nobody has shown how to do reliably".
3. **Sensibility and artefact.** The author defines algorithmacy as a sensibility of persons and places
   the remedy in artefacts. The paper has to say how the two relate.
4. **Media hold still.** External-representation research assumes a stable regularity and a designer who
   knows the constraints (Hutchins; Scaife and Rogers; Vicente and Rasmussen). An adaptive, interested
   mediator offers neither. This is Part 1's own caution (line 118) and the place the analogy breaks.

## 6. What survives

*Claude's statement of the strongest defensible thesis, for the author to accept, change or reject.*

Scribes once changed the page so that readers no longer had to find the words. The change lowered the
cost of reading for people still learning, spread over centuries for social reasons, and came as part of
a complex of conventions. It is a clear historical case of a human-factors principle: re-represent the
task so that the artefact carries work the person would otherwise carry.

Coordination through an algorithmic third party has no such conventions. Decision Experience Design is
the discipline that looks for them, and the evidence so far points to rules in the mediator that remove
a strategic task from the people it coordinates, made visible to them, and held in a form the mediator's
owner cannot withdraw alone.

Nadella's insider post shares the premise that the model cannot be read and the remedy of controls
outside it. It differs in whom the controls serve. His architecture gives authority to the organisation
that deploys the model and leaves a human to read evidence and pull a brake, tasks the oversight
literature finds people do badly. It says nothing of the people the model acts upon.
