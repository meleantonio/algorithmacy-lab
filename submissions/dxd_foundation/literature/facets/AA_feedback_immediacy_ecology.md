# AA — Ecological feedback immediacy

*Facet AA, round 5, 2026-10-15. Agent-drafted (Fable). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**The abstract's move this facet serves.** "… and ecological feedback immediacy (Shneiderman, 1983; Kahneman & Klein, 2009)." The sentence closes the abstract's list of three means by which a design change might "free algorithmacy from literacy" and let people "decide within irreducible triads."

**What the facet covers.** Four questions set in `round5_items.json`. (Q1) Shneiderman's direct manipulation and its cognitive account (Hutchins, Hollan & Norman 1985; Norman's gulfs) and Shneiderman's later statements. (Q2) Kahneman and Klein's conditions for trustworthy intuition and the kind/wicked literature behind it (Hogarth; Brunswik's lens model; ecological interface design). (Q3) Feedback-timing evidence that cuts both ways: immediate against delayed feedback in learning, feedback frequency, myopic loss aversion. (Q4) What "ecological" feedback can mean when the environment contains another person acting through a mediator: learning in games with noisy or pooled payoffs, outcome feedback on counterparts' judgments.

**What the facet does not repeat.** Facet N (2026-10-13) already holds Tiefenbeck, Karlin, Jessoe and Rapson, the eco-driving and trading-app studies, Lurie and Swaminathan (abstract), and the Consensus abstracts of Gneezy and Potters and Thaler et al.; facet I holds Kahneman and Klein, Klein 2008, Weick and the aviation sources; facet E holds Vicente and Rasmussen 1992. Cards `shneiderman1983direct` and `kahneman2009conditions` were read before searching and have an "Also used by facet AA" section appended.

**The question the synthesis answers.** Do the two cited sources, and the literatures behind them, support "ecological feedback immediacy" as one thing, and does any of it reach a triad?

## (c) Findings by question

### Q1. Direct manipulation: the principle and its cognitive account

**Shneiderman 1983** (`shneiderman1983direct`, card from facet F) gives the principle the abstract cites: continuous representation, physical action, and "rapid, reversible, incremental actions" whose results are immediately visible, so that "users can immediately see if their actions are furthering their goals" (pp. 57, 65). The same page lists the benefits as claims from observation. The article's own limits are the condition of "predictable system behavior" (p. 62) and the section on problems (p. 64).

**Hutchins, Hollan & Norman 1985** (`hutchins1985direct`, new card) supplies the account the 1983 article lacks. Directness is "an orienting notion," "a feeling," and "the feeling of directness results from the commitment of fewer cognitive resources" (p. 317). Two sources: *distance* across the gulfs of execution and evaluation (p. 318) and *engagement* with a model world in which "there is no intermediary between user and world" (pp. 317–318). On the evaluation side the water-tank display (p. 323) is the canonical re-representation: show the rate rather than make the user compute it. For direct engagement "the system should be responsive, with no delays between execution and the results, except where those delays are appropriate for the knowledge domain itself" (p. 333), and rapid feedback "removes the perception of the computer as an intermediary" and lets the user "make use of perceptual faculties in evaluating the outcome of actions" (pp. 333–334). The costs: "It is important not to equate directness with ease of use" (p. 336), and practice can mimic directness without reducing the gulf ("Automatization does not reduce the semantic distance," p. 327).

**Shneiderman's later statements.** The IUI '97 paper (`shneiderman1997direct`) restates the three principles (p. 33) and turns them against adaptive agents: "even occasional unexpected behavior has serious negative side effects," users "must pause to see what has happened" (p. 35); users "shy away from complex unpredictable behavior, such as the pilots who disengage automatic piloting devices" (p. 36); adaptation should sit under the hood "in the same way that turning on an automobile ignition is comprehensible to the user but invokes complex algorithms" (p. 36); and "a critical issue for designers is the clear placement of responsibility for failures" (p. 36). The debate with Maes (`shneiderman1997maes`) records the settlement: "two levels: the user interface level, which users want to be predictable and controllable … and the level below the table, where there may be some interesting algorithms" (p. 55), and Maes's slide "Make the user model available to the user," which Shneiderman endorses and says "I don't see that being done" (p. 58). Maes's Kasbah agents, which "buy and sell on behalf of users" against other people's agents (p. 52), are a 1997 sketch of the triad. Under uncertainty, Shneiderman says, operators "will disengage the automatic system" and "can only do what's rehearsed and predictable" (p. 59).

*Reading for the draft.* The 1983 principle is about acting on a stable object whose response is predictable; its authors said so in 1983 (p. 62), 1985 (pp. 318, 333) and 1997 (pp. 35–36). The tradition's own answer to an adaptive intermediary was a predictable surface and disclosure of the model, the second of which the author's D3 classifies as a literacy intervention.

### Q2. Kind and wicked environments; the lens model; ecological interface design

**Kahneman & Klein 2009** (`kahneman2009conditions`, card from facet I) gives two necessary conditions, "an environment of sufficiently high validity and adequate opportunity to practice the skill" (p. 520), and requires "prolonged practice and feedback that is both rapid and unequivocal" (p. 524). Validity is "the causal and statistical structure of the relevant environment" (p. 520).

**Hogarth, Lejarraga & Soyer 2015** (`hogarth2015two`, new card) is the source of the "wicked" term the 2009 article borrows and the fuller classification. Wicked: "feedback in the form of outcomes of actions or observations is poor, misleading, or even missing"; kind: feedback "links outcomes directly to the appropriate actions or judgments and is both accurate and plentiful" (p. 379). Six cases of match between the learning setting L and the target setting T (p. 380, Fig. 1 p. 381). Two sentences reach the triad: "A wicked learning environment can emerge as a result of actions taken by the person making the inferences (as in self-fulfilling prophecies, Case E)" (p. 380), and nonstationary environments "are wicked (a likely Case E). However, if the nature of the dynamic change can be inferred from the first setting, these environments can be kind" (p. 382). The prescription is "deliberately creating kind environments" (p. 382), and the closing concedes that "the way to achieve this remains uncertain" (p. 383). Speed appears nowhere in the definition.

**Hogarth & Soyer 2011** (`hogarth2011sequentially`, new card; working-paper version) is the engineered kind environment. Naive adults who generated outcomes by clicking matched trained students: on the Bayesian task 5 of 29 (17%) correct analytically, 28 of 29 (97%) after experience, 23 of 29 (79%) at the paid final answer (WP pp. 15–16); in the regression-investment task the analytic group erred by −16 points on the probability of a loss against 0 and +5 for the experience groups (t = 7.1), and by +24 on beating no-investment against +1 and +3 (t = 7.7) (WP pp. 23–24). The authors ask whether people trust the mechanism ("grey box," WP p. 29) and note a "clash of intuitions" (WP p. 30).

**Brunswik and the lens model.** Brunswik 1955 was not accessible; the 1956 book scan was unreadable. Karelaia & Hogarth 2008 (read in part, UPF WP 1007) is the meta-analysis of 259 lens-model environments from 78 papers. Its learning result is the one that matters here: "the availability of task information magnifies the effect of learning. Neither outcome nor cognitive feedback helps to learn" (WP p. 25); with task information the index GRs rose by 0.35 on average against 0.28 with no feedback (WP p. 24); and the authors note earlier findings that outcome feedback "may even deter learning under uncertainty (Hammond, Summers, & Deane, 1973)" (WP p. 23). Their summary: "People learn best from feedback that instructs them about the characteristics of the tasks they face" (WP p. 28). This is a lead, not a card.

**Ecological interface design.** `vicente1992ecological` is held by facet E and was not reread; Vicente 2002 remains abstract-only. Neither was pursued further, because EID concerns a person and a physical process with laws the display can make visible, and the facet's question is about an environment that contains another person.

*Reading for the draft.* The kind/wicked tradition carries the "ecological" half of the author's phrase and attaches it to validity and match, not to speed. Its best demonstration (Hogarth & Soyer) is a simulation the person acts on, which is immediate and unequivocal by construction. Its lens-model branch says outcome feedback by itself does not teach in uncertain tasks; information about the task does.

### Q3. Feedback timing: when immediacy helps and when it hurts

**Learning and retention.** Schmidt & Bjork 1992 (`schmidt1992new`) state the assumption built into "the design of simulators," that feedback "more immediate, more accurate, more frequent" helps, and say it "must be qualified": feedback after every trial gave the best practice and the worst two-day retention in Schmidt et al. 1989, and 50% faded feedback beat 100% at retention (pp. 212–213); the mechanisms offered are that "frequent feedback comes to be a part of the task" and blocks the learner's own error detection (p. 213). McKay et al. 2022 (`mckay2022meta`) test this across 61 papers (k = 75, N = 2,228): reduced frequency against 100% gives g = 0.19 (95% CI −0.11 to 0.49) at acquisition, 0.01 (−0.30 to 0.32) at immediate retention, 0.19 (−0.05 to 0.43) at delayed retention, prediction intervals wider than ±1 SD, bias-corrected estimates near zero (RoBMA g = 0.021, BF01 = 4.22), median group size 13; "our results are thus uniformly inconclusive" (ms p. 32) and scientists "should abstain from making substantive recommendations regarding feedback frequency" (ms p. 35). Fyfe et al. 2021 (`fyfe2021manyclasses`) ran immediate against 1–5-day-delayed feedback across 38 college classes (2,081 students): effect 0.002 (95% HDI −0.05 to 0.05), no credible moderator among 40 (ms pp. 2, 10, 14). They characterise Kulik and Kulik 1988 as "small to moderate advantages for immediate feedback in 10 out of 11 studies conducted in classroom settings that often lacked experimental designs" (ms pp. 17–18). Kulik & Kulik and Butler et al. 2007 were not accessible and are described here only through Fyfe.

**Frequency and outcomes in the field.** Casas-Arce et al. 2017 (`casasarce2017performance`, working-paper version) randomised about 800 repair professionals to monthly or weekly, aggregate or detailed feedback on customer detractor scores. Monthly-detailed did best (8.37% detractors; 2 points below the control, a 20% improvement; in difference-in-differences −3.4 against −2.5 points), weekly groups did no better than the control, and monthly-detailed beat weekly-detailed (WP pp. 23–27). For process metrics the professional can see directly, "the professionals receive immediate feedback simply by executing these tasks," and the treatments made no difference (WP pp. 3, 29). The mechanism argued is salience of the last report, shown by worse first-week performance after a bad report "despite the fact that both groups have the same amount of information" (WP p. 33). The authors' caution: "In settings where timely information is a key feature (e.g. brokerage firms), our results for feedback frequency may not apply at all" (WP p. 37). Lam et al. 2011 (abstract only) report an inverted-U between feedback frequency and performance mediated by effort.

**Myopic loss aversion.** Gneezy & Potters 1997 (`gneezy1997experiment`): with feedback and choice every round, subjects bet 50.5% of the endowment; with feedback and a fixed bet every three rounds, 67.4% (z = −2.86, p = 0.002; Table I p. 639); the low-frequency group earned more (2,134 against 1,822 cents, p = 0.038; Table II p. 640). The difference was present in round 1, before feedback (p. 639), and the treatment bundles frequency with commitment (p. 635). Thaler et al. 1997 (abstract only, reused key) report the same direction. Haddara & Rahnev 2022 (abstract only, N = 518) find trial-by-trial feedback changed response bias and confidence calibration but not perceptual sensitivity after seven days.

*Reading for the draft.* Across these sources immediacy helps performance while acting and has no settled effect, or a negative one, on retention and on outcomes judged over a longer period. The negative cases have two mechanisms: dependence and blocked self-evaluation (Schmidt & Bjork), and salience of the latest sample or loss (Casas-Arce; Gneezy & Potters). The null cases (Fyfe; McKay) mean neither "immediate is better" nor "delayed is better" can be cited as established. The draft can say when immediacy helps (acting on a stable object whose response is the outcome) and when it hurts (outcomes that are samples from a noisy judgment, where frequency sets the sample size the person acts on).

### Q4. Feedback when the environment contains another person acting through a mediator

**Noise in outcomes with perfect monitoring.** Bereby-Meyer & Roth 2006 (`berebymeyer2006speed`): in ten-period prisoner's dilemma supergames, with the partner's action always visible, deterministic payoffs let period-1 cooperation rise from 0.61 to 0.93 over 20 supergames; payoffs paid as lotteries with the same expectation left it at 0.40 to 0.48 and never above 60% (pp. 1035–1037). Players conditioned on their own lottery: after mutual cooperation, losing the draw cut next-period cooperation from 0.83 to 0.68 (Table 1, p. 1038). "A small change in the payoff environment … can have a large effect on collective behavior" (p. 1029), because "the game provides a feedback loop between what players learn and what there is to learn" (p. 1040).

**Learning from one's own payoff when the others are invisible.** Nax et al. 2016 (`nax2016learning`): in a "black box" where subjects did not know others existed, five payoff-based search rules held (a payoff fall raised the search probability by 12 ± 2 points; p. 5), and the same rules held with full information (p. 7), where "providing more information can actually lead to decreasing play of the dominant strategy" (p. 7). Burton-Chellew & West 2021 (`burtonchellew2021payoff`, author manuscript) give the quantity: influence, the share of one's payoff range under one's own control (eq. 1, ms p. 4). With 43% influence black-box contributions fell from 46% to 15% in 16 rounds; with 11–12% they "finished at just over 50%, indicative of no learning on average" (ms p. 6). Across 237 published games (17,940 participants) influence predicted the rate of decline (ms p. 6), and when groupmates' payoffs were shown learning ran "as if" the correlation were −1 (ms p. 7). Their closing: "much of modern life is at least partially like a black box" (ms p. 10).

**Outcome feedback on a counterpart's judgment through an intermediary.** Casas-Arce et al. (above) is the field case closest to a rating system: the signal is a customer's score, delivered by the firm that sets the bonus, and more frequent delivery hurt.

*Reading for the draft.* No source tests a convention in a mediated triad with a behavioural outcome; exposure 1 of the argument map stands. The nearest evidence says three things. Noise in what comes back is enough to stop two people learning to cooperate even when each can see what the other did. People learn from their own outcomes when their own action moves the outcome enough to be seen, and a mediator that pools counterparts and optimises on its own account lowers that share. Showing counterparts' outcomes restored learning in the games; that is disclosure of outcomes, not of the mechanism. "Ecological" in a triad therefore means a visible correlation between the person's act and the return, which the mediator can raise or lower by design; "immediacy" is not the variable these studies moved.

## (e) What this means for the abstract's sentence

The sentence: "… enabling people to decide within irreducible triads through epistemic action (Kirsh & Maglio, 1994), strategic offloading (Li, 2017), and ecological feedback immediacy (Shneiderman, 1983; Kahneman & Klein, 2009)." Facet AA tests the last item.

| Claim in the sentence | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Feedback immediacy is a means by which people decide, i.e. act and adjust | **Supported, in a narrower form** | Shneiderman 1983 (p. 65) and Hutchins et al. 1985 (pp. 323, 333–334) give the principle and the account: immediate, visible results let perception replace inference while acting on a stable object. Hogarth & Soyer 2011 show naive adults reaching expert accuracy by generating outcomes and watching them (WP pp. 15–16, 23–24). The narrowing: the evidence is for performance during action in a person–system setting with a fixed generating process, and the authors of the principle made predictability a condition (1983 p. 62; 1985 p. 333; 1997 pp. 35–36) |
| "Ecological" feedback, feedback from a valid environment, is what makes learning by acting possible | **Supported** | Kahneman & Klein 2009 (pp. 520, 524); Hogarth et al. 2015 (pp. 379–380); the games evidence isolates the condition: noise in outcomes alone defeats learning to cooperate (Bereby-Meyer & Roth, pp. 1035–1038), and learning tracks the correlation between one's own act and one's return (Burton-Chellew & West, ms pp. 5–7). Karelaia & Hogarth (read in part) add that outcome feedback alone does not teach in uncertain tasks; information about the task does |
| Immediacy as such improves learning or outcomes | **Cuts against, or unsettled** | Schmidt & Bjork 1992 (pp. 212–213): frequent feedback best in practice, worst at retention; McKay et al. 2022: the effect is undetectable either way (g ≈ 0.0–0.2, PIs beyond ±1 SD; ms pp. 26–32); Fyfe et al. 2021: 0.002 (−0.05, 0.05) across 38 classes; Casas-Arce et al. 2017: weekly feedback no better than monthly, worse than monthly-detailed (WP pp. 25–27); Gneezy & Potters 1997: more frequent feedback, lower bets and lower earnings (pp. 639–640). The draft should not claim that faster feedback teaches |
| Shneiderman (1983) and Kahneman & Klein (2009) support one thing called "ecological feedback immediacy" | **Narrower: they support two different things** | Shneiderman's "immediately visible" is a property of an interface's response to the user's own action; Kahneman & Klein's "validity" is a property of the environment's structure, with "rapid and unequivocal" as a conjoined clause about learning (p. 524). The two coincide only where the interface *is* the environment, which is the dyad the abstract has just set aside. The draft should either split the phrase into its two citations or say what joins them |
| This applies "within irreducible triads" | **Not tested; the nearest evidence is conditional** | No study varies a feedback convention in a mediated triad with a behavioural outcome (argument map, exposure 1). Hogarth et al. 2015 classify an environment that reacts to the person's actions, or changes over time, as "a likely Case E" (wicked) unless the dynamics can be inferred (pp. 380, 382). Burton-Chellew & West's influence measure (ms p. 4) says what a mediator would have to leave the person: a visible share of the outcome under the person's own control. Casas-Arce et al. show that an intermediary delivering a counterpart's judgment more often made things worse (WP pp. 25–27, 33) |

**Where the sources cut against the author.** Three places. (1) The design tradition the abstract cites treated unpredictability as the thing to eliminate and proposed disclosure of the model as the remedy for adaptive systems (Shneiderman 1997, pp. 35–36; Shneiderman & Maes, p. 58); the abstract's D3 line that disclosure is a literacy intervention is therefore also a departure from Shneiderman. (2) The feedback-timing literature does not support "immediacy" as a learning principle, and two field and laboratory studies show more frequent outcome feedback lowering outcomes. (3) The kind/wicked literature places a reactive, nonstationary mediator in the wicked cases by default (Hogarth et al. 2015, pp. 380, 382); the author's own Part 1 says the same, and the second convention in P10 remains the inference that answers it.

**Where they support the author.** People do learn by acting and reading the return even with no account of the mechanism (Nax et al., pp. 5–7; Hogarth & Soyer, WP pp. 14–16), which is D7 in its conditional form; adding a description of the mechanism did not help convergence in one condition (Nax et al., p. 7), which is on the side of P8; and the games evidence gives the triad a measurable design variable, the person's influence over the return (Burton-Chellew & West, ms pp. 4–7), which is a candidate for the "smoking gun" the abstract asks for.

**Suggested wording the author may take or leave (Claude's, not the author's).** "ecological feedback" with Kahneman & Klein and Hogarth et al. 2015 for the environment's validity, and "feedback immediacy" with Shneiderman 1983 and Hutchins et al. 1985 for the interface's response, stated as two conditions rather than one.
