# T — Dyad versus irreducible algorithmic triad

*Facet T, round 5, 2026-10-15. Agent-drafted (Fable). The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

## What this facet covers

The abstract's sentence: "On digital platforms, though, counterparts—a driver and a rider, or a buyer and a seller—coordinate through an algorithmic mediator that binds and optimizes, forming an irreducible algorithmic triad." The facet tests four claims folded into it: (1) that a literature models platform coordination as three parties with the platform as a party with its own objective; (2) that human-factors or HCI work treats a human–human exchange through a mediating system as a triad; (3) that "irreducible" has an operational meaning; (4) that there are documented cases in which the mediator *binds* (sets prices or matches with authority) and *optimizes* (pursues its own objective). Thirteen sources were read in full and carded; one was read in part; seven existing cards received an "Also used by facet T" section. Every bibliographic field in `T_dyad_to_triad.bib` came from Crossref on 2026-10-15, except Stark and Pais (Crossref had no record; fields from the journal page's citation metadata) and Simmel (title page of the 1950 edition).

## Findings per question

### Q1. Which literatures model platform coordination as three parties with the platform as a party with its own objective?

Three bodies do, in different registers.

**Economic sociology of platforms.** Stark and Pais state it flatly: "This relationship is typically three-sided: on one side, the platform owner; on the second side, the provider …; and on the third side, the user-customer" (`stark2020algorithmic`, p. 53), and what the algorithmic-management literature misses "is that this triangular geometry … and the power asymmetries along its various axes are key" (p. 54). Their mechanism is co-optation: the owner enrols the other two sides' ratings "without managerial authority having been delegated to them" (p. 47), so that feedback loops are "twisted by the triadic relations of owner-provider-user" (p. 54). Vallas and Schor supply the ancestor: platforms "govern the service triangle that links employers, workers, and customers" and play "the role of tertius gaudens—'the third who rejoices'—as in Simmel's classic theory of the triad" (`vallas2020what`, 16.10; published p. 282 per Stark and Pais). Cruz and Gameiro add the service-work lineage (Leidner's "three-way interest alliances") and the complaint that most research sees only platform–worker (`cruz2023digital`, pp. 2, 4–5). Wood et al. show the triangle at work on remote freelance platforms: clients rate, "the platforms' algorithmic ranking of workers within search results" allocates, and control sits "at the end of the labour process rather than during it" (`wood2019good`, Findings). Griesbach et al. (read in part) add the delivery case, including a mediator that uses one party's rating to govern future matches: on Shipt, "If a customer rates their shopper as a one or two stars, that shopper will be permanently blocked from shopping for that customer," while "workers are not able to similarly rank the customers" ("The context of technical control").

**Simmel.** The source of the triad concept. The third member makes possible a majority (p. 137), mediation and arbitration (pp. 145–154), exploitation as *tertius gaudens* (pp. 154–162) and *divide et impera* (pp. 162–169); these forms "are impossible if there are only two elements" (`simmel1950sociology`, p. 145). Simmel also separates a third who "only guides the process of coming to terms" from an arbitrator who "ends up by taking sides" (p. 148), and the self-interested third from both (p. 154). The abstract's "binds and optimizes" fuses arbitrator and *tertius gaudens*.

**Economics of two-sided markets.** The platform is a profit-maximising party by construction. Rochet and Tirole define two-sidedness as the case in which "the volume of transactions between end-users depends on the structure and not only on the overall level of the fees charged by the platform" (`rochet2006two`, pp. 2–3) and derive the platform's price level and structure from its own profit (pp. 17, 24). Hagiu and Wright define a multi-sided platform by "direct interactions" in which the sides "retain control over the key terms" plus "affiliation," and set the platform's fixed and variable fees to maximise its profit (`hagiu2015multi`, pp. 5, 12–13).

**The dissenting frame.** Kellogg, Valentine and Christin model employer–worker control with the algorithm as the employer's instrument and customers as raters (`kellogg2020algorithms`, "What it cuts"; "Also used by facet T"). Stark and Pais themselves bound the triad: where the platform employs directly, "especially where there is no triangular relationship. For example, Amazon's enormous warehouses," management is Taylorist (p. 54, n. 13). The triad is a property of the marketplace form, not of algorithms as such.

Not reached: Rahman (2021, *ASQ*), Möhlmann et al. (2021, *MIS Quarterly*), Curchod et al. (2020, *ASQ*), Obstfeld (2005), Burt (1992). See the leads list.

### Q2. Does human-factors or HCI work treat a human–human interaction through a mediating system as a triad, and what does the mediator do to each party's task?

The nearest field, AI-mediated communication, defines its mediator so that the answer is no. Hancock, Naaman and Levy: AI-MC is communication "in which an intelligent agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages" (`hancock2020ai`, p. 1); autonomy "is similar to the principal–agent relationship" (p. 4); the optimization goal is a dimension of the sender's tool (Table 1, p. 3). Platform ranking and matching are excluded by name: "content ranking, recommendation and classification algorithms … we therefore exclude" (p. 4). The experiments in that tradition (`hohenstein2023artificial`) accordingly randomise a delegate's availability to one or both partners; the effect that moved was the partner's suspicion, not any property of a third party with interests. What the mediator does to each party's task in AI-MC is therefore: it writes for one of them and alters how the other reads (trust, attribution), which is a dyad with a delegate.

Facet-T's conclusion for the paper: there is no HCI literature that models the abstract's triad, and the paper should say so rather than borrow AI-MC as if it did. The closest design to borrow is Hohenstein et al.'s instrumented assignment of a mediator to one side (card, "Also used by facet T"). The one triadic HCI frame surfaced (Gamus et al. 2026, triadic telemedicine) was abstract only.

### Q3. What would "irreducible" mean operationally, and who has argued for or against irreducibility of mediated coordination?

Four candidate senses came out of the reading, none of them the author's Φ as applied to a platform.

1. **Formal (Simmel).** The triad's forms belong to the configuration, not to any pair; adding a third "completely changes" a dyad while a fourth does not change the triad (`simmel1950sociology`, pp. 138, 141, 145). This is irreducibility as a qualitative claim, argued by example.
2. **Price-structure non-neutrality (Rochet and Tirole).** A market is two-sided when the outcome depends on how the platform divides its fee between the sides, which is the case in which the two end-users cannot bargain the mediator's choice away (`rochet2006two`, pp. 6, 8, 11). This is the economists' own testable form of "the triad cannot be reduced to a price level between the two parties." Hagiu and Wright's model, by contrast, decomposes into N professional–platform relations plus a spillover term, which is how an economist would resist "irreducible" (`hagiu2015multi`, pp. 9–14).
3. **System feedback (Castillo, Knoepfle and Weyl).** Trip supply depends on both sides through pickup time, L = I + tQ + TQ; a rider's request removes an idle driver and lengthens every other rider's wait, and the labour-supply response feeds back, producing a failure mode (wild goose chases) that no party in either dyad can see or cause (`castillo2025matching`, pp. 5–8, 13–14). Garg and Nazerzadeh show the same dependence on the driver side: the driver's best response to a payment rule depends on the rider-side demand process through surge transitions (`garg2022driver`, pp. 10–13).
4. **Twisted control loops (Stark and Pais).** "Neither is it the case that providers and users are controlling each other … feedback loops … are twisted by the triadic relations of owner-provider-user" (`stark2020algorithmic`, p. 54): a prose claim of non-decomposability without a measure.

**Φ.** Engel and Malone's group Φ was computed on peers with "no mediator with its own objective" and on who-is-active states (`engel2018integrated`, "Also used by facet T"). No source read computes or proposes Φ for a mediated triad. Ruling D9's gauge remains a proposal.

**Against irreducibility.** Two empirical patterns cut the other way. Counterparts can ally against the mediator: Rahman's freelancers "forming online alliances with clients to game the performance evaluation system" (`vallas2020what`, 16.7; `rahman2019invisible`, "Also used by facet T"), and workers who "agree to allocate tasks directly without the need for platform intermediation" (`cruz2023digital`, p. 3). Where the match survives the mediator, the triad was reducible after all, at least for that pair. And Vallas and Schor's heterogeneity point (supplemental earners "can refuse low-paying tasks," 16.8) means the strength of the binding varies by party.

## What this means for the abstract's sentence

The sentence makes five claims. Taking them in order:

1. **"Counterparts … coordinate through an algorithmic mediator."** *Supported.* Stark and Pais (p. 53), Vallas and Schor (16.10), Cruz and Gameiro (p. 3), Wood et al. and every ride-hail and marketplace case describe three parties with the mediator between the two counterparts. One boundary: the triad is a property of the marketplace form; where the platform employs directly (warehouses) it is a dyad (Stark and Pais, n. 13).

2. **"… that binds."** *Supported in a narrower form.* The mediator sets the match with authority on Uber (first-dispatch; 15-second blind acceptance), sets the rider's price and the driver's pay separately (`garg2022driver`), picks the default seller on Amazon (`chen2016empirical`) and decides visibility on Airbnb. But the strength of binding varies: on remote freelance platforms it ranks and filters rather than prices; on Airbnb the host still sets the price. And by the Hagiu–Wright definition a mediator that takes control of key terms has moved from a pure platform toward a reseller or integrated firm (`hagiu2015multi`, pp. 5, 21). The paper should say that the triad it means is the hybrid, not the textbook two-sided market.

3. **"… and optimizes."** *Supported.* The economics models the platform as a profit maximiser over both sides (`rochet2006two`; `hagiu2015multi`); the insiders say what is optimised (bookings and conversions at Airbnb, `haldar2019applying`, p. 1; market clearing and allocation at Uber, `cohen2016using`, p. 7; incentive-compatible fulfilment, `garg2022driver`, p. 8); the sociologists add that the platform optimises partly by producing uncertainty (`stark2020algorithmic`, p. 55).

4. **"… forming an irreducible algorithmic triad."** *Supported in a narrower form; the word needs a definition.* The sources give three defensible senses (Simmel's formal one, Rochet–Tirole's non-neutrality, the ride-hail feedback structure), and none is Φ. No source computes Φ on a mediated triad, and Engel and Malone's Φ was estimated on peer groups. Two findings cut against a strong reading: counterparts can ally against or exit the mediator (`vallas2020what`, 16.7; `cruz2023digital`, p. 3), and the AI-MC literature, which is the HCI work closest to a mediated dyad, models the mediator as one party's agent and excludes platforms (`hancock2020ai`, p. 4). The abstract's next sentence ("having to game the mediator to coordinate with the other person") assumes the two counterparts each face the mediator; Rahman's case shows that coordinating with the other person can be the way the mediator is gamed.

5. **The implicit contrast with Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens's dyad.** *Supported by the sociologists, contested by the dominant management frame.* Kellogg, Valentine and Christin keep the dyad (employer–worker, algorithm as instrument); the paper's claim sides with Stark and Pais and Vallas and Schor against it and should say so, with the alliance and ride-hail evidence as the reason the customer is a party and not an input.
