# N — Feedback and presentation: evidence for and against the two conventions

Facet N, 2026-10-13. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.

*Audited 2026-10-13 (AUDIT_09.md); WRONG and OVERSTATED rows corrected in place.*

**What this facet covers.** Evidence the earlier facets did not hold on the two design conventions of outline sections 3 and 6. *Convention A* ("strategic offloading"): present a mechanism so that strategic manipulation is unnecessary and visibly so. *Convention B* ("radical feedback immediacy"): return the result of a decision quickly and plainly enough to learn from. Five questions: (Q1) experiments that manipulate the timing, granularity or form of feedback and measure behaviour; (Q2) information shown to platform workers and sellers; (Q3) experiments on how a strategy-proof or simple mechanism is presented; (Q4) display-design and learning principles the outline has not used; (Q5) evidence that immediacy is not enough or does harm. I do not repeat what facets E, H and I and the cards already hold (Wickens and Carswell abstract, Lurie and Swaminathan abstract, Hassidim abstracts, Li 2017, Rees-Jones and Skowronek, Bó and Hakimov abstract, Gonczarowski et al. 2023 theory, Gonzalez 2005, Kahneman and Klein 2009, Mejia and Parker, Li et al. 2026 on Lyft, Cook et al.).

**The question the synthesis answers.** Does anything beyond inference stand behind Convention B, does the evidence on Convention A say that a good presentation ends deviation, and which display principles should section 3 add?

**Legend for read status.** *Read in full* = I read the whole text. *Read in part* = I opened the text and read the named pages or sections. *Abstract only* = a database abstract (Consensus, Crossref, publisher page) and nothing else; every number and quotation for that source comes from the abstract. *Not accessible* = no text at all. **A source is quotable only at full-text depth (RESEARCH_PLAN §3). No source below reaches *read in full*. Five are *read in part*: Gonczarowski et al. 2024, Hakimov and Khanna 2025, Larson et al. 2016, Ye et al. 2022 (PMC full text) and Dreyfuss et al. 2019 (front matter only, so effectively abstract). Everything else is abstract only, and its quotations are for checking, not for print.** Where a paper has a published version and I read a working paper, the entry says so.

### B1. Feedback timing, granularity or form as the manipulated variable (Q1)

**tiefenbeck2018overcoming** — Tiefenbeck, V., Goette, L., Degen, K., Tasic, V., Fleisch, E., Lalive, R., & Staake, T. (2018). Overcoming salience bias: How real-time feedback fosters resource conservation. *Management Science, 64*(3), 1458–1476. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2016.2646. *Verification:* Crossref. *Read status:* abstract only (Consensus); the Nature Energy paper below (read in full) describes this study as a two-month trial with "an opt-in sample of 697 Swiss households" and a 22% saving (0.592 kWh per shower). *Reports:* a "large-scale field experiment" gave households "real-time feedback on the resource consumption" of showering. "Real-time feedback reduced resource consumption for the target behavior by 22%"; savings of 1.2 kWh per day and household; larger for high-baseline users (abstract). N not in the abstract. Run by the authors; the Nature Energy abstract says most large effects in this literature come from opt-in volunteer samples. *Bearing:* faster feedback helped, strongly. The task has a continuous quantity under the person's control, an instant display, a clean signal and no counterpart. The setting is the opposite of a triad.

**tiefenbeck2018real** — Tiefenbeck, V., Wörner, A., Schöb, S., Fleisch, E., & Staake, T. (2018/2019). Real-time feedback promotes energy conservation in the absence of volunteer selection bias and monetary incentives. *Nature Energy, 4*(1), 35–41. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41560-018-0282-1. *Verification:* Crossref; publisher page opened. *Read status:* **read in full** (open HSG repository copy, 8,010 words; two Publisher Corrections, March and September 2019, fix Table 3 values and a figure credit, not the headline numbers). *Reports:* "an uninformed sample of guests at 6 hotels (265 rooms, N = 19,596 observations)"; "11.4% reduction in energy use" in a context where guests "were not financially responsible for energy costs" (abstract, quoted). The outcome is energy per shower (0.215 kWh, from a control mean of 1.883 kWh), in a short stay. *Bearing:* helped, at about half the size of the opt-in estimate, and without a money motive. The authors say they "do not seek to quantify the self-selection effect": the two studies differ in duration (one or a few nights against two months), in setting, and in baseline use (control means 1.88 against 2.62 kWh per shower; they say the baseline gap "may also partially explain" the smaller effect). The 22% to 11.4% drop is not a measure of selection.

**fang2023complementarities** — Fang, X., Goette, L., Rockenbach, B., Sutter, M., Tiefenbeck, V., et al. (2023). Complementarities in behavioral interventions: Evidence from a field experiment on resource conservation. *Journal of Public Economics, 228*, 105028. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2023.105028. *Read status:* abstract only. *Reports:* of two interventions, shower energy reports (knowledge) and real-time feedback (salience), "only the latter induced significant conservation effects when implemented in isolation, each intervention became more effective when implemented jointly". *Bearing:* supports B over information about the system, in one setting. It also says feedback works better with an explanation of what the number means. That is a qualification of "radical immediacy" and a point against D3's "disclosure doesn't affect" read as a general rule.

**pereiradoel2024reducing** — Pereira-Doel, P., Font, X., Wyles, K., & Pereira-Moliner, J. (2024). Reducing shower duration in tourist accommodations. *Journal of Travel Research, 64*(5), 1100–1120. https://doi.org/10.1177/00472875241245045. *Read status:* abstract only. *Reports:* covert experiment, six accommodations in three countries, "over 17,500 showers"; a smart timer gave the feedback, coupled with persuasive messages; "continuous, real-time eco-feedback reduced water runtime by 25.79% (CI = 8.24%; 39.98%)" (abstract). The outcome is shower run-time, not energy per shower. *Bearing:* helped; the interval is wide, and the figure is not comparable with the 22% and 11.4% energy figures.

**karlin2015effects** — Karlin, B., Zinger, J. F., & Ford, R. (2015). The effects of feedback on energy conservation: A meta-analysis. *Psychological Bulletin, 141*(6), 1205–1227. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0039650. *Read status:* abstract only. *Reports:* 42 studies, 1976–2010. "feedback is effective overall, r = .071, p < .001, but with significant variation in effects (r varied from -.080 to .480)"; moderators include "frequency, medium, comparison message, duration, and combination with other interventions" (abstract). The abstract does not give the direction of the frequency effect. *Bearing:* the pooled effect is small and the range includes harm. Open the article for the frequency moderator before any claim that faster is better.

**kluger1996effects / kluger1998feedback** — Kluger, A. N., & DeNisi, A. (1996). The effects of feedback interventions on performance. *Psychological Bulletin, 119*(2), 254–284. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254. Kluger, A. N., & DeNisi, A. (1998). Feedback interventions. *Current Directions in Psychological Science, 7*(3), 67–72. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8721.ep10772989 (title as in Crossref). *Read status:* 1996 abstract (OpenAlex, checked at audit): 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations, average d = .41, "over 1/3 of the FIs decreased performance"; 1998 first paragraph only. *Reports (1998, opening paragraph):* feedback interventions "yield highly variable effects on performance"; "although FIs improve performance on average, they reduce performance in more than one third of the cases" (the extraction breaks "reduce" in two). *Bearing:* in this meta-analysis feedback interventions reduced performance in more than one third of the cases (effect sizes), across all kinds of feedback. This is a qualification of Convention B that any reviewer will know.

**thaler1997effect** — Thaler, R. H., Tversky, A., Kahneman, D., & Schwartz, A. (1997). The effect of myopia and loss aversion on risk taking: An experimental test. *QJE, 112*(2), 647–661. https://doi.org/10.1162/003355397555226. *Read status:* abstract only. *Reports (quoted):* "The investors who got the most frequent feedback (and thus the most information) took the least risk and earned the least money." *Bearing:* **faster feedback harmed.** Lab, students, a repeated risky choice. This is the clean counter-case: more information, more often, worse outcomes.

**gneezy1997experiment** — Gneezy, U., & Potters, J. (1997). An experiment on risk taking and evaluation periods. *QJE, 112*(2), 631–645. https://doi.org/10.1162/003355397555217. *Read status:* abstract only. *Reports (quoted):* "the more frequently returns are evaluated, the more risk averse investors will be." *Bearing:* same direction as Thaler et al. Consensus attached the abstract to two records, one labelled JEEA; Crossref confirms QJE.

**gneezy2003evaluation** — Gneezy, U., Kapteyn, A., & Potters, J. (2003). Evaluation periods and asset prices in a market experiment. *Journal of Finance, 58*(2), 821–837. https://doi.org/10.1111/1540-6261.00547. *Read status:* abstract only. *Reports:* "more information and more flexibility result in less risk taking. Market prices of risky assets are significantly higher if feedback frequency and decision flexibility are reduced." *Bearing:* the effect survives market interaction, so it is not a one-person artefact. A market is closer to a triad than a shower.

**langer2008does** — Langer, T., & Weber, M. (2008). Does commitment or feedback influence myopic loss aversion? *JEBO, 67*(3–4), 810–819. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2006.05.019. *Read status:* abstract only. *Reports:* "a strong effect depending on the length of commitment, a much less pronounced effect of feedback and a strong interaction between both variables." *Bearing:* qualifies the above. Part of the "frequent feedback harms" result is the freedom to change one's mind at each look. Feedback and the right to act on it are confounded in the earlier papers.

**larson2016can** — Larson, F., List, J. A., & Metcalfe, R. D. (2016). Can myopic loss aversion explain the equity premium puzzle? Evidence from a natural field experiment with professional traders. NBER Working Paper 22605. https://doi.org/10.3386/w22605. *Verification:* Crossref; PDF opened. *Read status:* **read in part** (abstract; §1; §2 design; §3 results; PDF pp. 1–11). *Reports:* foreign-exchange traders invited to beta-test a trading platform were randomised to "a frequent information group (second-by-second price information) or an infrequent information group (price information every four hours)" (§2, quoted), over two weeks. Abstract: infrequent-information traders "invest 33% more in risky assets, yielding profits that are 53% higher" (quoted), from "over 864,000 price realizations". In the last 20% of the experiment the infrequent group put 36.4% of the portfolio in the risky asset (n=73) and the frequent group 27.4% (n=78) (§3, PDF p. 10). The text says: "even though traders prefer more frequent feedback, they are certainly worse off when they receive such information" (§3, PDF p. 11). *Check:* the group sizes are swapped between PDF p. 10 and the daily-observation note a few lines earlier (n=73 frequent, n=78 infrequent in the earlier line); treat the sizes as unsettled (the text does not settle which is right). Working paper; I found no journal version in Crossref. Directions: the infrequent group invests 33% *more* than the frequent group (36.4/27.4) and earns 53% *more* (615,715 against 401,634 units); the frequent group therefore invests about 25% less and earns about 35% less. The risky "Fund A" returned an average of 236% per period because one week had an extra-high return, which the authors say "warrants caution" (fn. 5 and §2). The 864,000 is the maximum number of prices a trader could have seen; the infrequent group saw about 14 and the frequent group about 1,555 on average (fn. 7). *Bearing:* **faster feedback harmed, in a field experiment with professionals,** and users chose the harmful condition. This is the strongest single piece of evidence against "faster is better" in a setting with money at stake and an exchange as the third party. Who ran it: the authors; the first author's affiliation is "Normann" (London), and the working paper does not say more in the parts I read.

**lurie2009timely** (held, facet I) — Lurie, N. H., & Swaminathan, J. M. (2009). Is timely information always better? *OBHDP, 108*(2), 315–329. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2008.05.005. *Read status:* abstract only (the Elsevier PDF is bot-walled). Full abstract now in hand (quoted): "Across four inventory management experiments, we find that in environments characterized by random noise more frequent feedback on previous decisions leads to declines in performance. Receiving more frequent feedback leads to excessive focus on and more systematic processing of more recent data as well as a failure to adequately compare information across multiple time periods." *Bearing:* **harmed under noise**, and the mechanism is recency. The abstract states the condition. This is the sentence Convention B needs to carry.

**jessoe2014knowledge** — Jessoe, K., & Rapson, D. (2014). Knowledge is (less) power: Experimental evidence from residential energy use. *AER, 104*(4), 1417–1438. https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.104.4.1417. *Read status:* abstract only. *Reports:* randomised trial; the AER abstract says "Informed households are three standard deviations more responsive to temporary price increases, an effect that is not attributable to price salience. Conservation extends beyond pricing events in the short and medium run". The sentence the facet first quoted ("0 to 7 percent ... 8 to 22 percent") is not in the AER abstract; I found it only in a seminar summary, and it describes responses to price increases by households with and without information, not feedback alone [unverified: see AUDIT_09]. *Bearing:* helped, and it matches the project's inference: feedback lets people learn the quantity they are responding to. The display carried high-frequency usage and price. One of the better tests of "feedback makes a decision learnable".

**dahlinger2018impact** — Dahlinger, A., Tiefenbeck, V., Ryder, B., Gahr, B., Fleisch, E., et al. (2018). The impact of numerical vs. symbolic eco-driving feedback on fuel consumption. *Transportation Research Part D, 65*, 375–386. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trd.2018.09.013. *Read status:* abstract only. *Reports:* randomised field trial, "62 road assistance drivers over a period of 10 weeks, covering over 245,000 km"; "only the symbolic feedback design led to significant reductions of 2–3% in fuel consumption" (abstract, quoted); numerical feedback did not. *Bearing:* **form, not speed, decided the outcome.** Same speed of feedback; plain symbols worked and numbers and gauges did not. It is the nearest test of "plainly" in Convention B.

**sanguinetti2020average** — Sanguinetti, A., Queen, E., Yee, C., & Akanesuvan, K. (2020). Average impact and important features of onboard eco-driving feedback: A meta-analysis. *Transportation Research Part F, 70*, 1–14. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trf.2020.02.010. *Read status:* abstract only. *Reports:* 17 studies, 23 effect sizes, "6.6% improvement"; "Feedback that included information about both instantaneous and accumulated performance predicted larger effects"; "Length of feedback intervention negatively related to effects". *Bearing:* supports B for a controllable quantity; the decay with duration is a warning that novelty drives part of it.

**fyfe2021manyclasses** — Fyfe, E. R., de Leeuw, J. R., Carvalho, P. F., Goldstone, R. L., Sherman, J., et al. (2021). ManyClasses 1. *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 4*(3). https://doi.org/10.1177/25152459211027575. *Read status:* abstract only. *Reports:* "Across 38 classes, the overall estimate for the effect of feedback timing was 0.002 (95% highest density interval = [−0.05, 0.05])" (quoted), immediate against delayed by a few days; no credible effect for 40 preregistered moderators. *Bearing:* **timing alone did nothing** in the best-powered test of timing I found. Education, not decision-making, and the delay was days, not seconds.

**vanderkleij2015effects / mckay2022metaanalysis** — Van der Kleij, F. M., Feskens, R. C. W., & Eggen, T. J. H. M. (2015). Effects of feedback in a computer-based learning environment on students' learning outcomes. *Review of Educational Research, 85*(4), 475–511. https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314564881. McKay, B., et al. (2022). Meta-analysis of the reduced relative feedback frequency effect on motor learning and performance. *Psychology of Sport and Exercise, 61*, 102165. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2022.102165. *Read status:* both abstract only. *Reports:* 40 studies, 70 effect sizes; "elaborated feedback (EF; e.g., providing an explanation) produced larger effect sizes (0.49) than feedback regarding the correctness of the answer (KR; 0.05) or providing the correct answer (KCR; 0.32)"; "negatively affected by delayed feedback timing", with no significant timing-by-task interaction. McKay et al.: 61 papers, k = 75, N = 2,228, "no significant effect of reduced feedback frequency at any time point" and the studies "severely underpowered". *Bearing:* what the feedback says (elaboration) matters more than when. The motor-learning literature, often cited for "feedback less often is better", does not support a claim either way.

### B2. Information shown to platform workers and sellers (Q2)

**ye2022virtual** — Ye, T., Ai, W., Chen, Y., Mei, Q., Ye, J., & Zhang, L. (2022). Virtual teams in a gig economy. *PNAS, 119*(51). https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2206580119. *Verification:* Crossref; PMC9907148. *Read status:* **read in part** (abstract, design, Table 1, competing-interest statement; HTML, no page numbers). *Reports:* natural field experiment, "27,790 drivers across three cities" in China, autumn 2018, on a ride-sharing platform. Teams were randomised to a team leaderboard, an individual ranking within the team, or "only individual performance information (control)"; "With the exception of the normal piece rate, there is no additional monetary incentive in any of the experimental conditions" (Results, quoted). Table 1 (all cities, difference-in-differences, weekly revenue): treated in a virtual team +34.53 CNY (SE 15.37), against a control of own-performance information; the paper puts this at 1.66% of average weekly revenue (p < 0.05), pooling the team-ranking and individual-ranking arms, over a 3-week intervention in fall 2018 (a 1-week cash-prize contest came before it). Abstract: treated drivers "work longer hours and generate significantly higher revenue". **Who ran it:** the platform; "J.Y. and L.Z. were employees of the platform" (Competing interest, quoted). *Bearing:* what the platform shows a worker (a ranking against peers) changes hours. The information raised effort, which serves the platform. It is feedback that does not help the worker understand the mediator. It speaks to the risk in "immediacy" as a goal.

**ai2023putting** — Ai, W., Chen, Y., Mei, Q., Ye, J., & Zhang, L. (2023). Putting teams into the gig economy. *Management Science, 69*(9), 5336–5353. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4624. *Read status:* abstract only. *Reports:* team contests for cash prizes; treated drivers "work longer hours and earn 12% higher revenue during the contest", half the effect two weeks later. Platform-funded (Funding note in the abstract). *Bearing:* as above; a prize contest, not a display change.

**bargill2024helping** (was bargill2023helping) — Bar-Gill, S., Brynjolfsson, E., & Hak, N. (2024). Helping small businesses become more data-driven: A field experiment on eBay. *Management Science, 70*(11), 7345–7372. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2021.02026 (working paper: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4407497). *Read status:* abstract only for the Management Science version (published as 70(11), 7345-7372, 2024, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2021.02026; its Crossref abstract says "more than a third"); the NBER working paper w31089 was read in part at audit (SH launched in 2016 "using eBay's experimentation platform", seven randomised ramp-up stages; 3.6% is the effect of access on weekly sales; the "over a third" comes from a moderation analysis among early adopters: 2.5% standalone plus 1.4% per weekly visit to the Performance tab). *Reports:* "randomized introduction of eBay's Seller Hub (SH), a data-rich seller dashboard"; "access to SH increases e-retailers' revenues by 3.6% on average, as more items are transacted and service quality increases, without increases in average prices"; "more than a third of the SH impact is driven by active performance monitoring" (quoted). *Bearing:* a worker-facing analytics display with a randomised measured effect. It helped sellers, and the gain came through monitoring, i.e. through reading. That is a literacy-type route (Part 1, line 66, and D3). It supports information for sellers, and complicates the claim that reading dashboards is the wrong remedy.

**fong2026how** — Fong, J., Manchanda, P., & Song, Y. (2026). How effective is suggested pricing? Experimental evidence from an e-commerce platform. *Journal of Marketing Research, 63*(5), 789–810. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222437261432198. *Read status:* abstract only. *Reports:* field experiment "in collaboration with Mercari"; "a 30% change in suggested prices leads to a 5% change in listing prices in the same direction"; stronger "for new sellers and used items"; lower suggested prices raised the chance of sale and seller revenue. *Bearing:* a default-like suggestion moves seller choices and is most influential when the task is hard. It fits Convention A's logic of taking a task off the person. It also shows how a platform's suggestion steers.

### B4. Display-design and learning principles (Q4)

Each item: the claim, the best evidence I opened, and one sentence on application. Application sentences are my inference and are marked so.

**1. Proximity compatibility principle.** *Claim* (held, facet E, abstract only): "Displays relevant to a common task or mental operation (close task or mental proximity) should be rendered close together in perceptual space (close display proximity)" (Wickens & Carswell 1995, abstract). *Best evidence I opened:* none beyond the abstract; the 1995 review is the evidence. *Application (inference):* the pieces a person must combine to judge a mediated decision (what they asked, what the mediator did, what it cost them) are the "close mental proximity" set, so they belong in one place, and unrelated items belong apart. Convention B's "plainly" is a proximity requirement.

**2. Split attention and cognitive load.** *Claim:* learners do better when related information is integrated and not split across space or time. *Evidence (abstracts):* Ginns (2006), 50 studies, "increasing either the spatial or temporal contiguity of related elements of information can lead to substantial learning gains", especially for complex material; Schroeder and Cenkci (2018), "58 independent comparisons (n = 2426) produced an overall effect size of g = 0.63". **Qualifier:** Schroeder and Cenkci (2020), 41 comparisons: "measures of cognitive load largely do not support the extraneous cognitive load explanation, nor did we find compelling evidence that integrated graphic designs significantly influence any measure of cognitive load". The effect is robust. Sweller's explanation of it is not supported by load measures. *Application (inference):* the menu description is an integrated presentation of a proof that would otherwise be spread across steps; cite the effect, and do not claim cognitive load as its mechanism. Sweller (1988, 1998) are in the bib as not accessible; Kalyuga's expertise-reversal paper (abstract only, in the bib) is titled for the finding that integration helps novices more than experts (I read only the title and abstract record); I have not tested whether that bears on the higher baseline understanding Gonczarowski et al. report at Cornell.

**3. Stimulus–response compatibility.** *Claim:* responses are faster and more accurate when the layout or meaning of the response set corresponds to the stimulus set. *Evidence (abstracts):* Chan and Chan (2009), 32 participants, "an improvement of 135 ms in reaction time was obtained with the correspondence of the stimulus and response key" (quoted); Tsang et al. (2020), "the more incompatible the stimulus-response mapping, the longer the delay for both tasks". These are spatial, motor and reaction-time results, far from decisions. I did not find or open a review (Proctor and Vu). *Application (inference):* the mechanism's report format is the response set and the user's preferences are the stimulus set; Hakimov and Khanna's attribute-bundle result (error 35% where surface and true dominance conflict) is a conceptual version, but they do not call it SRC. The transfer is an analogy.

**4. Affordances, signifiers, and the gulfs of execution and evaluation.** *Claim:* a user must bridge a gulf between intention and available action (execution) and between system state and interpretation (evaluation). *Evidence (abstracts only):* Vermeulen et al. (2013): "feedforward is a powerful design principle for bridging Norman's Gulf of Execution"; Djajadiningrat et al. (2002) argue "the essence of usability in electronic products lies not in communicating the necessary action" but in "feedforward and inherent feedback". Both are design-theory papers with no behavioural test. **Norman's own texts (the gulfs; the signifier) were not opened;** I cannot give a locator for them. The bib holds Hutchins, Hollan and Norman (1985), which is where the gulfs are usually traced; I did not re-read it for this facet. *Application (inference):* Convention A narrows the gulf of execution (what do I submit?) and Convention B narrows the gulf of evaluation (what did the mediator do with it?). The pair has a ready vocabulary in HCI.

**5. Feedforward.** *Claim:* showing what an action will do before it is taken. *Evidence:* Gonzalez 2005 (held, abstract only): feedforward "improved their performance considerably and continued to exhibit improved performance even after discontinuation", where "Neither outcome feedback nor cognitive feedback resulted in improved performance" (one lab task). Vermeulen et al. above. *Application (inference):* a preview of what a submission will produce is the missing partner of Convention B, and it is the one that works when the result arrives too late or too noisily to learn from.

**6. Direct manipulation** is already carded (shneiderman1983direct); I add nothing.

## (c) Synthesis by question

**Q1. Does faster, finer or plainer feedback change behaviour?** Yes, in one kind of setting and no or worse in another. *Helped:* feedback on a quantity the person controls continuously, read from a clean signal: showering (22% less energy per shower in an opt-in sample of 697 Swiss households over two months; 11.4% less energy per shower among hotel guests, 19,596 observations, in a short stay; 25.8% less water run-time with a shower timer in more than 17,500 showers, CI 8.2 to 40.0%: three outcomes and settings, not one estimate), residential electricity (households with high-frequency usage information were three standard deviations more responsive to temporary price increases and kept conserving beyond pricing events; the "8 to 22% against 0 to 7%" ranges are [unverified: see AUDIT_09] and concern responses to price increases, not feedback alone), driving (6.6% pooled fuel-economy gain; 2 to 3% less fuel with symbols, no significant reduction with numbers and gauges). *Harmed:* repeated risky choices under noise, with the freedom to act on each look: students (Thaler et al.; Gneezy and Potters), markets (Gneezy et al.), professional FX traders in the field (with second-by-second prices the frequent group held 27.4% of its portfolio in the risky asset against 36.4% with four-hourly prices, and earned 401,634 units of profit against 615,715; equivalently the infrequent group invested 33% more and earned 53% more; working paper), inventory decisions under random noise (Lurie and Swaminathan). *Null:* timing as the only variable, in 38 classes (0.002, 95% HDI [-0.05, 0.05]; immediate against delayed by a few days). *Base rate:* in Kluger and DeNisi's meta-analysis (607 effect sizes, average d = .41) feedback interventions reduced performance in more than one third of the cases (1996 abstract; 1998 paper). *What decided the outcome was not speed.* It was the signal's validity, the form (symbols against numbers; elaboration against correctness), and whether the person could change course at each look. So Convention B has support, but it is conditional, and the condition is the one Kahneman and Klein already set ("rapid and unequivocal" [unverified: see AUDIT_09]; their abstract says valid cues and an opportunity to learn, held): speed is the part that hurts when the signal is noisy. No study in this set involved two humans and an adaptive third party. The inference in T_adjudication D7 remains an inference, now with a better-defined boundary.

**Q2. Information shown to workers and sellers.** The measured effects are real and mostly serve the platform or the seller who reads. A ranking among peers raised hours and revenue (+34.53 CNY of weekly revenue, 1.66% of the average and p < 0.05, against drivers who saw only their own performance; 27,790 drivers in three cities; run with the platform, two authors being platform employees); a seller dashboard raised weekly sales by 3.6% on average (randomised roll-out on eBay's own experimentation platform), with over a third of the effect coming through active performance monitoring among early adopters; a suggested price moved listings by 5% for a 30% change (Mercari). Who ran each: the platform or its collaborators, in every randomised case. No controlled test of upfront fares, destination display, earnings dashboards for drivers or surge maps was found; the workers' own accounts say they need them and build their own tools. The seller-dashboard result sits awkwardly with "reading is the wrong remedy": over a third of the gain came from reading.

**Q3. Presenting a strategy-proof mechanism.** The best presentation tested (a stripped-down menu statement of strategyproofness) raised understanding from 54% to 71% and straightforward play from 48% to 59% (N = 542; p = 0.01 against the Null, unadjusted). The straightforward share stays at 59%; the authors call the behavioural shift small on average. Two things moved the results. Stating the property and not the mechanics moved understanding (Menu SP 71% over Menu DA 58% and Traditional DA 56%), though not straightforward play in the same way: Traditional DA's 56% is not distinguishable from Menu SP's 59% (p = 0.47), and Menu DA (50%) and Textbook SP (53%) are not distinguishable from the Null. Having understood it moved play (80 to 100% above about 75 to 80% understanding). Describing the mechanics did nothing for understanding of strategyproofness and in Guillén and Hakimov's field experiment reduced truth-telling. Showing subjects the out-of-equilibrium strategies played by others reduced truth-telling (Guillén and Hakimov 2017, a within-subject lab design). Showing a process (the clock, drop-outs) changed behaviour more than the theoretical obviousness did (Breitmoser and Schweighofer-Kodritsch). Part of the residue is representational error with no allocation incentive attached (Hakimov and Khanna: where reversed dominance makes the truly better option look worse, participants side with surface dominance in 35% of those pairs against 6% where dominance and utility align; the 94% against 58% contrast compares the accuracy treatment with the direct serial-dictatorship treatment and mixes incentive with framing, as the authors say), and part may be intended (Dreyfuss et al.; Chen et al. think it small). So Convention A as worded ("unnecessary and visibly so") is supported for understanding and weakly for behaviour; the figure to put in the paper is about four in ten rounds still not straightforward in the best condition.

**Q4. Principles.** Add proximity compatibility and split attention (g = 0.63 over 58 comparisons [unverified: see AUDIT_09]; measures of cognitive load largely do not support the load explanation); the gulfs of execution and evaluation and feedforward (design theory, abstract only); stimulus–response compatibility only as an analogy. See B4 for the sentence on each.

**Q5. Immediacy is not enough.** Faster and more frequent hurt under noise (Lurie and Swaminathan; Larson et al.); people learning from experience rely on small samples of recent outcomes and show a hot stove effect, avoiding options that once hurt (Plonsky et al.; Fujikawa; abstracts only, "over-react" is my reading); notifications helped the informed and harmed the mistaken (Chapkovski et al.); gamified nudges raised risk-taking, most for low literacy; feedback that serves the platform raised hours. The counter-evidence is thin: colour-coding blunts noise; a simulation says fast learners can do well. The convention to defend is "quickly *and* validly *and* plainly", and "radical" is the word the evidence argues with.

### Claims in the paper's position that these sources contradict or qualify

1. **"Return the result quickly and the person learns."** Qualified by Lurie and Swaminathan, Thaler et al., Gneezy and Potters, Larson et al. and ManyClasses (null for timing alone). Contradicted for noisy, repeated, revisable choices.
2. **"Immediacy" as the active ingredient of Convention B.** Dahlinger et al. (form), Van der Kleij et al. (elaboration, 0.49 against 0.05) and Cardinaels et al. (colour coding) put the effect in the form of the signal.
3. **Convention A removes the need to strategise, visibly.** About 41% of play in the best condition is not straightforward; the effect on average behaviour is small (Gonczarowski et al. 2024); some deviation is intended or representational (Dreyfuss et al.; Hakimov and Khanna).
4. **Explaining the mechanism helps.** Contradicted for understanding (Menu DA and Traditional DA did not rise above the Null) and for truth-telling in Guillén and Hakimov 2018, where describing the mechanism lowered it. On straightforward play, Traditional DA (56%) is not distinguishable from Menu SP (59%).
5. **Obviously strategy-proof as the thing that works.** Qualified for clock auctions: in the replication, the dynamic structure had no significant effect and framing and feedback did (Breitmoser and Schweighofer-Kodritsch). Li's serial-dictatorship result (36% against 7%) was not tested there.
6. **Disclosure is a literacy-based intervention and does not help (D3).** Qualified: the working treatment was a plain statement of a property; in showering, an explanation raised the effect of feedback (Fang et al.); a seller dashboard worked by being read (Bar-Gill et al.).
7. **Showing the user more information.** Qualified in one lab setting: information about the out-of-equilibrium strategies of others lowered truth-telling (Guillén and Hakimov 2017). That is information about others, not a display of the mediator's own process.
8. **Cognitive load explains why integrated displays work.** Largely not supported by load measures (Schroeder and Cenkci 2020, abstract), though the integration effect stands.
9. **Worker-facing feedback is good for the worker.** Qualified: the randomised cases serve platform goals (hours, revenue), and the transparent-nudge survey found no earnings gain.

## What the outline should add

*Section 3 (the human factors of re-representation).*

- **3.a. Name the two gulfs.** Claim: Convention A narrows the gulf of execution and Convention B the gulf of evaluation; the scribes' move did both for the reader. Sources: Vermeulen et al. 2013 (names only the gulf of execution in its abstract) and Djajadiningrat et al. 2002 (neither abstract names the gulf of evaluation, so that half rests on Hutchins, Hollan and Norman 1985, not re-read); Norman's own text and Hutchins, Hollan and Norman 1985 still to be opened.
- **3.b. Proximity compatibility and the split-attention effect, with the qualifier.** Claim: bringing together what a person must combine is a general display rule with a measured effect, g = 0.63 over 58 comparisons [unverified: see AUDIT_09], and measures of cognitive load largely do not support the load explanation. Sources: Wickens and Carswell 1995 (abstract only, held); Schroeder and Cenkci 2018 and 2020; Ginns 2006 (all abstract only).
- **3.c. Representational error as a human-factors problem.** Claim: with no incentive to misreport, people still err when the report format does not match how they hold their preferences; that is the response-compatibility problem at the level of meaning. Sources: Hakimov and Khanna 2025 (read in part); Chan and Chan 2009 and Tsang et al. 2020 for the motor analogue (abstract only; use only as analogy).
- **3.d. Feedback validity before feedback speed.** Claim: learning by acting needs a valid signal, and speed turns harmful when the signal is noisy. Sources: Kahneman and Klein 2009 (held); Lurie and Swaminathan 2009 (abstract); Larson et al. 2016 (read in part); Plonsky et al. (abstract only).

*Section 6 (DXD conventions).*

- **6.a. Convention A: replace "theory only" with an experiment and report the residue.** Claim: stating the property of strategyproofness raises understanding from 54% to 71% and straightforward play from 48% to 59% (N = 542; p = 0.01 against the Null, unadjusted; the authors call the behavioural effect small); describing the mechanics does not raise understanding, though on straightforward play Traditional DA (56%) is not distinguishable from Menu SP (59%). Sources: Gonczarowski et al. 2024 (read in part); Guillén and Hakimov 2018 and Katuščák and Kittsteiner 2025 (abstract only). Report "about four in ten rounds still not straightforward" in the same sentence.
- **6.b. Convention A: add that showing a process counts as much as the property.** Claim: in the clock-auction replication, framing and feedback moved behaviour and dynamic structure did not. Sources: Breitmoser and Schweighofer-Kodritsch 2022 (abstract only); Li 2017 (held); Bó and Hakimov 2024 (abstract only).
- **6.c. Convention B: restate it as valid, plain and (only then) quick, and say it is tested outside triads.** Claim: real-time feedback on a controllable quantity reduces energy per shower by 22% (opt-in households) and 11.4% (hotel guests), and fuel use by 2 to 3% with a symbolic display but not significantly with a numeric one (Dahlinger); frequent feedback on noisy, revisable choices harms. Drop the Jessoe and Rapson 8 to 22% figure [unverified: see AUDIT_09]. Sources: Tiefenbeck et al. 2018 Nature Energy (read in full; the Management Science paper abstract only), Dahlinger et al. 2018 (abstract only); Lurie and Swaminathan 2009 (abstract); Larson et al. 2016 (read in part). This is Claude's restatement of what the sources show. Whether "radical" survives is the author's call.
- **6.d. Add feedforward as the companion to B.** Claim: a preview before the act improved lasting performance where outcome feedback did not. Sources: Gonzalez 2005 (held, abstract only); Vermeulen et al. 2013 (abstract only).
- **6.e. Platform-side evidence, with who ran it.** Claim: what a platform shows its workers and sellers changes behaviour, and every randomised case was run by or with the platform. Sources: Ye et al. 2022 (read in part), Bar-Gill et al. 2024 (Management Science 70(11); working paper read in part), Fong et al. 2026 (abstract only). State that no controlled test of upfront fares or surge maps was found.
- **6.f. A guard against gamified immediacy.** Claim: fast notifications helped the informed and amplified mistakes in the mistaken; gamified nudges raised risk-taking. Sources: Chapkovski et al. 2024 and 2025 (abstract only). Sailer and Homner 2019 does not support this item: its abstract reports small positive effects of gamification on learning outcomes (g = .25 to .49), so cite it only for "small and less stable on behaviour".
- **6.g. Resolve the D3 tension in the text.** The treatment that worked in 6.a is a statement of a property. The paper needs a sentence saying why that is a design convention and not a literacy intervention, or it should accept that the boundary is blurred. Sources: Gonczarowski et al. 2024 (p. 4, read); Fang et al. 2023 (abstract only).
