## Working title

*The Space Between Decisions: Word Separation as a Guide to Designing for Algorithmacy*

## Abstract

Human factors has a standing answer to a task that only the trained can do: change the representation
of the task and leave the person as they are. This paper takes the oldest case of that answer, the
scribes' move from continuous script to spaces between words, as a guide to a new problem. People now
decide through algorithmic third parties that read both sides of an exchange and commit outcomes
neither side controls. The usual remedy is disclosure—explanations, published rules, logs—and Satya
Nadella's (2026) proposal to treat models as "insider risks" is its most prominent recent statement. I
argue that disclosure is a literacy-based intervention: it hands the person a text and asks for a
reader. Algorithmacy, the sensibility to navigate irreducible triads, is exercised in deciding. I review
the history of word separation, restate the scribes' move in the
vocabulary of distributed cognition, and assemble the evidence on disclosure, which points in more than
one direction and which I did not find tested where two people meet through a mediator. I then identify
two candidate conventions for Decision Experience Design, compare courts, foreign-exchange markets and
platforms to ask who could hold them, and state the test that remains.

*(200 words.)*

## Introduction

<!-- 1. Common ground -->
Human factors has a standing answer to a task that only the trained can do. The answer is to change the
representation of the task and leave the person as they are. Zhang and Norman (1994) gave the principle
its name—the representational effect, in which "different isomorphic representations of a common formal
structure can cause dramatically different cognitive behaviors" (p. 88)—and Hutchins (1995) showed it at
work in a cockpit, where movable markers on the airspeed indicator let pilots reach "the same results"
by "judgments of spatial proximity" (p. 282) that they would otherwise reach by reading, remembering and
comparing numbers. The oldest case of the principle is the page. Roman and early medieval books ran
their letters together without a break. Irish scribes were separating words by the end of the seventh
century, Continental scribes took up the practice from the middle of the tenth, and by the middle of the
eleventh it was standard (Saenger, 1997, pp. 83, 100, 176–177). The space added no information, since
every letter was already on the page. It changed what a reader had to do with the letters, and readers
who have the convention do badly without it: Rayner, Fischer, and Pollatsek (1998) report that taking
the spaces out of English text cuts reading rate by about half.

<!-- 2. Complication -->
That answer has not reached the decisions people now make through algorithms. A rider and a driver, a
seller and a buyer, an applicant and an employer meet through a third party that reads both of them and
commits an outcome neither controls. Faced with a third party that people cannot read, designers and
regulators have reached for disclosure: explain the model, publish the rules, keep the log. The evidence
on disclosure points in more than one direction. Jhaver, Appling, Gilbert, and Bruckman (2019) surveyed
Reddit users whose posts had been removed; those who received an explanation judged the removal fairer,
and those who had read the community's rules beforehand judged it less fair. Seira, Elizondo, and
Laguna-Müggenburg (2017) put disclosures on credit-card statements; the message stating the interest
rate changed nothing, and the message stating how many months the debt would take to repay raised
delinquency among two groups of clients. Poursabzi-Sangdeh and colleagues (2021) showed people a clear
model with few features and found them less able to detect and correct its mistake on an unusual case
in three of four experiments. Matsuo, Liu, Hiraoka, and Wada (2026) supply the strongest result on the
other side: in a driving simulator, one of fifteen drivers given a warning cue with its reason collided,
against six of fifteen given the cue alone. That study has a person and a machine and no second party.
In the sources searched for this paper I found no study that tests disclosure against a behavioural
outcome where two people meet through a mediator.

<!-- 3. Concern -->
The gap matters because of what disclosure asks of a person. A disclosure hands the person a text—a
rule, a rationale, a record—and so asks for a reader, which makes it an intervention in literacy.
Deciding through a third party draws on something else. I call it algorithmacy, the sensibility to
navigate irreducible triads, as literacy is the sensibility that reading and writing require. A remedy
built for readers adds to the reading of the people it means to help and leaves their decision where it
was. It also settles, without argument, who holds the remedy. Nadella's (2026) proposal to treat
frontier models as "insider risks" is the clearest recent case. He asks that every meaningful model
action leave "tamper-proof human readable evidence," that an authorized person be able to halt a model
in mid-task, and that organizations "separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it."
Each provision serves the organization that deploys the model. What the proposal gives a person is a
record to read and a brake to pull, and the people on whom the model acts enter it once, as the
recipients of a notice after something has failed. The scribes shared their readers' interest in an
easier page. A platform that owns the interface between two parties has interests of its own, and in
all fourteen sets of platform terms that Suzor (2018) examined, the terms "provided broad, unfettered
discretion to platform owners."

<!-- 4. Course of action -->
I therefore return to the page and ask what the scribes did, using their history as a guide and not as
proof. A guide is all the history can be. Saenger (1997) writes that his book "is not a general history
of literacy" (p. x), classicists dispute his account of how unspaced script was read, and the nearest he
comes to a claim about training is a single sentence: word separation and three other devices in
Insular books "provided shortcuts for achieving the reading skills that an elite among the ancients had
mastered only through a prolonged and arduous grammatical apprenticeship" (p. 85). I review that history
and its dispute, restate the scribes' move in the vocabulary of distributed cognition, and mark it off
from the two things it is usually mistaken for, disclosure and training. I then carry the question
across. I propose that deciding has taken the place reading and writing held: people come to understand
a mediator by acting through it and taking in what comes back, and they can learn that way only where
"feedback that is both rapid and unequivocal" follows the act (Kahneman & Klein, 2009, p. 524). From
that premise I identify two candidate conventions. One removes a strategic task from the people a
mediator coordinates and shows them that it is gone. The other returns the result of a decision quickly
and plainly enough to learn from. I close by comparing courts, dealer markets in foreign exchange and
digital platforms, to ask who could hold a convention against the party that owns the interface. I take
the triad to be irreducible in the sense the lab gauges with integrated information (Φ), and I do not
recompute that result here.

<!-- 5. Contribution -->
The paper makes three contributions to human factors. It supplies a precedent with its limits attached:
word separation as a case of re-representation, set out without the claim that spacing spread reading,
which none of the histories of literacy searched for this paper makes. It draws a line between
literacy-based and algorithmacy-based interventions, a line that sorts the remedies now on offer by what
each asks a person to do and gives a reason for disclosure's uneven record. And it gives Decision
Experience Design its object—the conventions by which people decide through a third party—together with
two candidates for the most important of them and the conditions of a test that I did not find in the
literature. The sections that follow take these in order: the page before and after word separation,
the scribes' move in human-factors terms, disclosure as a literacy-based intervention, the move from
reading to deciding, the candidate conventions, the question of who holds them, and the test.

### 2. The page before and after (about 15%)

- **What changed, where and when.** Continuous script in Roman and early medieval books; Irish
  separation by the late seventh century; Continental adoption from the mid-tenth; standard by the
  mid-eleventh (Saenger, 1997, pp. 83, 100, 176–177 `[lib]` for a print check of pages). State which
  spelling the paper uses: Saenger writes *scriptura continua*; Parkes and the classicists write
  *scriptio continua*.
- **A known aid, used selectively.** Romans used word dividers and gave them up (Heilmann, 2020,
  p. 182). Ancient teachers divided words and syllables for pupils (Cribiore, 1996, p. 48 `[lib]`). The
  convention was available; who it was provided for is the variable.
- **What Saenger claims, and under his name.** Speed, silent reading, reference reading, the scholastic
  book; the p. 85 "shortcuts" sentence, which names four devices. He disclaims a history of literacy
  (p. x).
- **The dispute.** Knox (1968): "Ancient books were normally read aloud" (p. 435), with silent reading
  attested. Vatri (2012, via Heilmann, 2020, p. 181 `[lib]` for Vatri) rejects the physiological
  mechanism. Svenbro (in Cavallo & Chartier, 1999 `[lib]`) calls unspaced script "not an insurmountable
  obstacle" and still cites Saenger for word division as a condition of silent reading's spread.
- **What nobody claims.** No history of literacy searched credits separation with more readers; the
  histories of medieval schooling describe letters, syllables and the psalter continuing afterwards
  (snippets; `[lib]`). The paper does not say "ignited literacy" and does not say reading "no longer
  required the training" in its own voice.
- **A complex, with one large member.** Separation arrived with punctuation, the hyphen and layout
  (Saenger; Parkes, 1992 `[lib]`); the parallel cases are bundles too (Hangul: word separation became a
  principle only in 1933, Ivanova, 2026, p. 144; numerals; staff notation; double entry, where Yamey and
  Ryan still dispute cause) `[author]` for "the most critical move".

### 3. What the scribes did, in human-factors terms (about 15%)

- **Re-representation.** The representational effect (Zhang & Norman, 1994, p. 88); a conceptual task
  given a perceptual implementation (Hutchins, 1995, pp. 282–283); diagrams that replace search and
  inference with perception (Larkin & Simon, 1987).
- **Not disclosure, not training.** The same letters before and after; no new information; no change in
  the reader. Offloading built into the task environment by a designer (Risko & Gilbert, 2016, Box 3).
  Kirsh (1995) on arranging space to cue another person.
- **The measured cost, in one direction.** Removing spaces from readers used to them costs about half
  of reading rate (Rayner et al., 1998 `[abs]`); the "40–70%" figure belongs to Rayner and Pollatsek, as
  cited by Mirault et al. (2019).
- **The other direction is mixed.** Added spaces: no gain for skilled Chinese adults (Bai et al., 2008:
  5,221 against 5,239 ms); faster words and slower sentences in Thai (Chantavarin & Leung, 2025; Winskel
  et al., 2009); a gain in Tibetan (Wang et al., 2024); a loss for child learners of Chinese (Bassetti &
  Masterson, 2012, submitted version). The paper therefore claims dependence on a convention once it is
  in place, and does not claim that spacing opens reading to learners.
- **Course crosswalk.** Perception (boundary detection, crowding), cognition (task transformation,
  offloading), design (who the page is built for).
- **Where the analogy strains.** A page holds still and its designer knows the constraints; an adaptive
  mediator with interests does neither (Vicente & Rasmussen, 1992; Part 1, line 118).

### 4. Disclosure as a literacy-based intervention (about 18%)

- **The ruling, stated** `[author]`: disclosure hands the person a text and asks for a reader; it is not
  an algorithmacy-based intervention. Wording the evidence carries: disclosure "has not been shown to
  change how well a person navigates a mediated coordination".
- **Evidence where disclosure did nothing or harm.** Jhaver et al. (2019, rule-readers, β = −.314);
  Seira et al. (2017, pp. 292, 295); Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al. (2021); Cabrera et al. (2023: accuracy
  rose mostly through heavier reliance).
- **Evidence where it helped.** Matsuo et al. (2026: 1, 6 and 7 collisions of 15); Vasconcelos et al.
  (2023: the hard task only); Jhaver et al. (2019: an explanation raised perceived fairness, β = .384).
  None is a triad with a behavioural outcome.
- **The field stops short of it.** Korbak et al. (2025, p. 7) decline to recommend showing chain of
  thought to users; the EU Platform-to-Business Regulation says providers are "not required to disclose
  algorithms" (Art. 5(6)).
- **The insider post, read closely.** Its question is how an organization contains a model it cannot
  read. Neuralese (P9) is a problem of reading a model, which is not algorithmacy `[author]`. What it
  gives a person: evidence (P15), audit (P18), incident disclosure (P20), a brake (P19). What it keeps
  for the organization: model diversity (P14), independent controls (P11–P12, P17). It concedes that
  chain-of-thought transparency is "not sufficient or dependable" (P9). The enterprise reading
  `[author]`, D5: the people the model acts upon appear only in P20.
- **Caution.** The paper does not say "his only move is disclosure" or "the exact opposite", and does
  not rest on people reviewing records badly: insider-threat practice endorses review by people (CERT,
  2022, practice 12), and the one direct measure is modest (Kutasov et al., 2025: AUC 0.65, 103 minutes
  a transcript).

### 5. From reading and writing to deciding (about 14%)

- **The proposal** `[author]`: understanding now comes through deciding and taking in the results.
- **Support.** Weick (1988): "people think by acting", with his warning that action can intensify the
  crisis it clarifies. Kirsh and Maglio (1994, p. 513): epistemic actions "make mental computation
  easier, faster, or more reliable". Drivers with over 2,500 trips earn 14% more an hour than those
  under 100 (Cook et al., working paper). Heiland's (2023) own trials corrected riders' false theories.
- **The condition.** A valid environment and "feedback that is both rapid and unequivocal" (Kahneman &
  Klein, 2009, p. 524). Delay and noise do different damage (Rahmandad & Gary, 2023, pp. 13, 17). Part 1,
  line 66: the mediator disrupts both conditions.
- **The pilot analogy, narrowed.** Pilots do monitor (Hutchins, 1995, pp. 283, 285). What holds: crews
  caught 35.4% of deviations in primary procedures, 14.4% in checklists and 5.6% in monitoring (Dismukes
  & Berman, 2010). People correct what they do more often than what they watch.
- **Caution.** No source gives this as a historical shift; it is the paper's proposal.

### 6. Candidate conventions (about 14%)

- **A rule that removes a strategic task and shows it is gone.** Li (2017, working paper, §5.3: 36%
  against 7% of games off the dominant outcome); Rees-Jones and Skowronek (2018: 23% still misreport in
  a strategy-proof match, N = 1,714). Limits: clearinghouses have no objective of their own; the removed
  task moves elsewhere (Arteaga et al., 2022).
- **A convention that returns the result of a decision quickly and plainly** `[inf]`. Follows from
  section 5. Thin and mixed evidence for loop-shortening tools (Kuhl et al., 2023 did not replicate its
  own gain).
- **How the two relate.** The first takes a task away; the second keeps the person acting. The paper
  should say whether they serve different decisions or compete.
- **What does not hold.** Bounded control beating free control (Dietvorst et al., 2018: not significant
  under random assignment); "the more demanding design worked best" (Buçinca et al., 2021: only where
  the AI was wrong).

### 7. Who holds the convention (about 12%)

- **Three settings, described and not ranked** (Tyler, 1988, p. 131: "no universally fair procedures").
- **Courts.** Rules written by judges under statute; an interested judge is barred (*Tumey v. Ohio*,
  1927); cost, delay and repeat players (Galanter, 1974).
- **Dealer foreign exchange.** The FX Global Code asks much (Principles 7, 9, 36, 39, 40) and binds
  little; the 4pm fix was manipulated by dealers, and five banks were fined £1.1bn (FCA, 2014).
- **Platforms.** Owners' discretion in their own terms (Suzor, 2018); thin appeals (Klonick, 2018,
  p. 1665), in a system she also calls "impressively intricate and responsive" (p. 1664); EU statute
  (P2B Art. 3; DSA Arts. 17, 20, 53–54); Seattle, where the company decides the worker's challenge.
- **The pattern.** In each setting the third party gave ground under outside force: litigation, scandal
  and regulators, statute, collective action (Nagaraj Rao et al., 2026: over 1,400 reactivations won
  before any tool). "Draconian" is the author's judgment and should be stated as his.

### 8. A test in an irreducible triad (about 8%)

- **Irreducibility** `[author]`: Φ gauges whether a given triad is irreducible; the paper cites the
  lab's results. *Open: whether this arm computes anything.*
- **The missing study.** Two people and a mediator with an objective; conditions that (a) disclose,
  (b) remove a task by rule, (c) return fast, plain results; a behavioural outcome. *Open: the author
  has not said whether the paper proposes this.*

### 9. Limits (about 4%)

- Sources read as snippets or not at all: Saenger and Parkes beyond the confirmed passages, Vatri,
  Gavrilov, Burnyeat, the histories of schooling.
- Working-paper versions for several experiments; small samples in the unspaced-script studies.
- The history guides the question and does not license a design.
- "Algorithmacy" has an earlier printed use in another sense (Oberschelp, 1995, p. 121).
