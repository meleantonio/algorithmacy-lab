## Judgement on the three priority questions

**1. Is the L1 sorting of remedies right? Yes for Table 2, with one text-level qualification and one overstated sentence.**
- Table 2 and the p. 457 text put mandated "stopping points" under Guide (product design) and both tax proposals (tax incentives for employer programmes; tax on the most addictive offerings) under Guide (price). Restrict is: bans on addictive or predatory experiences and on behavioural manipulation; the advertising limit for children; Wi-Fi or network blocks, willpower limits and device-to-device lock-outs; and "mandate minimum pricing" / ban of certain "freemium" models. Informing includes labels, disclosure of design mechanisms and business model, campaigns and addictiveness ratings. Education of frontline specialists, digital hygiene and mindfulness teaching sit under Guide, not Inform. L1's table and its correction of H (Q5 items 14-15, 31) are right on all of this.
- Qualification: on p. 461, in the place-and-distribution paragraph that opens "In terms of legal restrictions", the text adds "Similar efforts could force natural stopping points as well". So a place-based forced-stopping-point proposal is sorted under restriction in the prose, even though the product-design mandate is Guide in Table 2. L1 should say so (C5, C6).
- Overstated: "informing is 'one of the least controversial'" (the phrase is about mandatory labelling), "in every case the paper's verb is mandate ..." (several rows say only "could"), and "information and education are necessary but not sufficient" (the paper says it of information, p. 457, and of informational campaigns alone, p. 460; not of education) (C1-C3).

**2. Does Kamperman and Graff leave four of the five failures untouched? Yes.** A full-text search of the article finds no voting test, statute, treaty, deed, monopoly, licence, patent, slavery, colonial or residential schooling, immigration or exclusion. The overselling failure is the article's own argument (Myth #2, "Literacy is a certain or guaranteed path to economic security", p. 36). Three caveats keep the claim honest. (i) The five are the facet's own table, not the plan's five problems or the six facets A-F; the sixth, imposition, is touched only at the edge (p. 36, laptops and tablets "thrust" into the hands of marginalized teens), and the facet's table files that under withholding (C12). (ii) Ohio State's class-of-2029 "AI fluent" proclamation (p. 35) is a requirement on students, which the facet rightly does not call a gate. (iii) The article does treat print literacy as a technology (Table 1, Areas 1 to 3, p. 37), which the facet's "history invoked" list omitted (C13).

**3. Is the require/reward verdict right? Yes on the headline; no on "contradicted".** I searched the three Graff files for "requir", "rewar", "benefit" and "demand" and read every hit. None states a distinction between a society requiring literacy and literacy rewarding the literate, so "not stated in the parts supplied" is right. The nearest passages are: ch. 7 p. 292 ("the level of literacy demanded by society is also relative"; "the structures of demands, needs, and uses for literacy skills"); p. 271 (literacy "was not a requirement for adaptation or some success"); p. 302 (access to work "did not often demand much literacy", but at the artisanal level literacy "was more often needed", with "differential access to this occupational level and to its rewards"); p. 316 (education "a requirement for the maintenance and transmission of their class and status", of the middle class); p. 319 ("literacy could benefit workers, particularly some skilled and nonmanual employees"); p. 320 ("entry into skilled work was more difficult, and even some of the limited demands placed upon literacy skills could not be met by such disadvantaged individuals"); p. 321 ("potential literacy alone carried few concrete benefits"; "the higher uses of literacy and the corresponding benefits and status were often precluded"; "was not the benefit to individuals that it was promised to be"); 1979 introduction p. 16 ("Literacy requirements ... vary among different social and economic groups, regions, and communities") and p. 19 ("did carry certain benefits to those who possessed it"). Graff's pair is promised against delivered, and demanded against used, not require against reward. The facet's further claim that chapter 7 "points the other way" on "require" drops two limits: the p. 302 sentence is about unskilled and semiskilled workers only, and skilled work is described as requiring or favouring literacy (C9-C11).
