## Judgement on the Williams reading (user's request)

The facet says "as dangerous as it is futile" targets mindfulness among tech-industry employees and designers, not a consumer's own mindfulness. That is right, with one refinement.

What the passage says (pp. 101-102, in the order of the text):

1. "Above all, we must not put any stock whatsoever in the notion that advancing 'mindfulness' among employees in the technology industry is in any way relevant to or supportive of reforming the dynamics of the digital attention economy."
2. "The hope, if not the expectation, that technology design will suddenly come into alignment with human well-being if only enough CEOs and product managers and user experience researchers begin to conceive of it in Eastern religious terms is as dangerous as it is futile."

The grammatical subject of "is as dangerous as it is futile" is the hope in sentence 2 (design aligning with well-being if its makers adopt that framing). Sentence 1 names the notion (mindfulness among technology employees) as one to give no credit. Nothing in the chapter speaks of a consumer's or user's own mindfulness: the whole-book text contains "mindful" only here and in the index. So the facet's reading in Q5 point (1) is correct. Two qualifications. First, the facet's Contradiction 1 goes too far when it says Williams "does not discuss employee mindfulness programmes as such" (sentence 1 does) and should say only that "programs" is the draft's word and that the superlative attaches to the hope. Second, the user-side rejections on the same pages are real but are not about mindfulness: "unplug" or "detox" (p. 100), "just adapt" (p. 101), "education ... not sufficient" and "media literacy alone" (p. 101). The draft's "refuses the subject-side remedy by name" is therefore unsupported for mindfulness, as the facet says in Contradiction 2.

## Over-read and under-read in the two syntheses

1. Over-read, toward "the remedy is the user's". The synthesis's "prescription is mostly individual ... the remedy is the user's" (H1, Q1) rests on the typology. The paper's stated addressee is the manager (abstract; p. 1) and it never instructs consumers. What it does say is that against addictive platforms observational mindfulness "may be of little help", evaluative mindfulness "is needed" and wise mindfulness "is essential" (p. 6). The facet's headline ("at least three of the remedies are mandated by regulators") survives: labels, stopping points and taxes.

Wang, Berthon and Su (2025): the two "users acted upon" quotations are the authors reporting Leonard (2013) and O'Neil (2017), so they are weaker evidence of the authors' own view of users than the synthesis implies (C8). The p. 5192 sentences are future-research directions, as the facet says; I found nothing in the paper that proposes a regulation.

Under-read, to Berthon's favour: the paper's p. 6 "Now before we get all hot and bothered about nasty companies commandeering our minds" and the account of how free digital goods forced the advertising model are in the facet (H2), which is fair.
