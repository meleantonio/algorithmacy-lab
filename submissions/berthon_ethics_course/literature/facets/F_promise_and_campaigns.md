# F — What promoters promised literacy would do, what it did, and who was left out

*Facet F of the Berthon ethics course paper. 2026-10-13. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** (1) Harvey Graff's *The Literacy Myth* and his later restatements, their critics, and the distinction between a society requiring literacy and literacy rewarding the literate; (2) mass compulsory schooling and the purposes its promoters stated; (3) the international campaigns: UNESCO's Experimental World Literacy Programme (EWLP, 1967–1973), the UNESCO/UNDP 1976 critical assessment of it, the 1990 Jomtien and 2000 Dakar targets and whether they were met; (4) Freire's critique of "banking" education; (5) the current count of adults who cannot read, by sex and region, from the UNESCO Institute for Statistics (UIS); (6) the historical world literacy series (Our World in Data and its underlying sources) and the caveats on how literacy was measured.

**The question the synthesis answers.** What did promoters promise literacy would do, what did the record show it did, and which people were left out of the promise or paid for it? The facet records the history only. It draws no parallel to algorithmacy (Plan §8).

**graff2022resurgent** — Graff, H. J. (2022). The New Literacy Studies and the Resurgent Literacy Myth. *Literacy in Composition Studies*, 9(1), 47–53. https://doi.org/10.21623/1.9.1.4 (Crossref title matches.) PDF: https://licsjournal.org/index.php/LiCS/article/download/1393/1845
- *Read status:* **read in full** (7 pp.).
- *What it argues:* Graff restates the myth, credits Freire, Goodman and Kozol as foundations of the new literacy studies, and says the myth continues in the "effort to gain credibility by proclaiming anything and everything a 'literacy'", which is "often tied to commercialization" (p. 51). The last claim concerns the present and is left for facet Z.
- *Quotations (page numbers are the journal's):*
  - p. 48: "The “literacy myth” presumed the unique and innate power of “literacy by itself.”"
  - p. 48: "Individuals, age groups, gender groups, racial and ethnic groups, tribes, territories, regions, nations, and even continents were labeled essentially as superior or inferior." (Graff describing what the myth did to groups.)
  - p. 48: "As I summarized in 2010, “The Literacy Myth refers to the belief, articulated in educational, civic, religious, and other settings, contemporary and historical, that the acquisition of literacy is a necessary precursor to and inevitably results in economic development, democratic practice, cognitive enhancement, and upward social mobility” (“The Literacy Myth” 635)."
  - p. 48: "By reference to myth, I did not argue that these foundational assumptions were completely false. If that were the case, the “myth” would never have achieved its hegemony."
  - p. 48: "Literacy is never a “neutral skill”; it is always historically determined and value laden."
  - p. 47: Graff dates the myth: it "dated from antiquity but was articulated and promoted by the “invention” of alphabets, especially the Greek alphabet; the diffusion of the printing press and movable typography; progressive elements of the Renaissance and Enlightenment; nineteenth-century institutional school reforms; and twentieth-century presumptions of the essentialist demands of modern civilization."
  - p. 51: "Despite the development and institutionalization of the New Literacy Studies, the gross exaggeration of the power of literacy by itself continues, outside of any meaningful context including foundational reading, writing, and in some cases arithmetic."
- *Citation discrepancies inside the article:* its Works Cited gives Graff & Duffy as "pp. 2-11" and Black & Yasukawa as "pp. 1-21"; Crossref gives 31–42 and 213–228 (see the entries below). It names the 2010 paper "The Literacy Myth at 30"; Crossref's title is "The Literacy Myth at Thirty".
- *Bearing:* **P5 supports** (the myth's promise was "necessary precursor… inevitably results"). **P2:** Graff names the group-labelling ("superior or inferior") as a harm of the myth, which gives a contemporary-standard hook for deficit framing, but he writes in 2022. **P3:** he speaks of "continents", yet his own evidence is Ontario. **Complicates** any use of the myth against the paper's thesis: p. 48 says the assumptions were not "completely false".

**schudson1982literacy** — Schudson, M. (1982). Charting the "Rise of the West". *Journal of Economic History* 69(2), 409–445. https://doi.org/10.1017/s0022050709000837 (Crossref title matches). **Not accessible** (closed). OWID's metadata says its authors estimated literacy "using manuscript and book production as indirect indicators" for ten European countries to 1800, and that this approach has "clear limitations".
- *Bearing:* **complicates P3 and P5.** The historical "literacy rate" is a signature rate for a few European countries; reading ran ahead of writing, and women are undercounted. Mitch's Liverpool abstract is the one source here that tests the "required but not rewarded" pairing in a labour market, and it fits Graff's Ontario finding.

## (c) Synthesis

### Q1. Graff's myth and the distinction between requiring and rewarding

**What the sources establish.** Graff defines "the literacy myth" as the belief "that the acquisition of literacy is a necessary precursor to and inevitably results in economic development, democratic practice, cognitive enhancement, and upward social mobility" (graff2022resurgent, p. 48, quoting his 2010 paper). The evidence behind the 1979 book was mid-century Ontario census and tax records. Through Black and Yasukawa (ms pp. 3–4) Graff reports that literacy "proved of remarkably limited value in the pursuit of higher status or greater rewards", that many "illiterate" people held relatively skilled jobs, and that ethnicity, class, race, age and sex mattered more (1979, p. 198). Manufacturers believed the best educated workers were "more moral, loyal, cheerful, and contented" (1979, p. 203, as quoted), and Graff concludes that "Literacy was not primarily or by itself a vehicle for economic advancement, but rather a means of inculcating values and behaviours" (2011, p. 43, as quoted). Mitch's Liverpool abstract points the same way for a different city and period.

**The distinction the draft attributes to Graff.** `design_ethics_DRAFT.md:85` says "societies came to *require* literacy for civic, legal, and economic standing; nothing here claims literacy *rewarded* the literate, which is the narrower, defensible half of a distinction Graff's *Literacy Myth* (1991) insists on and does not contest." I did not find that distinction stated in these terms in anything I could open. What I found supports the second half (literacy did not reliably reward the literate; Graff 1979, p. 198, as quoted). For the first half, the only evidence in Graff's words as quoted is that schooling was required of the poor for discipline, not that civic, legal or economic standing was conditioned on literacy. Alexander (1980, p. 31) adds that Graff's data come from "a society which was already overwhelmingly literate by the 1860s", which limits what it can say about an earlier stage in which standing depended on literacy. **Not found: a passage in Graff that states the require/reward distinction.** The draft's attribution needs the 1991 introduction (pp. i–xliv) and chapter 7 read by a human with access (see section d).

**Who objected, and to what.** Schudson (1982, abstract-level) thinks Graff's target is a twentieth-century social-science version of the myth ("necessary and sufficient" for wealth and status) and not what Ryerson and Mann actually argued, which was moral. Alexander (1980) finds Graff confirms what others had found and cannot speak to whole economies. Greer (1979) and the Brookes review (unverified source) dispute method and data. Graff, speaking in 2017 about his 2010 paper, says the myth was "partly true" (interview, p. 54), and in 2022 says he "did not argue that these foundational assumptions were completely false" (p. 48). A source that confirms everything is suspect; this one does not.

### Q2. Mass schooling and its stated purposes

**What was said.** Mann in 1849 (pp. 14–15) gives three stated purposes of free schools: the continuance of republican government; wealth ("An educated people is always a more industrious and productive people"); and the moral argument against "vice and crime". Graff, via Black and Yasukawa, reads the Ontario case as discipline: "industry, thrift, order, and punctuality required for the successful operation of the factory" (ms p. 4, quoting Graff 2011, p. 43). **To whom, where, when:** Massachusetts and Upper Canada, 1840s–1870s. **Who objected at the time:** *not found* in the sources I could open. Mann himself (p. 14) says the republican argument alone would give a monarchist reason to oppose the schools, so he looked for a broader ground. That is an internal critique, not an objection from the people schooled. **What ended it:** not applicable; the question is what schooling promised.

### Q3. The campaigns: what was promised, what happened, who was left out

**Jomtien (1990).** What was done: the conference, sponsored by UNICEF, UNDP, UNESCO and the World Bank (ERIC record), adopted the Declaration and the Framework for Action, whose ¶8 proposed, among several dimensions that "countries may wish" to adopt, reducing the adult illiteracy rate "to, say, one-half its 1990 level by the year 2000". To whom: the "more than 960 million adults, two-thirds of whom are women" (Preamble). On what stated ground: education as a right and as a means to "survive, to develop their full capacities, to live and work in dignity" (Article 1, ¶1). **Was it met?** The Dakar text of 2000 says of the Jomtien vision in general that "reality has fallen far short of this vision" (Expanded Commentary ¶4); it does not say whether the adult-illiteracy dimension was met. By the UIS series (my arithmetic), the illiteracy rate went from 25.37% in 1990 to 18.88% in 2000, a fall of 25.6%, about half of the 50% the target named ("say, one-half"). The count of illiterate adults in 2000 (801.9 million in today's UIS series) is lower than the 880 million Dakar reported, which means the series has been revised since (see contradictions).

**Dakar (2000).** The target was "a 50 per cent improvement in levels of adult literacy by 2015, especially for women". The 2015 monitoring report's summary says illiteracy fell "from 18% in 2000 to an estimated 14% in 2015, which means the Dakar target of halving illiteracy was not achieved"; "Only 17 out of the 73 countries" with rates below 95% in 2000 halved theirs; adult literacy was "underfunded". Today's UIS series gives 14.04% for 2015, matching. The monitoring report's interpretation of "50 per cent improvement" as halving the illiteracy rate is its own; the Dakar text does not define the phrase in the paragraphs I read.

**The EWLP (1967–1973).** What was done: UNESCO and UNDP funded functional-literacy pilot projects in 11 countries to "test and demonstrate the economic and social returns of literacy" (UNDP/Unesco 1976, p. 9, as quoted by Lind & Johnston). To whom: selected groups of working adults. On what stated ground: human-capital theory (Lind & Johnston, p. 51). **What it delivered (all through Lind & Johnston unless stated):** the evaluation was criticised for unreliable data; the programme's own aim of preparing a world campaign was "certainly no nearer than when the programme began" (1976, p. 190); the average drop-out rate was about 50%; Lind and Johnston calculate about 12% of enrollees as success; many programmes were "functionalized in economic terms resulting in the virtual exclusion of female participation" (Gillette and Ryan 1983, p. 15). **Who objected at the time:** UNESCO's expert team in July 1975 said functionality must include "political, economic, social and cultural" dimensions; the Persepolis symposium of September 1975 declared that "Literacy is a political act"; Freire's movement had been criticising "banking" practice since 1970. **What ended it:** Lind and Johnston date the programme 1967 to 1972 (p. 51), the brief gives 1967–1973 and Jones's title says 1966–74; I found no source that settles the end date. Its critics reframed the concept, and functional literacy itself did not end (Lind & Johnston, p. 55). **Not found:** the 1976 report's own text, and any primary evidence of what the learners themselves said.

**Who was left out.** Women: Jomtien (¶15) and Dakar (¶37) both say two-thirds of illiterate adults are women; UIS shows 63% in 2024; the EWLP is said to have excluded them. The poor and rural: Dakar (¶37) says adult education sits "at the periphery of national education systems and budgets"; the 2015 summary says adult literacy was underfunded. Regions: 77% of illiterate adults in 2024 are in Central and Southern Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa (my arithmetic from UIS). Freire's p. 74 passage adds a different left-out claim: people treated as "marginals" to be "integrated" were already "inside" the structure that made them "beings for others".

### Q4. Freire's critique

**What was said and by whom.** Freire (1970, p. 72) describes teaching as depositing: "the scope of action allowed to the students extends only as far as receiving, filing, and storing the deposits." **Stated standard:** that people "cannot be truly human" apart from inquiry (p. 72). **What the sources do not show:** that any campaign was run on the banking model. Lind and Johnston (p. 22) say the EWLP's own critics and the Freire movement shaped the Persepolis turn, but they do not say EWLP classes were banking pedagogy; L&J (p. 54) say the 1976 assessment criticises the experts for trying to market a pre-packaged product, but the scan is poor and I did not quote it.

### Q5. The current count

739,139,712 adults aged 15 and over could not read and write in 2024; 466,257,152 (63%) were women (UIS, February 2026 release; SDG World aggregate; definition: ability to read and write a short simple statement on everyday life, from censuses and household surveys, often self-reported). Regional breakdown above. Rural/urban and wealth-quintile splits exist for some countries; I found none for the world aggregate for 2020–2024.

### Q6. The long series and its caveats

The only world-scale figure for 1820 is OWID's 12% literate (12.05 in the CSV), from the OECD's *How Was Life?* (not opened). Signature-based and book-based estimates cover a handful of European countries; UNESCO's first global estimate is 1957 (about 700 million illiterate adults around 1950, UNESCO 1970, Table 1). The signature method understates reading, undercounts women and, as Nilsson's Swedish case shows, can differ from other methods by a factor of ten for the same place and years.

### Claims the sources contradict

1. **"880 million illiterate adults" (Dakar, 2000) against the UIS series.** Dakar ¶5 and ¶37 say 880 million. Today's UIS series puts the 2000 figure at 801,852,096. The 2015 monitoring summary says 781 million; the UIS series for 2015 is 755,758,528. The Jomtien figure of "more than 960 million" for 1990 has no counterpart because the UIS count series begins in 1993 (947,961,216). The population base and method were revised; I did not find the revision note. A draft must not set the official reports' counts beside current UIS counts as if they measured the same thing.
2. **Our World in Data against UNESCO's own mid-century estimate.** OWID's world literacy is 35.96% for 1950 and 41.62% for 1960; UNESCO (1970, Table 1) implies 55.7% and 60.7% for adults 15+. The 20-point gap is unexplained here.
3. **The draft's attribution of the require/reward distinction to Graff (1991).** I found Graff's words (as quoted) on the second half only; the first half is not in anything I could open.
4. **"Graff showed literacy was a myth" against Graff's own statements.** He says the assumptions were not "completely false" (2022, p. 48) and the myth was "partly true" (2017, p. 54). Schudson and Alexander contend further that he refuted a narrower claim than the one the nineteenth-century promoters made.
5. **EWLP dates.** Lind and Johnston: 1967 to 1972; the brief: 1967–1973; Jones (2018) title: 1966–74; the UNESCO 1970 survey: the General Conference authorised it in 1964. These are different starting points (authorisation, first projects, evaluation) and I did not reconcile them.
6. **"The EWLP failed" against what the assessment's reported results show.** Lind and Johnston call it "unsuccessful" (p. 55) but note the Tanzanian project produced most of the literates and was not discussed in the assessment (p. 53). The 1971 panel promised a judgment "on the value of functional literacy" by 1974; the 1976 assessment judged the evaluation, so whether functional literacy failed or was never properly tested is open.
7. **Reported citation details.** Graff (2022) gives Graff & Duffy as pp. 2–11 and Black & Yasukawa as pp. 1–21; Crossref gives 31–42 and 213–228. Graff & Duffy is dated 2017 (pp. 31–42) and 2014 (online, pp. 1–12) in two Springer records; the encyclopedia record (pp. 457–468) has no issued date in Crossref (deposited 24 January 2008).
8. **The 1996–1997 step in the UIS series.** A 72.7-million drop in one year (all UIS_EST) is an artefact until shown otherwise; the full-range claim "the count fell 22% since 1993" depends on it.
