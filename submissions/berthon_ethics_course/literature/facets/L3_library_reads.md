### 1. Moody-Adams (1994), `moodyadams1994culture`

Moody-Adams, Michele M. (1994). Culture, Responsibility, and Affected Ignorance. *Ethics* 104(2): 291–309. DOI 10.1086/293601. JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/2381578.
*Verified:* Crossref title, volume, issue and pages match. *Read:* **read in full** (the 19 printed pages). The text layer was checked against rendered images of pp. 296 and 302. *Scale:* philosophical; examples from ancient Greece, Nazi Germany, American and English history, and psychology.

**The argument, in her order.**

*The target (pp. 291–293).* She names two groups of views and rejects both: "Against both kinds of views, I contend that the link between culture and agency does not undermine the standard attributions of responsibility for action and hence cannot exempt human beings from responsibility." (p. 292.) The first group holds that a whole era's cultural limits can make widespread moral ignorance blameless. She calls the core claim "the inability thesis": "sometimes one's upbringing in a culture simply renders one unable to know that certain actions are wrong." (p. 293.) Her three named holders are Slote (ancient Greek slave owners were "unable to see what virtue required in regard to slavery", p. 292), Donagan (a Sandhurst or West Point graduate is culpable for ignorance of duty to noncombatants, "an officer bred up from childhood in the Hitler Jugend might not be", p. 293) and Wolf (the "social circumstances" of "slaveowners of the 1850's, Nazis of the 1930's, and many male chauvinists of our fathers' generation" may have made their values inevitable, p. 293). The second group (Section III) holds that culture can impair capacity, as in "cultural insanity".

*The empirical case against the inability thesis (Section I, pp. 293–298).* Her first charge is a method charge: the theories "base hypotheses about what some agent(s) could not do solely on evidence of what the agent(s) did not do." (p. 294.) Deciding between culturally induced blindness and "an unwillingness to consider that some practice might be wrong" is "hardly a simple empirical matter" (p. 294); the data underdetermine the hypotheses, as in debates on psychological egoism. Her positive claim rests on Hart's "internal perspective": where members of a group show one, "it will be difficult, if not impossible, to make a rationally compelling case for the inability thesis", and "a thorough account of each case offered as an instance of the inability thesis would reveal the existence of such a perspective." (p. 295.)

*Her one worked case is ancient Greek slavery (pp. 295–296).* She lists the evidence: a "complex legal structure regulated and preserved the institution of slavery" (p. 295); the law, religion, popular morality and even comedy supported it (pp. 295–296); Aristotle "takes on some unnamed opponents of slavery who denied that slavery is natural", though "there is no obvious evidence of who these opponents were, and it is difficult to find much antislavery material in the surviving literature of the period. But, as Finley reminds us, the literature that does survive is principally the product of those who had reason to support the institution." (p. 296.) She adds that even the poorest Athenian citizen might have seen himself as part of an elite (p. 296). Her verdict: "The belief that slavery was justified was insufficiently examined by those who held it. But there is no convincing evidence that the blame for this should be traced to anything other than the affected ignorance, in Aquinas's phrase, of those who wanted to perpetuate the culture of slavery." (p. 296.)

*What affected ignorance is.* "Affected ignorance—choosing not to know what one can and should know—is a complex phenomenon, but sometimes it simply involves refusing to consider whether some practice in which one participates might be wrong." (p. 296.) Later: "Affected ignorance is essentially a matter of choosing not to be informed of what we can and should know." (p. 301.) She distinguishes four varieties (p. 301): torturers' euphemisms; the executive who insists on knowing nothing of the means; the person who will "ask no questions" despite evidence of wrongdoing; and the refusal to admit one's own fallibility, "perhaps the most common form". She places the Greek and Wolf-type cases in the fourth: "much of the moral ignorance at issue in the examples discussed by Wolf, Donagan, and Slote can be understood in terms of this variety of affected ignorance." (p. 302.)

*A capacity argument that needs no evidence of objection.* In a footnote on Barnes's version of the thesis she writes that "there is simply no evidence to suggest that the Greeks 'could not' (or even 'did not') imagine a world in which there were no slaves", and that "merely in virtue of learning a language, every human being has the capacity to imagine (to conceive) that her social world might be organized on quite different principles"; so "one has the capacity to question existing social practices merely by virtue of learning to form the negation of any statement." (p. 296, n. 14.) She says "I cannot argue this case here."

*The "standards of their own day" objection (p. 298).* She states it fairly: "it is a commonplace of everyday moral reflection that it is 'only fair' to judge people by 'the standards of their own day'", because a future society may condemn what we now approve. Her answer: "this commonsense view simply presumes without argument that our own moral ignorance is best attributed to 'cultural limitations' rather than to our failings as human beings who perpetuate cultures." (p. 298.)

*Section II: the banality of wrongdoing (pp. 298–303).* Arendt on Eichmann; Milgram's subjects, some of whom averted their eyes and one of whom said "I refuse to take responsibility" (pp. 300–301), read as affected ignorance, not inability; torture regimes. She asks why the resistance to this view is so strong, and gives two sources: sympathetic identification with historical agents, "particularly in history" (p. 302), and the belief that there are only two responses to wrongdoing, a moralistic model without forgiveness and a therapeutic model without blame (pp. 302–303). She proposes a third, the "forgiving moralist's" model, "both consistent with respect for the worth of persons as agents and compatible with facts about how hard it is to merit moral esteem", and adds: "the most estimable of human qualities will sometimes be revealed in the effort to forgive the wrongdoing of our cultural predecessors, rather than simply to ignore the ways in which their practice amounted to wrongdoing." (p. 303.)

*Section III: culture is not an agent (pp. 303–309).* "A culture—independent of agents who perpetuate culture—cannot be an 'agent' of anything." (p. 304.) "It is unhelpful in such cases to blame the culture, or 'cultural stresses,' or even a clash of cultures, when the individual who chooses how to perpetuate the culture is to blame." (p. 305.) Cultures are modified in each individual's application of their rules (pp. 306–307). One concession: an agent who "(for the first time) confronts a radically different cultural practice against her will, or before she is fully capable of choice, will have a mitigating—sometimes even an exculpatory—excuse" (p. 308). Her closing claim: "only a misguided cultural relativism could support the view that moral criticism of another culture is never justified." (p. 308.)

**Her examples, as she uses them.** Ancient Greek slavery (Slote; Barnes; Finley; Dover), pp. 292, 295–296 and n. 14. Hitler Jugend officer (Donagan), p. 293. "slaveowners of the 1850's" (in Wolf's phrase), p. 293. Huckleberry Finn and Jim, p. 296 n. 16. Eichmann, p. 298; Milgram, pp. 299–301; torture, pp. 299, 301; an investment-bank executive; a mother and a son's drug money; a university administrator and sexual harassment; a bigot (all p. 301–302). Victorian male chauvinists, p. 302. Jefferson on slavery, p. 302 n. 35. Hume's racial prejudice, p. 303 n. 37. *People v. Poddar*, pp. 303–304; Walker on "ghetto" psychology, pp. 304–306; *Chase v. United States* (draft-record protesters), p. 304. The American South appears only through Wolf's phrase, Huckleberry Finn and Jefferson; she gives it no extended treatment. She does not discuss literacy, writing, records, schooling, or any institution that made a competence a condition of standing.

**Does she hold that a practice can be condemned, and its practitioners blamed, where no contemporary objected?** Yes, in two ways, and she limits both. (1) On the evidence: she accepts that surviving antislavery material is scant and the opponents unnamed, and holds that the Greeks "might be to blame, in some degree" (p. 302, in a rhetorical question) and that "there is no convincing evidence that the blame for this should be traced to anything other than the affected ignorance" of those who wanted to perpetuate slavery (p. 296), because the surviving literature comes from those with reason to support the institution (p. 296) and because absence of objection does not show inability (p. 294). (2) In principle: the negation argument (p. 296, n. 14) says no objector need exist for the criticism to have been available. Her limits: blame comes "in some degree" and "with varying degrees of culpability" (p. 302); she says the question "is hardly a simple empirical matter" (p. 294) and that "no empirical considerations alone can decisively resolve the debate" (p. 296); and forgiveness is part of the model (p. 303). The condemned thing is "their practice" and the blamed thing is persons who chose to perpetuate it; she does not separate the two the way Levy does (X, entry 14).

**Bearing on the five problems.** Problem 2 (presentism): complicates and supports. She answers the "own day" defence directly (p. 298) and so removes the main reason to confine the charge to internal standards. She also places the burden on the paper to name persons who chose, not a "community" (pp. 304–305). Problem 3 (scale): cuts against "global community" as a subject of blame (p. 304). Problems 1, 4, 5: not addressed.

### 2. Hunnisett (1981) on Clanchy, `hunnisett1981clanchy`

Hunnisett, R. F. (1981). Review of M. T. Clanchy, *From Memory to Written Record: England, 1066–1307* (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1979, xiii, 330 pp.). *American Journal of Legal History* 25(1): 67–68. DOI 10.2307/844998. JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/844998.
*Verified:* Crossref title, volume, issue, pages match. *Read:* **review read in full; the book itself not opened.** Reviewer: Keeper in Charge, Public Record Office. The review is of the 1979 first edition, so its page references are not the third-edition pages of facets A and G.

**Reports of Clanchy.**
- The move from memory to record: "In 1066 nearly all legal and administrative business in England was transacted orally and often impressed on the memory of witnesses by symbolic actions. By 1307 such business, although frequently retaining an element of symbolism, was invariably committed to writing. This book surveys that development and argues that its main result was the gradual spread of literacy from a few monasteries to all ranks of society." (p. 67.)
- The mechanism: "the enormous proliferation of documents issuing from the royal administration in the thirteenth century", illustrated by "the increasing amounts of wax used for sealing, as recorded on the liberate rolls", "made it ever more necessary for the king's subjects to have at least a nodding acquaintance with letters, not excluding peasant smallholders who by 1300 were making grants by charter in large numbers." (p. 67.)
- Reading and writing: "in the middle ages 'reading' normally meant 'hearing' and 'writing' meant 'dictating,' there being a clear distinction between authors and scribes", so Clanchy "allows that it was therefore not essential for kings or for such officials as sheriffs to be literate in the modern sense (p. 219)". Elsewhere (pp. 160–1, 261) "he argues that foremen of juries and knights who collected testimony for the county court would have been able not only to speak English and French but also to read English, French and Latin". (p. 67.)
- Records: Hubert Walter is the book's "hero", "the principal progenitor of the English public records" (p. 68). Hunnisett adds in his own voice, not as claims he attributes to Clanchy: "It can be no coincidence that it was during his justiciarship (1193-8) that feet of fines and plea rolls were written and preserved"; "An early recognition of the value of the archival work of Hubert Walter was the establishment of 1189 as the legal limit of memory throughout the later middle ages"; and, as "another piece of evidence that the years around 1200 were a real watershed", that "it was in John's reign that the 'record' sent up from an inferior court came to be written rather than transmitted orally" (p. 68).
- Trust, forgery, seals: the book discusses "the use of seals in lieu of signatures", "the slow and halting adoption of the practice of dating documents", and "how the change from memory to written record, and the quo warranto proceedings, made inevitable the large-scale forgery of documents, such forgeries having a greater chance of acceptance than had genuine Anglo-Saxon charters" (p. 68).
- Who gained or lost: the reviewer reports growth in the use of documents by the king's subjects, and the central records as "of less use to the royal administration than to those individuals and institutions who had the originals (or knew their dates or precise references to their enrollment) or than they now are to historians" (p. 68). He reports no person or class that lost standing for being unable to read, and no objector.
- Scribes and clerks: the author/scribe distinction above (p. 67). Nothing on scribes as third parties, their reputation or abuses.

**What Hunnisett criticises.** "The extent and, even more, the depth of lay literacy in 1300 must remain conjectural, and Dr. Clanchy is not completely consistent about this." (p. 67.) The claim that jury foremen and knights read in the modern sense: "Many would regard that claim as excessive." (p. 67.) Clanchy "exaggerates the occasional failure of the administration to find an enrolled document" (p. 68). On seals: "it is untrue that a legend bearing the sealer's name was an essential element of medieval seals (pp. 244-5)" (p. 68). Style "rather prolix and repetitious"; "a score or so of grammatical and printing errors" (p. 68). His overall verdict is strongly favourable: "an outstandingly good book" (p. 67), "likely to become a standard work" (p. 68).

*Scale:* one kingdom, 1066–1307. *Bearing:* Problem 1: complicates. The reviewer reports growth of documents and of literacy and a hedged claim that a "nodding acquaintance with letters" became necessary; he reports no victim class. Problem 2: nothing. Problem 3: national.

### 3. Robertson (1980) on Clanchy, `robertson1980clanchy`

Robertson, D. W., Jr. (1980). Review of Clanchy, *From Memory to Written Record* (and of Richter, *Sprache und Gesellschaft im Mittelalter*). *American Historical Review* 85(3): 612–613. DOI 10.2307/1854942. JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/1854942.

**Reports of Clanchy.**

**Reports of Stock.**

Customary rights were now written down, encouraging forgery not merely as a redaction of the known facts but as their creative manipulation. ### 5. Andrien (2012) on Burns, `andrien2012burns`

Andrien, Kenneth J. (2012). Review of Kathryn Burns, *Into the Archive: Writing and Power in Colonial Peru* (Durham: Duke University Press, 2010, 247 pp.). *Journal of Interdisciplinary History* 43(1) (Summer 2012): 144–145. DOI not found. JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/41487910.

**Reports of Burns.**
- Subject: the book "attempts to 'historicize' the colonial archives of Cuzco in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries by examining the role of notaries as legal intermediaries who translated the needs and desires of citizens into official notarized records (instrumentos)." The records were "used to transact a sale, make a loan, write a dowry, or to give testimony in a lawsuit". (p. 144.)
- Burns's argument: "notary offices were actually sites of negotiation, where the greed and trickery of both notaries and their clients helped to shape the production of notary account books (protocolos)." Andrien finds Burns "thoroughly convincing in debunking the 'transparency' of these apparently mundane documents". (p. 144.)
- Reputation and safeguards: "Given their powerful societal role as 'ventriloquists,' who prepared the documents that gave voice to people's legal needs in everyday life, notaries often enjoyed a reputation in Spain and the Indies as rogues and scoundrels (2). As a result, the Crown attempted to regulate their activities by providing approved documentary forms and procedures. Legal self-help manuals emerged by the mid-sixteenth century to give notaries guidance about performing their functions properly." (p. 144.) The "(2)" is Burns's page.
- Abuse: notaries prospered "by investing in land, commerce, mining, and other (often illegal) economic enterprises" (p. 144); the Crown "sold the offices and allowed the holders to turn them over to family members or close associates after paying a fee to the treasury" (p. 145). "Notaries used a variety of clever legal strategies to circumvent the intent of the law, such as fake contracts (confianzas) and documents that protested other legally notarized statements (exclamaciones). In short, notaries could manipulate the legal record in the service of a powerful client or on their own account to shelter assets, disguise debts, sell merchandise, control land, or engage in a host of other shady ventures." (p. 145.) "To serve their clients and themselves, notaries sometimes manipulated the very documentary record that they were sworn to uphold." (p. 145.)
- Indigenous notaries: Burns "points out gently that modern historians must not assume that 'indigenous notaries spoke for their communities, faithfully representing their language and desires' (145)" (p. 145; the inner quotation is Burns's, from her p. 145).
- Illiterate or non-reading clients, petitions by them, a victim of a falsified instrument: **not found.** The abuses reported run through "a powerful client" or the notary's own interest; the review does not say who was harmed.

**What Andrien criticises.** Burns "is less successful in presenting the changing historical context in which these documents were produced" (p. 144), and "does not explain effectively how two centuries of changing political, social, and economic conditions in Cuzco might have altered or influenced the work of notaries" (p. 145). His verdict: "her fine book should appeal to a wide audience" (p. 145).

*Scale:* one city, two centuries. *Bearing:* Problem 2 (and facet G's check): supplies a documented divergent interest at Cuzco as reported by a reviewer; does not supply a harmed illiterate party.

## (c) Synthesis by question

### Question 1. Moody-Adams

**The argument.** Moody-Adams holds that a culture's influence on a person is no excuse that removes responsibility. She calls the opposing claim the inability thesis, and she makes three moves against it. (1) The evidence for it is only evidence of what people did not do, which cannot show what they could not do (p. 294). (2) Wherever members of a group share Hart's "internal perspective", the criticism of a practice was available to them, so that a full account of any case "would reveal the existence of such a perspective" (p. 295). (3) The usual cause of "moral ignorance" is affected ignorance, "choosing not to know what one can and should know" (p. 296). Her central claim, in her own words, is the sentence on p. 292: culture "cannot exempt human beings from responsibility". She holds blame comes in degrees and sits beside forgiveness (pp. 302–303).

**Her examples** are in entry 1 above: ancient Greek slavery is the only worked case; the American South appears through Wolf's "slaveowners of the 1850's" (p. 293), Huckleberry Finn (p. 296 n. 16) and Jefferson (p. 302 n. 35).

**Can a practice be condemned and practitioners blamed where no contemporary objected?** She says yes for the Greeks, on the grounds given in entry 1: the surviving record is the record of the supporters (p. 296), absence of objection is not inability (p. 294), and every speaker can form the negation of a statement (p. 296 n. 14). She hedges: "in some degree" (p. 302), "no empirical considerations alone can decisively resolve the debate" (p. 296).

**Agent's reading (the author has not seen this).** The three standards are: (i) a contemporary named the wrong; (ii) the actors broke their own professed standard; (iii) the outcome fell short of the promise. The author ruled out adding a present-day standard.

1. *Does she support the three?* In part. Standard (ii) fits her own frame, because her "internal perspective" includes "self-criticism of their own lapses in conformity to the rules" (p. 295), so a breach of a group's own standard is exactly where she locates available knowledge. Standard (i) fits as evidence, not as a requirement: a named contemporary would be evidence that the criticism was available, and she says that evidence of what agents did not do cannot show what they could not do (p. 294). Standard (iii), outcome against promise, is not in her article.
2. *Does she supply a fourth?* She supplies a test of blame, not a test of failure. Affected ignorance (could have known; had an interest in not asking; did not examine) says when persons are blameworthy for a practice already judged wrong. She does not say how to judge a practice wrong; she takes slavery as wrong without argument. So she supplies no fourth standard of "failure", and a paper that wants to blame persons for a failure needs her test or an equivalent as an added condition.
3. *Does she cut against the ruling?* The ruling stands, and her article removes its strongest justification. The ruling avoids a present-day standard so that the paper escapes the charge of presentism. Her reply to that charge (p. 298: the "own day" defence "simply presumes without argument that our own moral ignorance is best attributed to 'cultural limitations'") and her closing claim (p. 308: only a "misguided cultural relativism" holds moral criticism of another culture unjustified) mean a present-day standard would not be unfair on her view. The author could therefore keep the three standards as a choice of economy, and the paper should not say that presentism forbids more.
4. *Where she cuts against the plan.* She denies that a culture can be an agent (p. 304) and says the person who chooses to perpetuate the practice is to blame (p. 305). "As a global community" as the subject of "failed" sits badly with that. Her model also differs from Levy's split in X entry 14 and `T_adjudication.md` §5: Levy condemns the act and declines to blame the person (p. 19 of Levy); Moody-Adams condemns the practice, blames persons in some degree, and forgives (p. 303). X and T attribute the practice/person split to Levy, correctly, and should not carry it into her.

**Record over voice, with few contemporary objectors.** The case rests mostly on later courts (*Marshall*, ¶14, ¶40; *Delgamuukw*). What her argument offers:
- It removes one objection. "No contemporary objected" does not show the officials and courts of 1760 or 1840 could not have seen the wrong (p. 294), and the surviving record favours those who supported the practice (p. 296). The paper may say the thin record of objection does not clear the actors.
- It sets what the paper must still show. Her case for the Greeks rests on an internal perspective (a legal structure, supporting religion and custom, p. 295), an interest ("an institution that benefited nonslaves in various ways", p. 296) and a refusal to examine. For record over voice the matching facts would be: the officials who made the written text were the ones who gained by it, they knew the other party relied on an oral account, and nothing shows they asked. The Mi'kmaq treaty and Waitangi material may or may not carry these; I have not checked them against this test.
- It does not supply a standard. She says the decision "is hardly a simple empirical matter" (p. 294). The paper can claim "plausibly affected ignorance", not proof.
- Her argument concerns persons who perpetuate a practice. For this row the paper has to name the actors (a Crown officer, a court, a company), not "the community".
