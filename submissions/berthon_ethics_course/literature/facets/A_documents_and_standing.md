# A — Documents and standing

*Facet A of the Berthon ethics course paper. Agent-drafted on 2026-10-13. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** The medieval and early-modern stretch of the literacy transition, plus its colonial and present-day legal afterlife: when written records (charters, writs, deeds, treaties, statutes) replaced memory and oral testimony as proof, who lost legal or civic standing, and who said so at the time. The leads were Clanchy on England 1066–1307, Stock, benefit of clergy, charters against customary tenure, and colonial treaties and deeds signed by people who could not read them (the Treaty of Waitangi's two texts, North American deeds). **The question the synthesis answers:** which losses of standing can be documented with a date, a place, an identifiable group and a stated ground, and which of the leads turn out to be a different kind of event (a privilege that widened, a dispute about meaning, a fraud allegation that the sources dispute).

**Legend for read status.** *Read in full* = I read the whole text this session. *Read in part* = I obtained text and read the passages cited (pages or paragraphs given), not the whole work. *Abstract only* = a database abstract or opening-page text only; I paraphrase and do not quote beyond it. *Not accessible* = no text and no abstract. Two notes on locators. (1) Clanchy is read through Google Books search-inside snippets of about 40 words; the locator is Google's page id (for example PP18), and printed page numbers are mostly not shown, so every Clanchy quotation needs its surrounding paragraph and printed page re-checked in the book. I normalised the OCR spacing around punctuation and nothing else. (2) The Supreme Court of Canada judgments give paragraph numbers; the Waitangi Tribunal report gives the printed page (I derived printed page = PDF page − 26 in the body and checked it against the page number printed at the foot of PDF pages 22 and 411).

### Group 1. Memory to written record in England

**clanchy2013memory** — Clanchy, M. T. (2013). *From Memory to Written Record: England 1066–1307* (3rd ed.). Wiley-Blackwell. ISBN 9781405157919. (1st ed. Edward Arnold 1979; 2nd ed. Blackwell 1993.)

- *What it argues:* England moved, across 1066–1307, from trusting persons and memory to trusting documents; writing spread down the social scale; and writing was distrusted for good reason.

PhD thesis, University of Liverpool. https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/id/eprint/3174128
- *Verification:* repository record and the downloaded PDF, whose title page matches. No DOI.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (abstract, pp. 1–2, chapter 1 pp. 18–23; the other chapters not read).
- *What it argues:* The peasantry, "although largely illiterate, developed a 'document consciousness'" is widely accepted (abstract); her Chapter 3 finds "no significant shift towards the use of written evidence over the course of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries" in litigation at Wakefield and Alrewas (abstract). - *Quotations and facts (thesis pagination):*
  - p. 2, summarising Clanchy: "literate modes were familiar even to serfs" is Harrison's quotation of Clanchy; she gives the Clanchy source as p. 2 of the 2nd edition (note 4). The Peterborough *Carte Nativorum* is "a section of a cartulary of Peterborough Abbey consisting of copies of charters confiscated from villein tenants" (p. 2). Harrison quotes Clanchy's estimate (2nd ed., p. 50) that "the number of peasants' charters produced amounts to hundreds of thousands or even millions", and notes that "No other historian has made an estimate for the level of the use of charters by the peasantry" (n. 7).
  - p. 20, on the 1381 rising at St Albans, translating Walsingham's *Gesta Abbatum*: the tenants of Barnet "demanded with great impudence a certain book drawn up from the court rolls, in order to burn it"; the reason Walsingham alleged was that "these deceitful and false men had made free charters, one to another" during the Black Death, while the book was proof that "almost all the houses in Barnet were in fact held 'by the rolls'".
  - p. 21: Abbot Roger's ordinances of 1275 ruled that "no free man should enter and hold the land of our villeins", and that no villein might lease or sell land or rents to anyone except the abbey. "From the earliest presentment for the illegal use of a charter in 1296, there is a steady trickle of these cases", with clusters in 1311–14 and 1344–45. "From around 1323, individual tenants were regularly called upon to show by what means they claimed to hold their land (*ad ostendere qualiter tenere clamat*). The right to demand proof applied to both free and unfree ... In the majority of such cases, the result of the inquiry was the seizure of the tenement and the surrender of the charter. The land was typically re-granted to the same tenant, but by admittance in the full court ... to be held *in villenagio* for services."
  - pp. 21–22: in 1344 the abbey brought William Atte Penne before the assize "accused of forging charters in the hope of holding his customary land freely"; Harrison writes that "it was alleged that William had attempted to age his charters by smoking them over his hearth", and that the abbey feared that if the court found for him other tenants might try the same, "and thus by chance all the land which had been customary might become free land" (Harrison's translations of the *Gesta*).
  - p. 22: in the court of June 1345 eleven tenants were presented for transferring villein land by charter; each surrendered the charter and was re-admitted "by the rod at the will of the lord", in the example for a fine of 12d.
- *Scale:* a manor (Barnet, St Albans Abbey), with comparison to Peterborough Abbey; the court-roll chapters cover two manors.
- *Bearing:* **Problem 1** — supports, for one place: a group (customary tenants of Barnet), a date (1296–1345, then 1381), a loss (charters seized, tenure re-fixed as villein), a lord-side stated ground (the abbey's right, and the fear that customary land would become free). **Problem 2** — complicates: Harrison reports the dispute as tenure control and does not call it a wrong; the tenants' own stated ground survives only in Walsingham's hostile account. **Problem 3** — one manor. **Problem 5** — complicates Clanchy: use of writing by peasants was real, but lords answered it by demanding or confiscating writing, and Chapter 3 finds no general shift to written proof in the manor courts it studied.

### Group 2. Benefit of clergy

**blackstonendcommentaries** — Blackstone, W. (18th century; year not found on the page). *Commentaries on the Laws of England*, Book IV, ch. 28, "Of the Benefit of Clergy." https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/blackstone_bk4ch28.asp
- *Verification:* the Avalon Project page carries the chapter title and Blackstone's text. The page I read gives no publication year (the URL path places it in the 18th century); I do not state one.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (the passages on the reading test, 4 Hen. VII c. 13 and 5 Ann. c. 6). Blackstone is an 18th-century legal treatise, so he is a contemporary of the 1706 repeal, not of the medieval origin. Quotations normalise the long s. The Avalon transcription prints "cloud" for "could" in the sentence on women, peers and commoners; I corrected that one word in the quotation below.
- *Quotations (print pagination not shown; locator = Book IV, chapter 28):*
  - "ORIGINALLY the law was held, that no man should be admitted to the privilege of clergy, but such as had the habitum et tonsuram clericalem. But in process of time a much wider and more comprehensive criterion was established: every one that could read (a mark of great learning in those days of ignorance and her sister superstition) being accounted a clerk or clericus, and allowed the benefit of clerkship, though neither initiated in holy orders, nor trimmed with the clerical tonsure."
  - "And those men, who could not read, if under the degree of peerage, were hanged." (the sentence follows a list: "All women, all peers, and all commoners who could read were therefore discharged in such felonies ... for the first offence, upon burning in the hand, if lay").
  - "AFTERWARDS indeed it was considered, that education and learning were no extenuations of guilt, but quite the reverse: and that, if the punishment of death for simple felony was too severe for those who had been liberally instructed, it was, a fortiori, too severe for the ignorant also. And thereupon by statute 5 Ann. c. 6. it was enacted, that the benefit of clergy should be granted to all those who were intitled to ask it, without requiring them to read by way of conditional merit."
  - On statute 4 Hen. VII. c. 13 (the year is not in his text): laymen admitted to the privilege "shall be burnt with a hot iron in the brawn of the left thump" (sic: the transcription prints "thump" for "thumb").
- *Scale:* a nation (England), with the criminal law as a whole.
- *Bearing:* **Problem 1** — supports with a qualification. Before the repeal of the reading condition, a convicted commoner who could not read was hanged while a commoner who could read was branded and discharged: standing before the court turned on literacy, and the person named is "the ignorant". The qualification is that benefit of clergy was a mercy that literacy unlocked, not a penalty on the illiterate. **Problem 2** — the repeal's stated ground is on record in Blackstone's words (the ignorant should not die for what the learned survive), and the ground is a standard of proportion. Who moved the Bill of 1706 or objected earlier: not found. **Problem 5** — the 18th-century legislature removed the literacy test, which shows a gate taken down by statute.
- *Presentism flag:* the year of 5 Ann. c. 6 is not in Blackstone's text. Web summaries give 1706 and 1827 for the end of the reading test and the privilege; I did not open a source for either date and do not rely on them.

Treaties resulted in misunderstandings and deliberate deceit in the translation and recording of meaning, and they privileged a signature any signature on a document over the recognized verbal authority of legitimate leaders." (The Consensus text prints "a signature any signature" with the dashes lost.) The same page asserts that allotment accepted "a signature or its substitute ... in the place of true comprehension".
- *Bearing:* **Problem 1** — an assertion, not a documented case: no treaty, date, number or archival source appears in the part I read. Treat it as a claim to test, not as evidence. It does show that a scholar named the signature-for-comprehension issue as a wrong in 1992.

**goodman2009american** and **bottino2024turff** — both **abstract only.** Goodman argues that to understand 17th-century New England land transfers "we must look beyond the existing archive of deeds, charters, and patents" to the Algonkian spoken language recorded by Williams and Eliot. Bottino says that "As most of Maine's English colonists were not fully literate, legal instruments such as land deeds existed simultaneously as oral, material, and textual representations of the authority of the law" and rested on ritual. Together they complicate the assumption that only the Native party worked from speech (the colonists' own land claims there drew on oral ritual, per Bottino's abstract).

### Group 4. Aotearoa New Zealand: the two texts

**waitangi2014declaration** — Waitangi Tribunal (2014). *He Whakaputanga me te Tiriti / The Declaration and the Treaty: The Report on Stage 1 of the Te Paparahi o Te Raki Inquiry* (Wai 1040). Legislation Direct, ISBN 978-1-986956-308-0. https://forms.justice.govt.nz/search/Documents/WT/wt_DOC_85648980/Te%20Raki%20W.pdf
- *Verification:* the official PDF linked from the Tribunal's inquiry page; its metadata title matches and its cataloguing data give the ISBN.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (chairperson's letter pp. xxii–xxiii; section 5.5 on literacy pp. 257–259; section 7.6.2 pp. 357–358; section 7.10 p. 386; the other 560 pages not read). Official report of a statutory tribunal, so a primary source for what the Tribunal found; its evidence is secondary.
- *What it argues:* The Tribunal concluded that "in February 1840 the rangatira who signed te Tiriti did not cede their sovereignty. That is, they did not cede their authority to make and enforce law over their people or their territories. Rather, they agreed to share power and authority with the Governor." (p. xxii.) On the cause: "Though Britain intended to obtain the sole right to make and enforce law over Māori as well as Pākehā, Hobson did not explain this." (p. xxii.) The agreement lay in "what signatory rangatira ... were prepared to assent to, based on the proposals that William Hobson and his agents made to them by reading te Tiriti and explaining the proposed agreement verbally" (p. xxii).
- *Numbers and place:* "In all, over a period of some six months, nine copies of the treaty ... were signed at about 50 meetings around the coast of both islands by more than 500 rangatira. Only 39 rangatira signed the English text (at Waikato Heads and Manukau Harbour), it being the text offered for signature." (p. 386). The signings in the report's own first count were at Waitangi, Waimate and Mangungu in February 1840 (p. xxii).
- *Literacy evidence (section 5.5):* McKenzie's question, quoted by the Tribunal, was whether the claim that northern Māori had moved from oral to literate culture "implied not only a widespread ability to read and write fluently but also a readiness to shift from memory to written record, to accept a signature as a sign of full comprehension and legal commitment, to surrender the relativities of time, place and person in an oral culture to the presumed fixities of the written or printed word" (p. 258, quoting Donald McKenzie). The Tribunal's own finding: "In our view, there can be no doubt that the spoken word retained primacy among Bay of Islands and Hokianga Māori throughout the 1830s. Relatively few rangatira could read and write fluently" (p. 259). Estimates of the number who read ranged from "some hundreds" (Yate, 1833) to "not less than ten Thousand" (Markham, 1834) (p. 258); Buller in 1839 "was still counting literate Māori in the hundreds" (p. 258). Belich: "Maori literacy in the 1830s has been exaggerated somewhat by writers overeager to praise the Maori for being like 'Us'" (p. 259). Colenso printed a Māori New Testament, "5,000 copies of which became available early in 1838" (p. 258).
- *Who said so at the time (section 7.6.2, the debate at Waitangi, February 1840):* Te Kēmara, in Colenso's 1890 account, said "my land is gone, gone, all gone ... stolen, gone with the missionaries" and "for the Governor to be up and Te Kemara down ... No, no, no." Rewa, in Hobson's dispatch, said "Send the man away; do not sign the paper; if you do you will be reduced to the condition of slaves, and be obliged to break stones for the roads. Your land will be taken from you, and your dignity as chiefs will be destroyed" (pp. 357–358). Their objections concern land and rank; none of the passages I read has a chief objecting to a signature or to the writing as such.
- *Scale:* a territory about to become a colony; Stage 1 inquires into the Northland (Te Paparahi o te Raki) rangatira.
- *Bearing:* **Problem 1** — supports in a narrow form: two texts, a verbal explanation that omitted the English intention, and a signatory class (more than 500 rangatira in all across the nine copies, of whom 39 signed the English text; p. 386). The Tribunal says its report "does not contain findings in respect of claims" (p. xxii–xxiii), so it documents meaning, not harm. **Problem 2** — the standard is the Tribunal's reading of agreement. **Problem 3** — national. **Problem 5** — complicates: Māori literacy was substantial and growing (the 1827 translations; the demand for books), contrary to a picture of an illiterate people tricked by a paper.

### Group 5. Canada: courts on oral history and the written treaty

**delgamuukw1997** — *Delgamuukw v. British Columbia*, [1997] 3 S.C.R. 1010 (Supreme Court of Canada, 11 December 1997). https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/1569/index.do
- *Verification:* official court site; the page lists case number 23799, date and report cite.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (headnote, summary of the trial decision, paras 84–87 and 94–108). Judgment of Lamer C.J. and Cory and Major JJ., delivered by the Chief Justice. The trial decision is reported at [1991] 3 W.W.R. 97 (McEachern C.J., who "heard 374 days of evidence and argument").
- *Quotations (paragraph numbers):*
  - ¶84: "adapt the laws of evidence so that the aboriginal perspective on their practices, customs and traditions and on their relationship with the land, are given due weight by the courts. In practical terms, this requires the courts to come to terms with the oral histories of aboriginal societies, which, for many aboriginal nations, are the only record of their past."
  - ¶87: "the laws of evidence must be adapted in order that this type of evidence can be accommodated and placed on an equal footing with the types of historical evidence that courts are familiar with, which largely consists of historical documents."
  - ¶98 (on the trial judge): "The implication of the trial judge's reasoning is that oral histories should never be given any independent weight and are only useful as confirmatory evidence in aboriginal rights litigation. I fear that if this reasoning were followed, the oral histories of aboriginal peoples would be consistently and systematically undervalued by the Canadian legal system."
  - ¶106: the exclusion rationale "places aboriginal claimants whose societies record their past through oral history in a grave dilemma ... if those histories are discussed too much, and too close to the date of litigation, they may be discounted as being suspect ... The net effect may be that a society with such an oral tradition would never be able to establish a historical claim through the use of oral history in court."
  - The trial judge's grounds (summarised in ¶98): the oral histories did not "accurately convey historical truth", were "confined to the communities whose histories they were" and were "insufficiently detailed".
  - Outcome: the Court held the trial's factual findings could not stand and ordered a new trial (the summary says "there must be a new trial").
- *Scale:* two nations (Gitksan and Wet'suwet'en), 58,000 square kilometres claimed (headnote); a ruling with national effect.
- *Bearing:* **Problem 1** — supports: a named group, a court, a year (trial 1991, appeal 1997), a stated ground, and an end to the practice by judicial ruling. The standard is the principle the Court draws from *Van der Peet* in the context of s. 35(1) of the Constitution Act, 1982 (¶84, ¶98); that is a present-day legal standard, so **Problem 2** applies: it is a standard stated now, applied to a 20th-century trial, not an 11th- or 19th-century contemporary naming a wrong. **Problem 4** — the case turns on who may prove history by speech in a literate court; whether anything corresponds to it elsewhere is not for this facet.

**marshall1999** — *R. v. Marshall*, [1999] 3 S.C.R. 456 (17 September 1999). https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/1739/index.do
- *Verification:* official court site; the headnote gives file no. 26014 and the dates; Binnie J. wrote for Lamer C.J. and L'Heureux-Dubé, Cory and Iacobucci JJ., with Gonthier and McLachlin JJ. dissenting.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (headnote, ¶12–14, ¶35, ¶40).
- *Facts:* a Mi'kmaq man, Donald Marshall Jr., charged with fishery offences (the year of the charge: not found in what I read), relied on the treaties of 1760–61; the treaty text of 10 March 1760 contained a restrictive trade covenant and nothing on the right to catch and sell.
- *Quotations:*
  - ¶12: "where a treaty was concluded verbally and afterwards written up by representatives of the Crown, it would be unconscionable for the Crown to ignore the oral terms while relying on the written terms".
  - ¶14: "The Indian parties did not, for all practical purposes, have the opportunity to create their own written record of the negotiations."
  - ¶35: the evidence "demonstrates the inadequacy and incompleteness of the written memorial of the treaty terms by selectively isolating the restrictive trade covenant".
  - ¶40: "the Nova Scotia judgments erred in concluding that the only enforceable treaty obligations were those set out in the written document of March 10, 1760".
- *Scale:* Atlantic Canada, with a national precedent; the year of the wrong is 1760 and the year of the ruling 1999.
- *Bearing:* **Problem 1** — supports: the Court itself names the failure (the written record was incomplete and one party could not make its own), with a date, a people and a stated ground ("honour of the Crown", "unconscionable"). Lower courts in Nova Scotia held the opposite. **Problem 2** — the stated standard is a modern doctrine of Crown honour, applied backward. The ruling is a court speaking in 1999 about 1760; it is not a Mi'kmaq or British contemporary speaking in 1760.

## (c) Synthesis

**Q. Who lost legal or civic standing when written records replaced memory and oral testimony, and who said so?** The sources document five distinct events, listed from best to worst documented. They do not document a single process, and the groups differ: customary tenants, First Nations, treaty signatories, commoners who could not read, and magnates.

**Failure 1. Customary tenants' charters seized, Barnet, St Albans Abbey, 1296–1345 (and 1381).**
- *What was done:* the abbey called individual tenants "to show by what means they claimed to hold their land", seized the tenement and the charter, and re-granted it "in villenagio for services" (Harrison pp. 21–22); eleven tenants in June 1345; William Atte Penne prosecuted in 1344 for allegedly forged charters.
- *To whom, where, when:* customary tenants of the manor of Barnet; 1296 first presentment, clusters 1311–14 and 1344–45.
- *Stated ground:* the abbey's right to the land and the fear that customary land "might become free land" (pp. 21–22). The 1275 ordinance forbade free men to hold villein land.
- *Who objected:* the tenants, by the 1381 demand to burn the court-roll book, on Walsingham's hostile report (p. 20); no tenant's own statement of grievance in the sources read.
- *What ended it:* the 1381 rising collapsed "before the abbot could be forced to hand over the book" (p. 20); no end to the practice is documented.
- *Strength:* best-documented medieval case in this facet, but a single manor, read in part, through a thesis.

**Failure 2. Judicial discounting of oral history, British Columbia, trial 1991 to appeal 1997.**
- *What was done:* the trial judge gave the Gitksan and Wet'suwet'en oral histories no independent weight, and found that they had not shown the occupation needed for "ownership" (¶107; summary of the trial).
- *Stated ground (trial):* the histories did not "accurately convey historical truth", were confined to the communities, and were "insufficiently detailed" (¶98).
- *Who objected:* the claimants on appeal; the Supreme Court said the approach would leave oral histories "consistently and systematically undervalued" (¶98).
- *What ended it:* the Supreme Court's 1997 ruling and a new trial (summary). Whether a new trial concluded: not found in what I read.
- *Strength:* primary source, read in part; modern, so the standard is a modern one.

**Failure 3. The Crown's reliance on a written treaty that omitted agreed oral terms; the Waitangi texts.**
- *Mi'kmaq, 1760:* the written treaty omitted the right to catch and sell; the Court held in 1999 that "the Nova Scotia judgments erred in concluding that the only enforceable treaty obligations were those set out in the written document of March 10, 1760" (¶40). At ¶12 Binnie J. restates, from Dickson J. in Guerin, that it is "unconscionable for the Crown to ignore the oral terms while relying on the written terms"; at ¶14 he says, of treaty interpretation in general, that "The Indian parties did not, for all practical purposes, have the opportunity to create their own written record of the negotiations."
- *Waitangi, February 1840:* more than 500 rangatira signed in all, and "Only 39" of them signed the English text (p. 386); the Tribunal found they did not cede sovereignty and that Hobson "did not explain" the English intention (p. xxii).
- *Who objected at the time:* Te Kēmara and Rewa (pp. 357–358), on land and rank; in neither case does a source I read have the speaker objecting to the writing.
- *What ended it:* nothing ended it in the sources read; the Tribunal's Stage 1 report states it makes no findings on claims (p. xxii–xxiii).
- *Strength:* both are primary sources, read in part; the 1840 case documents a disputed meaning, not an established wrong.

**Failure 4. Benefit of clergy: a reading test decided who was hanged.**
- *What was done:* to be discharged from a clergyable felony, a commoner had to read; Blackstone: "those men, who could not read, if under the degree of peerage, were hanged."
- *Stated ground for ending it (as Blackstone reports it for 5 Ann. c. 6):* learning is "no extenuations of guilt", so death was "too severe for the ignorant also".
- *Who objected:* not found by name; the Act of Anne is the objection.
- *What ended it:* 5 Ann. c. 6 removed the reading condition (date not in Blackstone; a later statute removed the privilege altogether: date not found in any source I opened).
- *Strength:* the mechanism is clear and primary-sourced via Blackstone; scope, dates and who was affected are not.

**Failure 5 (weakest). Magnates' unwritten claims, quo warranto, 1278–1290s.**
- Clanchy PP18: "Memory ... if unsupported by clear written evidence, was ruled out of court." PP56: Earl Warenne's "ancient and rusty sword". The affected group is the magnates, and the story's reliability is doubtful on the repo card's report of Clanchy. Under the plan's definition (a wrong to identifiable people, named as a wrong by a contemporary or by a stated standard), this is a loss of standing but not a documented ethical wrong, and the claimants were not the people the paper's theme concerns.

**What the sources do not support.**
- No source I read has a medieval contemporary calling the displacement of memory by writing a wrong done to the illiterate. The one contemporary-style complaint (Warenne) is a story about the powerful.
- The North American deed-fraud thesis is contested among the sources (Baker 1989 against the fraud reading; Blaeser 1992 and the Rhode Island Archives' caveat asserting non-comprehension; Lewandoski 2019 showing Banner and Greer disagree). I read no primary case that proves a signatory was unable to read the deed he signed: the Rhode Island transcription shows marks, and the archive's caveat is unsourced.
- Stock is unread, so nothing in this facet reports his argument.

**Claims the sources contradict.**
1. *"Documents displaced peasants' memory-based tenure."* Harrison's abstract finds "no significant shift towards the use of written evidence" in the Wakefield and Alrewas manor courts across the 13th–14th centuries, and her Barnet chapter shows lords seizing peasants' charters. Clanchy's thesis (as quoted in Harrison p. 2) that "literate modes were familiar even to serfs" is not refuted, but the two together show that serfs used writing and lords policed it.
2. *"Native signatories were defrauded by deeds they could not read."* Baker 1989 (abstract): "Maine Indians were not cheated of their lands in a fraudulent 'deed game.'" The Rhode Island Archives and Blaeser say the opposite in general terms, without a case.
3. *"Māori were an illiterate people who signed what they could not read."* Waitangi Tribunal pp. 257–259: some hundreds, by some accounts perhaps more, could read by 1839; the Tribunal sides with oral primacy. The missionary estimates and Markham's "ten Thousand" were rejected or doubted by the Tribunal's authorities (McKenzie, Belich).
4. *"The reading test of benefit of clergy was a barrier that harmed the poor."* Johnstone's abstract says the privilege "saved women, children and the poor from the gallows"; Blackstone says the unlearned commoner was hanged. They are compatible only if the privilege first excluded and then widened; neither source gives dates.
5. *"The Earl Warenne story shows barons shut out by writing."* The story is told by a chronicler of the 1280s and Clanchy reportedly treats it as doubtful (card, not verified).
