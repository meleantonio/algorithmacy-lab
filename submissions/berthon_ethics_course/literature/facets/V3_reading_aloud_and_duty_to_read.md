# V3: Reading aloud and the duty to read

*Facet V3 of research round 7 (version 4), 2026-10-14. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** How law and institutions treated a person bound by a text that person could not read. I asked four things. Was reading the text aloud, or explaining it, treated as enough? Who bore the burden? Did any rule do more than disclose, by adding a third party, a check on understanding, a remedy or a change of medium? Did anyone scaffold the person toward reading? The sources fall into six groups: (1) the duty-to-read rule (*L'Estrange*, *Upton*, and the US cases on illiterate and non-English-speaking signers); (2) *non est factum* and its narrowing in *Saunders v Anglia*; (3) read-over certificates for marksmen (affidavit jurats, statements of truth, probate of wills signed by blind or illiterate testators); (4) notarial reading in Spain, from the 1862 *Ley del Notariado* to the 2021 accessibility amendment; (5) interpreters at treaties, through the US canon of construction in *Jones v Meehan*; (6) modern echoes (California Civil Code §1632, and the empirical record on whether reading a consent form aloud produces understanding).

**Legend.** *Full* = I read the whole operative text this session. *Part* = I read the pages or paragraphs cited. *Abstract* = database abstract only; nothing beyond it is claimed. *Record* = catalogue or search-summary entry only; not relied on for any fact. Case pages are report pages (star pages for CAP texts, slip-opinion pages for circuit opinions as printed at the foot of each page).

**Rules honoured.** No e-mail address went into any request. I did not use Unpaywall. I did not bypass any bot wall. Downloads sit in `~/iit-playground/berthon_ethics_local/pdfs/V3/` and were only text-extracted (pdftotext, or HTML-to-text with `python3 -I`).

## Findings

### 1. The duty-to-read rule

**lestrange1934graucob.** *L'Estrange v F Graucob Ltd* [1934] 2 KB 394–407. *Full* (OCR).
- The headnote, p. 394, says the case was decided "by the Divisional Court (Scrutton and Maugham L.JJ.)" on appeal from Llandudno County Court. It is often called a Court of Appeal decision. The report says Divisional Court.
- The buyer replied that "at the time when she signed the order form she had not read it and knew nothing of its contents, and that the clause excluding warranties could not easily be read owing to the smallness of the print" (headnote, p. 394).
- **Reading aloud was in evidence and made no difference.** The seller's sales supervisor, Mr Page, testified "that he sat near the plaintiff and read to her the whole of the brown paper document including the small print, that the plaintiff asked no question about the small print" (statement of evidence, pp. 398–399; OCR page heads uncertain). She said "the defendants did not read it to her or tell her to read it." The county court judge found that "the defendants did not do what was reasonably sufficient to bring these conditions to the notice of the plaintiff" (Scrutton L.J., p. 402).
- Scrutton L.J., p. 403: "When a document containing contractual terms is signed, then, in the absence of fraud, or, I will add, misrepresentation, the party signing it is bound, and it is wholly immaterial whether he has read the document or not." He confines the notice cases to unsigned documents: "These cases have no application when the document has been signed" (p. 403).
- Maugham L.J., p. 405: "I regret the decision to which I have come, but I am bound by legal rules". The print was "regrettably small ... but quite legible" (p. 405). P. 407: "it is, in my view, an irrelevant circumstance that the plaintiff did not read, or hear of, the parts of the sales document which are in small print". He adds that "I could wish that the contract had been in a simpler and more usual form" (p. 407).
- **Bearing.** *For* wrong (a). The signature carries the risk, and the court treats the dispute over whether the text was read aloud as irrelevant ("did not read, or hear of"). *Narrows* the disclosure framing: the rule does not hold that disclosure suffices. It holds that **signature** suffices, so neither reading nor reading aloud is required.

**upton1875tribilcock.** Already carded (verified). P. 50: "if he will not read what he signs, he alone is responsible for his omission." The card notes that Tribilcock was a literate businessman with the documents in hand. *Morales* (below) quotes *Upton* at p. 50 as the root of the modern rule.

**washington2004bailey.** *Washington Mutual Finance Group, LLC v Bailey*, 364 F.3d 260 (5th Cir. 2004), No. 02-60794, filed 19 March 2004; Jolly J. for Garwood, Jolly and Clement JJ. *Full* (slip opinion).
- The issue, slip p. 3: "the effect of an individual's illiteracy on the enforcement of an arbitration agreement, which the individual admits he signed, but because of his illiteracy, denies he understood." The district court had held "the individual's illiteracy, coupled with a lack of oral disclosure, rendered the agreement procedurally unconscionable. We conclude the district court erred and REVERSE."
- Slip p. 9, quoting *Mixon v Sovereign Camp* (Miss. 1930): "the suggestion of illiteracy cannot prevail, for the manifest reason that there cannot be two separate departments in the law of contracts, one for the educated and another for those who are not". Quoting *J. R. Watkins Co. v Runnels* (Miss. 1965): "[a] person cannot avoid a written contract which he has entered into on the ground that he did not read it or have it read to him."
- Slip p. 10, quoting *Dixon* (S.D. Miss. 2003): "[a] person who cannot read has a duty to find someone to read the contract to him". The holding: "the inability to read and understand the arbitration agreement does not render the agreement unconscionable or otherwise unenforceable."
- **The court refused a duty of oral disclosure** (slip pp. 10–11). It rejected the holding that the agreement failed "because WM Finance failed specifically to inform the Illiterate Appellees that they were signing an arbitration agreement after having been made aware of the Illiterate Appellees' inability to read". The court found "no authority supporting the district court's assertion that illiteracy removes this affirmative obligation from a signatory."
- Slip pp. 11–12: the borrowers "informed WM Finance that they could not read and inquired as to the nature of the documents they were signing". Their questions "were met with a general response: you are signing insurance and financial paperwork. This response is not plainly misleading." A one-line oral gloss, given to people the lender knew could not read, was enough to defeat the claim that they were misled.
- Slip p. 13: any false impression "would indisputably have been cleared up had the Illiterate Appellees simply complied with their legal obligation to read the contract or have it read to them".
- **Bearing.** The strongest statement in this facet *for* wrong (a). The person who cannot read must find a reader, and the lender that knows of the illiteracy owes no oral explanation. *Narrows* the disclosure framing again: the lower court tried to impose a duty to disclose, and the appeal court removed it.

**morales2008sun.** *Morales v Sun Constructors, Inc.*, 541 F.3d 218 (3d Cir. 2008), No. 07-3806, filed 28 August 2008; Chagares J. for the majority; Fuentes J. dissenting. *Full*.
- Facts, slip pp. 2–3: a Spanish-speaking welder; the agreement was "written in English, a language Morales cannot understand". Arbitration took "nearly 8 of the 13 pages". The employer's orientation leader asked a bilingual fellow applicant, Hodge, "to explain to Morales what Langner was saying and help him fill out the documents". Hodge "did not specifically explain the arbitration clause".
- Slip p. 5 quotes *Upton* at 50 and then Williston: one "who is ignorant of the language in which a document is written, or who is illiterate ... is bound, in the absence of fraud, if the person does not require the document to be read to him".
- Slip p. 6: "In the absence of fraud, the fact that an offeree cannot read, write, speak, or understand the English language is immaterial to whether an English-language agreement the offeree executes is enforceable." The court cites *Wilkisius v Sheehan* (Mass. 1927): Lithuanian buyers were bound because their failure was due "to their own inability to read, write, speak or understand the English language, and to the incapacity of the interpreter".
- Slip p. 7: "It was Morales' obligation to ensure he understood the Agreement before signing." Slip p. 8: the "'incomplete translation' was due to Morales' failure to request any explanation or translation."
- Dissent, slip pp. 10–12. Fuentes J. reads *Kwetkauskas* as binding only the *negligent* illiterate. He finds that Sun "assigned Hodge, a coworker who himself was not fluent in English, to translate the document", and that Hodge "neglected to translate the arbitration clauses".
- **Bearing.** *For* wrong (a), extended from illiteracy to language. *Narrows*: the dissent shows that the bench split on whether an interpreter supplied by the stronger party shifts the risk back to it.

### 2. *Non est factum* and its narrowing

**foster1869mackinnon.** Already carded (verified). P. 711: the plea protected "a blind man, or a man who cannot read" who "has a written contract falsely read over to him". The protection required both a **false** reading and no negligence. A true reading aloud bound the listener.

**connell2016not.** Connell (2016), *VUWLR* 47(2): 245–266. *Part* (abstract; Part II pp. 248–251; Part III.A–B), read from `G/connell.txt`. Already in facet G and the arm's bib; it has no card.
- Abstract, p. 245: "In its early days, non est factum was available only to blind and illiterate persons who, without being careless, relied on another party's seriously flawed explanation of the document."
- P. 249: "The first major expansion of non est factum occurred at a time when a large proportion of the population was unable to read, and transactions involving deeds were commonplace. The plea was extended to blind and illiterate persons who had relied on someone else's explanation of the deed." Courts then "sought to limit its scope" by requiring a fundamental difference and no carelessness.
- *Saunders v Anglia* [1971] AC 1004, as Connell quotes it (nn. 18–20, pp. 249–250). Lord Reid at 1016 would extend the plea to those "unable through no fault of their own to have without explanation any real understanding of the purport of a particular document, whether that be from defective education, illness or innate incapacity". He adds: "In general I do not think he can be heard to say that he signed in reliance on someone he trusted." Lord Wilberforce at 1025 names a "residue of difficult cases" including "illiterate or senile persons who cannot read, or apprehend, a legal document".
- P. 251: the signer must prove that they were not careless ("it is up to the signer to prove that they have not been careless", citing *Saunders* at 1019). Lord Wilberforce's "compromise explanation" balances "the protection of innocent third parties who rely on the validity of apparently legitimate signed legal documents" against relief for signers who did not consent (p. 251, citing *Saunders* at 1023).
- The facts of *Saunders*, from Connell Part III (pp. 252–253). Mrs Gallie, a widow, signed on Lee's false explanation. The House of Lords refused the plea because the document was not fundamentally different from what she thought and because she had been careless.
- **Bearing.** *Narrows* the thesis. The one doctrine built for the person who could not read protected that person only against a **false** reading. The burden of proving no carelessness stayed on the signer. *Saunders* narrowed the plea expressly to protect third parties who rely on signed paper. The doctrine is *for* the thesis in its effect, since the burden falls on the person. It is *against* a simple "the law ignored them" story, because the law did recognise the illiterate signer as a special case.

### 3. Read-over certificates for marksmen

**moj1998practice32.** Civil Procedure Rules, Practice Direction 32 (Evidence), England and Wales, page updated 1 October 2023. *Full* for paras 7.1–7.2 and Annex 1.
- Para 7.1: "Where an affidavit is sworn by a person who is unable to read or sign it, the person before whom the affidavit is sworn must certify in the jurat that: (1) he read the affidavit to the deponent, (2) the deponent appeared to understand it, and (3) the deponent signed or made his mark, in his presence."
- Para 7.2: without the certificate "the affidavit may not be used in evidence unless the court is satisfied that it was read to the deponent and that he appeared to understand it."
- Annex 1, the jurat form: "I having first read over the contents of this affidavit to the deponent [if there are exhibits, add 'and explained the nature and effect of the exhibits referred to in it'] who appeared to understand it and approved its content as accurate, and made his mark on the affidavit in my presence."
- PD 22 para 2.4 (statements of truth) uses the same form for a person "unable to read or sign a document". The certifier adds that the person "appeared to understand ... the declaration of truth and the consequences of making a false declaration".
- **Bearing.** This is the read-over ritual, still in force. It adds a third party, the commissioner, who certifies two things: that the text was read and that the person **appeared** to understand it. The test is the certifier's impression, and nothing checks understanding. *For* the claim that read-over was a disclosure ritual that made the text usable against or for the person. *Narrows*: the rule does go beyond bare disclosure, because a named officer must attest to apparent understanding, and without that attestation the court must itself be satisfied.

**uk1987noncontentious.** Non-Contentious Probate Rules 1987 (SI 1987/2024), r. 13 ("Execution of will of blind or illiterate testator"), in force 1 January 1988; "district judge or" inserted by SI 1991/1876. *Full*.
- "Before admitting to proof a will which appears to have been signed by a blind or illiterate testator or by another person by direction of the testator, or which for any other reason raises doubt as to the testator having had knowledge of the contents of the will at the time of its execution, the [district judge or] registrar shall satisfy himself that the testator had such knowledge."
- **Bearing.** *Against* (in the useful sense). Here the law did more than disclose. A judicial officer must be **satisfied of knowledge** before the text takes effect, and the risk of an unread will falls on the person who propounds it, not on the testator. Probate is the one setting in this facet where the institution checks understanding instead of presuming it. The testator, however, is dead and cannot be bound, so the rule protects the estate's integrity rather than a living reader.

### 4. Notarial reading: Castile to modern Spain

**almeida2019firmo.** Already carded (verified): the Pragmática of 1503 required notaries to read their notes "presentes las partes e los testigos" (p. 24, quoting Marchant Rivera). The card records that "aloud" is **not** in the source. The source says the notes are read in the parties' presence.

**spain1862ley.** *Ley del Notariado*, 28 May 1862 (BOE-A-1862-4073), art. 25. *Full* for art. 25, original text (in force 19 June 1862) and current text (from 3 September 2021).
- Original and current para. 3: "Los Notarios darán fe de haber leído a las partes y a los testigos instrumentales la escritura íntegra, o de haberles permitido que la lean, a su elección, antes de que la firmen, ... y de haber advertido a unos y a otros que tienen el derecho de leerla por sí." The notary attests that he read the whole deed aloud, or let the parties read it, at their choice, and that he warned them of their right to read it themselves.
- Para. 1: deeds "se redactarán en lengua castellana".
- Para. added by Ley 8/2021 (from 3 September 2021): persons with disabilities "podrán utilizar los apoyos, instrumentos y ajustes razonables que resulten precisos, incluyendo sistemas aumentativos y alternativos, braille, lectura fácil, pictogramas, dispositivos multimedia de fácil acceso, intérpretes, sistemas de apoyos a la comunicación oral, lengua de signos ...".
- **Bearing.** The 1862 rule is disclosure by reading, with the person's own reading as an option and a right. The 2021 amendment changes the **medium** (easy-read, pictograms, multimedia, interpreters). That is the move the author says the post fails to make. It is *against* any claim that legal institutions never left the text. It is *for* the claim that they took 159 years to do it, and only for persons with disabilities.

**spain1944reglamento.** *Reglamento de la organización y régimen del Notariado*, Decreto of 2 June 1944 (BOE-A-1944-6578), arts. 147 and 193 as amended (RD 45/2007; partial annulment by STS 20 May 2008, which does not touch the passages quoted). *Part* (arts. 147, 193).
- Art. 193, para. 2: "se entenderá que ésta [la lectura] es íntegra cuando el notario hubiera comunicado el contenido del instrumento con la extensión necesaria para el cabal conocimiento de su alcance y efectos, atendidas las circunstancias de los comparecientes." A reading counts as complete when the notary has **communicated** the content as far as the persons' circumstances require for full knowledge of its scope and effects.
- Art. 193, para. 3: the notary attests "que después de la lectura los comparecientes han hecho constar haber quedado debidamente informados del contenido del instrumento".
- Art. 193, para. 4: a deaf party must read for themselves, and if unable, an interpreter of sign language "designado al efecto por el otorgante" must take part. A blind party: "será suficiente que preste su conformidad a la lectura hecha por el notario."
- Art. 147: the notary "informará a aquéllos del valor y alcance de su redacción". In final para., not annulled: "el notario insistirá en informar a una de las partes respecto de las cláusulas ... propuestas por la otra ... y prestará asistencia especial al otorgante necesitado de ella."
- **Bearing.** *Against / narrows.* Civil-law notarial practice redefined "reading" as communication sufficient for understanding, calibrated to the person. It also tasks a neutral official with "special assistance" to the weaker party. The burden sits on a third party, not on the reader. For the blind, though, consent to the notary's reading still suffices, so reading aloud remains the floor. Facet M3's caution on the UINL principles applies here too: this is the norm, and I read no evidence on practice.

### 5. Interpreters at treaties: the US canon

**jones1899meehan.** *Jones v Meehan*, 175 U.S. 1 (1899), Gray J. for the Court; argued 27–28 April 1898, decided 30 October 1899. *Part* (syllabus, statement, opinion pp. 1–12).
- Pp. 10–11: in construing a treaty with an Indian tribe, it must be "borne in mind that the negotiations for the treaty are conducted, on the part of the United States, ... by representatives skilled in diplomacy, masters of a written language, ... and assisted by an interpreter employed by themselves; that the treaty is drawn up by them and in their own language". The Indians, by contrast, "have no written language and are wholly unfamiliar with all the forms of legal expression, and whose only knowledge of the terms in which the treaty is framed is that imparted to them by the interpreter employed by the United States; and that the treaty must therefore be construed, not according to the technical meaning of its words to learned lawyers, but in the sense in which they would naturally be understood by the Indians."
- P. 11, quoting Marshall C.J. in *Worcester v Georgia* (1832), 6 Pet. at 551–552. On the Hopewell treaty: "every one makes his mark; no chief was capable of signing his name. It is probable the treaty was interpreted to them." And: "Is it reasonable to suppose that the Indians, who could not write, and most probably could not read, who certainly were not critical judges of our language, should distinguish the word 'allotted' from the words 'marked out'?" McLean J., concurring there: "The language used in treaties with the Indians should never be construed to their prejudice."
- P. 10 frames this through wardship: "they are in a state of pupilage" (quoting *Cherokee Nation v Georgia*).
- **Bearing.** *Against* the burden half of the thesis, for one setting. Here a court held that interpretation by the drafter's own interpreter did **not** suffice, and it moved the risk of the text onto the drafter through a rule of construction. It is a remedy after the fact. It rests on a paternalist premise ("pupilage"), and it did not stop the dispossession that later cases allowed. v3's Waitangi material, with the Māori text and the English text diverging, is the matching case from the other side.

### 6. Modern echoes

**california2025civil.** Cal. Civ. Code §1632 (Contract Translation Act), text "updated Jan. 1, 2025". *Full* (from the public.law mirror of leginfo).
- (a)(1): "This section was enacted in 1976 to increase consumer information and protections for the state's sizeable and growing Spanish-speaking population."
- (b): a business "who negotiates primarily in Spanish, Chinese, Tagalog, Vietnamese, or Korean, orally or in writing, ... shall deliver to the other party ... and before the execution thereof, a translation of the contract or agreement in the language in which the contract or agreement was negotiated, that includes a translation of every term and condition".
- (h): the duty does not apply if the party negotiates "through the party's own interpreter". That interpreter must be an adult who can "read with full understanding both the English language" and the other language, and must not be "employed by, or whose service is not made available through," the business.
- (k)(1): "Upon a failure to comply with the provisions of this section, the person aggrieved may rescind the contract". (l): "Any waiver of a provision of this section is contrary to public policy and is void and unenforceable."
- **Bearing.** *Narrows.* The statute moves the burden onto the business, adds a remedy (rescission), and treats an interpreter supplied by the business as no interpreter. That is the issue that split the *Morales* bench. But its cure is still a **text** to read, now in another language. It is triggered only when the business itself negotiated in that language, and it does not reach illiteracy.

**sudore2006use.** Sudore et al. (2006), *JGIM* 21(9). *Abstract*.
- The modified process was a consent form "written at a sixth-grade level" and "read to participants, combined with 7 comprehension questions and targeted education, repeated until comprehension achieved (teach-to-goal)". Among 204 participants aged 50 and over, 40% had limited literacy. "Only 28% of subjects answered all comprehension questions correctly on the first pass." After the second pass, 80% did. "With a teach-to-goal strategy, 98% of participants who engaged in the consent process achieved complete comprehension." Lower literacy was associated with needing more passes.
- **Bearing.** The modern measurement the historical record lacks. Reading a simplified text **aloud** left most people (72%) without full comprehension on the first pass. Iterated teaching, with checks, closed the gap. This is *for* wrong (c), that scaffolding and not disclosure produces understanding, at abstract depth.

**flory2004interventions.** Flory & Emanuel (2004), *JAMA* 292(13): 1593. *Abstract*.
- 42 trials. Multimedia: 3 of 12 improved understanding. Enhanced forms: 6 of 15, "but 5 of 6 trials were of limited quality". Extended discussion: 3 of 5 significant, with 2 trends. Conclusion: "Having a study team member or a neutral educator spend more time talking one-on-one to study participants appears to be the most effective available way of improving research participants' understanding". "Lower education" was associated with less understanding.
- **Bearing.** *For* (c), with care. Better texts helped little, and a person who talks the participant through helped most. This fits the sister arm's verified finding that disclosure evidence is mixed in direction (`wt-dxd-foundation/.../facets/L_disclosure_and_choice.md`). The evidence does not say that people review badly. It says that comprehension follows interaction more than text.

Further abstract-level support, not carded: Tamariz et al. (2013), *JGIM* 28(1): 121–126. Of six studies on low-literacy subjects, two that changed the IC's readability "resulted in the lowest comprehension", and teach-back or teach-to-goal "achieved the highest level of comprehension". Glaser et al. (2020), *MDM* 40(2): 119–143: "100% (3/3) of verbal discussion with test/feedback or teach-back interventions" improved comprehension, against "43% (6/14) of written interventions".

## Sorted by bearing on the thesis

### For
1. **Burden on the person (a).** *L'Estrange* (p. 403), *Upton* (p. 50), *Bailey* (slip pp. 9–11, 13) and *Morales* (slip pp. 5–8) state one rule. The signer bears the text, and the person who cannot read, or cannot read the language, must find a reader or translator. *Bailey* says it outright: "[a] person who cannot read has a duty to find someone to read the contract to him."
2. **Read-aloud as risk-shifting ritual.** In *L'Estrange* the seller's agent swore that he read the whole form aloud, small print included. The court held whether she read "or hear[d] of" it irrelevant (pp. 398–399, 407). PD 32's jurat records reading over and **apparent** understanding (para 7.1, Annex 1), and the Pragmática records reading in the parties' presence. Each makes the text usable once read, and none tests understanding.
3. **Non est factum narrowed to protect reliance on paper.** *Saunders* (via Connell pp. 249–251): the signer must prove no carelessness, and the plea yields to "innocent third parties who rely on the validity of apparently legitimate signed legal documents".
4. **Scaffolding (c).** I found no legal instrument in these sources that moved the signer toward reading. The empirical record (Sudore 2006; Flory & Emanuel 2004; Tamariz 2013, abstracts) shows that read-aloud alone leaves most low-literacy people short of comprehension, and that iterative teaching closes the gap.

### Narrows
1. **The historical rule did not treat disclosure as sufficient. It treated the signature as sufficient.** *L'Estrange* makes reading aloud immaterial. *Bailey* reversed a lower court that tried to require oral disclosure to known illiterates. If the post's error is to treat disclosure as sufficient, the common-law precedent is a weaker standard still, and the post sits nearer the reforms than the original fault. The author's analogy has to say which stage of the history the post repeats.
2. **Non est factum is a real exception for the person who cannot read** (*Foster* p. 711). Its trigger is a **false** reading, and the person must not have been negligent.
3. **Third parties added.** The affidavit commissioner (PD 32), the probate registrar (NCPR r. 13), the Spanish notary (Reglamento arts. 147, 193) and California's "own interpreter" who must be independent of the business (§1632(h)) all put a third person between the text and the person who cannot read it. Most of them certify, and only the registrar and the Spanish notary must reach knowledge.
4. **§1632 shifts the burden but keeps the text.** The business must translate, the consumer may rescind, and waiver is void. The cure is still a document, now in the reader's language.

### Against
1. **Law did more than disclose in at least three places.** (i) Probate: the registrar "shall satisfy himself that the testator had such knowledge" (NCPR r. 13). (ii) Treaties: *Jones v Meehan* (pp. 10–11) construes the text as the non-reading party "would naturally" have understood it, because the only interpreter was the drafter's. (iii) Spanish notarial law defines a complete reading as communication sufficient for "cabal conocimiento ... atendidas las circunstancias de los comparecientes" (art. 193), and it tasks the notary with "asistencia especial" (art. 147).
2. **Law did change the medium.** Spain's 2021 amendment to art. 25 allows easy-read, pictograms, multimedia and interpreters at the notary. That is the shift away from a literacy-based experience that the author says is missing. The thesis should say "rarely and late", not "never".
3. **Courts split.** *Morales* has a dissent, and *Bailey* reversed a district court. The burden rule was contested from the bench, not just from outside.

## What the paper can say (notes for the drafter, not prose)

1. The common law did not require that a text be read to a person who could not read it. In *L'Estrange v Graucob* the court held a signer bound "whether he has read the document or not" (Scrutton L.J., [1934] 2 KB at 403). It called it "irrelevant" whether she "did not read, or hear of" the small print (Maugham L.J. at 407), although the seller's agent swore he had read it all aloud.
2. In *Washington Mutual Finance v Bailey* (5th Cir. 2004) the court put the burden on the person who could not read: "[a] person who cannot read has a duty to find someone to read the contract to him" (slip op. 10). It reversed a ruling that a lender who knew of the borrowers' illiteracy had to tell them what they were signing (slip op. 3, 10–11).
3. The rule reached language as well as literacy. In *Morales v Sun Constructors* (3d Cir. 2008) the court held that a worker's inability to "read, write, speak, or understand the English language is immaterial" absent fraud (slip op. 6). The fault lay with his "failure to request any explanation or translation" (slip op. 8). A dissent disagreed.
4. The one doctrine built for the signer who could not read, *non est factum*, protected only against a text "falsely read over" (*Foster v Mackinnon* at 711). *Saunders v Anglia* narrowed it to protect "innocent third parties who rely on the validity of apparently legitimate signed legal documents", as Connell (2016, p. 251) summarises Lord Wilberforce at 1023.
5. Read-over survives as ritual. An English affidavit by a deponent who cannot read needs a jurat certifying that it was read to the deponent and that the deponent "appeared to understand it" (CPR PD 32, para 7.1). The certifier's impression is the test, and nobody checks the understanding.
6. Where the law did more than disclose, it checked knowledge, moved the risk, or changed the medium. A probate registrar must be "satisf[ied] ... that the testator had such knowledge" before a blind or illiterate testator's will is proved (NCPR 1987 r. 13). *Jones v Meehan* (1899, pp. 10–11) construed treaties as the signatories "would naturally" understand them, because their "only knowledge" of the terms came from "the interpreter employed by the United States".
7. Spanish notarial law turned "reading" into communication fitted to the person. A complete reading is one that conveys the deed "con la extensión necesaria para el cabal conocimiento de su alcance y efectos, atendidas las circunstancias de los comparecientes" (Reglamento Notarial art. 193). In 2021 Spain added easy-read, pictograms and multimedia to art. 25 of the 1862 *Ley del Notariado*, 159 years after that article first made reading the deed the notary's duty.
8. California's Contract Translation Act (Civ. Code §1632, 1976) moves the burden onto the business, allows rescission and voids waiver. Its remedy is still a document, a full translation the consumer must read.
9. Modern consent research measures what the historical rules assumed. When a simplified consent form was read aloud to older, often low-literacy adults, only 28% understood it fully on the first pass. Teaching to goal brought this to 98% (Sudore et al. 2006, abstract). Reviews find one-to-one discussion more effective than better texts (Flory & Emanuel 2004, abstract).
10. The historical charge has to be stated precisely. The law of the oral-to-written transition did not treat disclosure as sufficient ethical design. It treated the **signature** as sufficient. Disclosure duties, read-over certificates and translation mandates came later, as partial reforms. The author's claim that the post repeats the transition's mistakes is strongest as a claim that the post stops at the reform stage (disclosure, readable records) and does not take the further steps the record shows were possible: a knowledge check, risk moved onto the drafter, a change of medium, and teaching to goal.
