# Facet M1: the critical and social study of literacy, and who gains a position over learners

*Facet M1, 2026-10-13. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

## What this facet covers

Round 4, facet M1. The question is whether literacy studies already has a name for the parties who
enable, regulate or withhold literacy and gain by it (Brandt's "sponsors"), what Street's autonomous and
ideological models imply for treating "AI literacy" as a neutral skill, which works treat literacy as
violence, property or gatekeeping, which histories of mass literacy bear on the five practices, who has
already argued that "literacy" is the wrong name for a technical competence (Vee above all), and what a
literacy scholar would say the outline gets wrong. I do not redo Graff's *Literacy Myth*, Freire, Clanchy
or the Street/Graff interview (facets F, L2, Z).

## (b) Entries

### 1. Brandt, "Sponsors of Literacy" (1998) and its 1997 report version — `brandt1998sponsors`, `brandt1997sponsors`

- **Reference.** Brandt, Deborah. 1998. "Sponsors of Literacy." *College Composition and Communication* 49(2): 165–185. DOI 10.58680/ccc19983181 (Crossref-resolved, title matched). Earlier version: Brandt, Deborah. 1997. *The Sponsors of Literacy*. CELA Report Series 7.12. National Research Center on English Learning and Achievement, University at Albany, SUNY. ERIC ED412537, https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED412537.pdf.
- **Read depth.** The 1998 journal article: **metadata only** (NCTE site behind a bot wall; no open copy I was willing to use). The 1997 report: **read in part** (PDF pp. 4–10 and 24–25 of 31; the rest keyword-searched). The wording of the report may differ from the journal text, so every quotation below is cited to the report.
- **What a sponsor is.** "Sponsors, as I have come to think of them, are any agents, local or distant, concrete or abstract, who enable, support, teach, or model, as well as recruit, regulate, suppress, or withhold literacy and gain advantage by it in some way." (Report, PDF p. 6.) Morris Young quotes the same definition from Brandt's 2001 book (*Literacy in American Lives*, p. 19) with "and model" and a comma after "withhold" (Young 2013, p. 10). The two wordings differ; I could not check either against the journal.
- **Interests and compulsion.** "Although the interests of the sponsor and the sponsored do not have to converge (and, in fact, may conflict) sponsors nevertheless set the terms for access to literacy and wield powerful incentives for compliance and loyalty." (PDF p. 6.) "In whatever form, sponsors deliver the ideological freight that must be borne for access to what they have." (PDF p. 8.)
- **Withholding.** Withholding sits in the definition. The text gives it no sustained case of its own. The nearest passage names "the most notorious impositions and deprivations by church or state" in a list that also includes "benign sharing between adults and youths" and "euphemized coercions in schools and workplaces" (PDF p. 8). Brandt's own cases are 20th-century American learners; the one case of exclusion she works through is unequal access (Raymond Branch and Dora Lopez, PDF pp. 10–13), not a legal bar. Her only sentence on legal deprivation is a passing one in the same passage: "African Americans in slavery taught each other to understand the Bible in subversively liberatory ways" under "Baptist and Methodist ministries" (PDF p. 8, citing Cornelius 1991).
- **Rationing.** "[B]ut it also explains why the powerful work so persistently to conscript and ration the powers of literacy. The competition to harness literacy, to manage, measure, teach, and exploit it, has intensified throughout the century." (PDF p. 9.) She adds that sponsors "contribute to what is called 'the literacy crisis'", the gap between rising standards and people's ability to meet them (PDF p. 10). That is the paper's §3.5 and its "Promise" row seen from the supply side.
- **A case from computing.** Branch, the advantaged learner, met a mainframe in first grade (PDF p. 11) and grew up among computer shops and bulletin boards; Lopez, "sponsored by what her parents could pull from the peripheral service systems of the university" (PDF p. 12), learned biliteracy. Brandt's own example of unequal sponsorship is a computing competence, in 1975–1995.
- **Closing line.** "The history of literacy is a catalogue of obligatory relations." (PDF p. 25.)
- **Bears on.** §2.5 (locating responsibility), §3 (all five), §4 (the whole table), §6.1.
- **Does it pre-empt the paper?** In part, and a literacy referee will say so. Brandt already has a named category for the parties who enable, regulate or withhold literacy and gain by it, and she already says that they set terms and extract compliance. The paper's "positions around a compulsory competence" is not new at that level of description. What Brandt does not do: (i) she works from learners' recollections of acquisition, not from institutions that made a literacy a condition of standing, so the test-giver, the court and the record-keeper do not appear as sponsors; (ii) nothing in the report treats a written record overriding what was said (§3.3) as a sponsor's act; (iii) she has no third party that reads two people and commits an outcome. Her sponsors stand between a learner and literacy, whereas the paper's intermediary stands between two people and an outcome. So the construct the paper needs (a position in a triad) is not hers, but the paper must say how its positions differ from sponsorship.
- **Referee expectation.** Yes. A composition or literacy-studies referee will ask where Brandt is. Must cite.

### 2. Brandt and Clinton, "Limits of the Local" (2002) — `brandt2002limits`

- **Reference.** Brandt, Deborah, and Katie Clinton. 2002. "Limits of the Local: Expanding Perspectives on Literacy as a Social Practice." *Journal of Literacy Research* 34(3): 337–356. DOI 10.1207/s15548430jlr3403_4 (Crossref-resolved, title matched).
- **Read depth.** **Metadata only** (SAGE, 403). Quoted at second hand from Street 2003, pp. 79–80, which I read.
- **What it says (via Street 2003).** The authors write that "Literacy practices are not typically invented by their practitioners. Nor are they independently chosen or sustained by them. Literacy in use more often than not serves multiple interests, incorporating individual agents and their locales into larger enterprises that play out away from the immediate scene." (Quoted in Street 2003, p. 79.) They fear that "seemingly local appropriations of literacy may in fact be culminations of literate designs originating elsewhere" (p. 79). Street accepts the caveat and refuses to call the distant literacies "autonomous": they are "no more autonomous than those of local literacies"; their "distantness, their relative power over local literacies and their "noninvented" character ... do not make them 'autonomous', only 'distant', 'new', or hegemonic" (Street 2003, p. 80).
- **Bears on.** §4 and §5.2. A platform's rules are "literate designs originating elsewhere" in this sense, and the worker's local practice does not set them.
- **Referee expectation.** Yes, with Brandt. Optional if space is short.

### 3. Street, autonomous and ideological models — `street1984literacy` (reused), `street2003whats`

- **Reference.** Street, Brian V. 1984. *Literacy in Theory and Practice*. Cambridge University Press. (Key reused from `references.bib`; no Crossref record for the book.) Street, Brian. 2003. "What's 'New' in New Literacy Studies? Critical Approaches to Literacy in Theory and Practice." *Current Issues in Comparative Education* 5(2): 77ff. (end page not verified). DOI 10.52214/cice.v5i2.11369 (Crossref-resolved, title matched; open access).
- **Read depth.** 1984 book: **not opened**; its claim reaches me through Street 2003, Horner 2013 and Vee 2013 (the book is cited at p. 97 in Vee). Street 2003: **read in part** (pp. 77–86, about two thirds; the policy section was skimmed).
- **The precise claim.** "The standard view in many fields, from schooling to development programs, works from the assumption that literacy in itself--autonomously--will have effects on other social and cognitive practices. Introducing literacy to poor, 'illiterate' people, villages, urban youth etc. will have the effect of enhancing their cognitive skills, improving their economic prospects, making them better citizens, regardless of the social and economic conditions that accounted for their 'illiteracy' in the first place. I refer to this as an 'autonomous' model of literacy. The model, I suggest, disguises the cultural and ideological assumptions that underpin it so that it can then be presented as though they are neutral and universal." (Street 2003, p. 77.) The alternative: "literacy is a social practice, not simply a technical and neutral skill; that it is always embedded in socially constructed epistemological principles" (p. 77). "It is not valid to suggest that 'literacy' can be 'given' neutrally and then its 'social' effects only experienced afterwards." (p. 78.)
- **Particular versions of literacy are partisan.** "particular versions of it are always 'ideological', they are always rooted in a particular world-view and in a desire for that view of literacy to dominate and to marginalize others" (p. 78).
- **From Vee (quoting Street 1984, p. 97).** "literacy, of course, is more than just the 'technology' in which it is manifest. No one material feature serves to define literacy itself. It is a social process, in which particular socially constructed technologies are used within particular institutional frameworks for specific social purposes." (Vee 2013, p. 55, citing Street.) Second-hand; verify against the book.
- **What it implies for "AI literacy".** The claim has three parts that bear on the paper: (i) a skills-first programme presents a partisan arrangement as neutral; (ii) it attributes the learner's later fortunes to the learner's skill, "regardless of the social and economic conditions"; (iii) the effects of a competence depend on the practices and institutions around it. Horner (entry 6) adds that the autonomous model offers literacy "as an ideologically neutral phenomenon—a gift to the unfortunate, who can thence be blamed for failing to make appropriate, grateful use of it". This is the same argument as the paper's thesis (education aimed at the user addresses the wrong party), reached from the other side. Street's lineage already contains it.
- **A cut against the paper.** Street's remedy is to study literacies as local social practices, with the learner's own purposes. The paper's remedy is a design obligation placed on the intermediary. The two are compatible but not the same, and a Street-trained referee will ask why the paper's "algorithmacy" (a competence "demanded" by a triad) is not itself an autonomous-model noun (see §c, point 5).
- **Bears on.** §2.1 (rebutting Kamperman and Graff: Street is the long-established source), §3.5, §5.1, §6.1.
- **Referee expectation.** Must cite. The paper's thesis is a version of Street's.

### 4. Vee, *Coding Literacy* (2017) and "Understanding Computer Programming as a Literacy" (2013) — `vee2017coding`, `vee2013understanding`, `byrd2019review`

- **Reference.** Vee, Annette. 2017. *Coding Literacy: How Computer Programming Is Changing Writing*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press. DOI 10.7551/mitpress/10655.001.0001 (Crossref-resolved, title matched). Vee, Annette. 2013. "Understanding Computer Programming as a Literacy." *Literacy in Composition Studies* 1(2): 42–64. DOI 10.21623/1.1.2.4 (Crossref-resolved; CC BY-NC-ND). Byrd, Antonio. 2019. Review of *Coding Literacy*. *Literacy in Composition Studies* 7(1): 114–116. DOI 10.21623/1.7.1.7.
- **Read depth.** The 2017 book: **metadata only** (Crossref gives chapter titles and pages; MIT Press pages 403; I found no open copy and no sign that the book is open access, contrary to the brief). The 2013 article: **read in part** (pp. 42–43, 48–51, 53–55; the rest keyword-searched, 10.8k words in all). The Byrd review: **read in full**.
- **Which chapters exist.** From Crossref: Introduction "Computer Programming as Literacy" (pp. 1–42); "Coding for Everyone and the Legacy of Mass Literacy" (pp. 43–94); "Sociomaterialities of Programming and Writing"; "Literacy for Everyday Life" (pp. 179–214); Conclusion "Promoting Coding Literacy—Lessons from Reading and Writing" (pp. 215–226). Byrd gives the argument by chapter: ch. 1 "recalls a rhetorical history of campaigns for universal reading and writing and extends that timeline into the mass programming movements of the 20th and 21st centuries", showing that "programming promotion since the 1960s has used rhetoric and ideologies similar to literacy campaigns: economic productivity, individual empowerment, and creative expression"; chs. 3–4 trace how writing moved from "a technology that government and church institutions used to manage the overflow of information to the foundation for individuals' everyday life" and argue that this history "can be a model" for coding (Byrd 2019, pp. 114–115).
- **What the 2013 article does.** It takes the parallel from van Rossum's 1999 DARPA application, which Vee reports Cynthia Selfe as calling "rhetorically opportunistic because it is a trigger for funding" (p. 43), and then argues that it is "more than just rhetorical flourish" (p. 43). Its history has two transitions: medieval England (Clanchy, Stock), when texts "became central to people's lives because they aided developing institutions—government bureaucracy, written contract law and the enterprise of publishing—to scale up", and the long nineteenth century, when "institutions such as the postal service, written tax bills, public signage and mass education were built on the assumption that a majority of citizens could read and write" (p. 49). In the first, "those who could not read text began to be recast as 'illiterate' and power began to shift towards those who could" (p. 51). Her conclusion for code: "In computation as well as text, the illiterate person is 'less the maker of his destiny than the literate person' ... As more communication, social organization, government functions and commerce are being conducted through code—and as computational literacy becomes more infrastructural—the power balance is once again shifting toward those who are skilled in this new literacy technology." (p. 55.) She cites Graff only to caution that possessing literacy does not by itself lift people out of low incomes, and then argues that illiteracy still impedes a person in an infrastructural world (p. 54). She also notes that software built for consumption leaves the design to "the programmers and software designers (or the companies they work for)" who "still call the shots" (p. 49).
- **Verdict on pre-emption.** Partly, and in a way the paper has to meet. Vee (a) draws the same comparison, from the mass-literacy campaigns and the move of texts into institutions to a computing competence; (b) already says the campaign rhetoric is reused, which pre-empts the paper's §3.5 and the "Promise" row for programming; (c) names the institutional dependence on writing as the mechanism by which illiteracy was created and power moved. She differs in three ways that matter. She argues that programming *is* a literacy and that literacy educators should teach it (2013, pp. 55–56); the paper argues that the literacy frame points at the wrong party. She reads the shift of power as toward the skilled (p. 55), though she also notes that the programmers and software designers "(or the companies they work for) still call the shots" (p. 49); the paper reads the shift as toward whoever holds the test, access and record, and these can be people who cannot be reached by acquiring the skill. She notes that "land laws had begun to favor written contracts over personal witnesses" in the late 13th century (p. 50), which is the paper's §3.3 in outline, but she does not (in what I read) treat that or the other practices as abuses, and I found nothing on the 1901 Alabama convention, the slave codes, treaty texts or the Stationers. None of that is shown for the book, which I could not open. So: Vee does not pre-empt the five-practice mapping or the argument about positions, but she does pre-empt the claim that the historical comparison for a technical competence is new, and the paper cannot say "the parallel is already established but only as an overpromised skill" (Intro, ¶1) without addressing her: her parallel is institutional, not only about promises.
- **Bears on.** §2.1 (Kamperman and Graff are not the only critics of the metaphor, and Vee is its defender), §2.2 (her two transitions are a rival periodisation), §3.5 and §4 (campaign rhetoric), §5.1.
- **Referee expectation.** Yes, without doubt. The paper's key comparison is hers. Must cite and must respond. The book needs a library copy before the response is written.

### 5. Graff, "The Legacies of Literacy Studies" (2013) — `graff2013legacies`

- **Reference.** Graff, Harvey. 2013. "The Legacies of Literacy Studies." *Literacy in Composition Studies* 1(1): 15–17. DOI 10.21623/1.1.1.4 (Crossref-resolved).
- **Read depth.** **Read in part** (pp. 15–16 of three).

- **Referee expectation.** Optional but cheap, and it fits the Graff material the paper already uses.

### 6. Horner, "Ideologies of Literacy, 'Academic Literacies,' and Composition Studies" (2013) — `horner2013ideologies`

- **Reference.** Horner, Bruce. 2013. *Literacy in Composition Studies* 1(1): 1–9. DOI 10.21623/1.1.1.2 (Crossref-resolved).
- **Read depth.** **Read in part** (pp. 1–2 and keyword search of the rest).
- **What it says.** Lea and Street's work, in Horner's account, shows the autonomous model to be "ideological not only in the sense of being at variance with the facts on the ground ... but also in the sense of working in the interests of some and against the interests of others—for example, those whose literacy is refused recognition. In other words, the autonomous model is powerful in claiming an autonomy for literacy that hides its ideological character, purporting to offer literacy as an ideologically neutral phenomenon—a gift to the unfortunate, who can thence be blamed for failing to make appropriate, grateful use of it to improve themselves." (p. 2.)
- **Bears on.** §3.5 and §6.1. It is the most compact statement I found of the move the paper makes from "teach the user" to "address the institution", stated for literacy and with the "blame the learner" step in it. Quote it.
- **Referee expectation.** Optional, but a strong supporting citation.

### 7. Vieira, "On the Social Consequences of Literacy" (2013) and *American by Paper* (2016) — `vieira2013social`, `vieira2016american`

### 8. Prendergast, "The Economy of Literacy" (2002) and *Literacy and Racial Justice* (2003) — `prendergast2002economy`, `prendergast2003literacy`; Kynard 2013 — `kynard2013literacy`

- **Reference.** Prendergast, Catherine. 2002. "The Economy of Literacy: How the Supreme Court Stalled the Civil Rights Movement." *Harvard Educational Review* 72(2): 206–230. DOI 10.17763/haer.72.2.l8112t70x6klx6j0 (Crossref-resolved, title matched). Prendergast, Catherine. 2003. *Literacy and Racial Justice: The Politics of Learning after Brown v. Board of Education*. - **Referee expectation.** Yes, in a composition or critical literacy venue. Must cite, after the book is read.

### 9. Stuckey, *The Violence of Literacy* (1991) — `stuckey1991violence`

## (c) Synthesis by question

### 1. Is there an established concept for the parties who enable, regulate or withhold literacy and gain by it?

Yes: Brandt's "sponsors of literacy" (1998; 2001). The definition names enabling, supporting, teaching and modelling, and also recruiting, regulating, suppressing and withholding, and it adds that sponsors "gain advantage by it in some way" (report, PDF p. 6). It says sponsors' and learners' interests may conflict and that sponsors set terms and exact compliance. It does not pre-empt the thesis, because it describes the supply of literacy to a learner and does not describe a gate, a record or a third party that commits an outcome. It does pre-empt the claim that nobody in literacy studies had named the positions. The paper has two honest moves: adopt "sponsor" for §3.2 and §3.4 (withholding and monopoly) and say what §3.1, §3.3 and §4's triad add (an adjudicative position and a recording position). Young (2013, p. 10) and Kynard (2013, p. 63) show the term is now ordinary in the field.

### 2. Street's models and "AI literacy" as a neutral skill

Street's claim is that a model which treats literacy as a technical, neutral skill whose effects follow "regardless of the social and economic conditions" (2003, p. 77) hides a partisan arrangement behind a claim to neutrality. For "AI literacy", the implication is direct and does not need the history: an AI-literacy programme presents a skill as neutral while the arrangement that gives the skill its value is the platform's. The paper's thesis and Street's agree. What the paper adds is the five documented practices and a design remedy. It should give Street the credit, since Kamperman and Graff are not the first to say it.

### 3. Literacy as violence, property or gatekeeping

Opened: Kynard (in full), Vieira (in part), Horner (in part), Young (in part). Abstract only: Prendergast 2002. Not opened: Stuckey, Prendergast 2003, Bourdieu, Luke, Collins and Blot, and Graff's later books. What I can report: Prendergast's "white property" is, on the evidence of Kynard and the HER abstract, an argument from critical race theory (property in whiteness) that the right to literacy is rhetorical; Stuckey's, on the publisher's own description, is that the literacy profession perpetuates injustice. Neither is opened, so neither can be quoted in the paper from this facet.

### 4. Histories of mass literacy

I did not open Vincent, Kaestle, Cipolla, Stone or Furet and Ozouf. Vee's two transitions (2013, pp. 49–51) are the only history I read that is aimed at the paper's comparison. The historians' point that matters for §2.6 comes from Brandt: Protestant Sunday Schools offered "basic reading instruction to working-class families as part of evangelical duty" and "these families insistently, sometimes riotously demanded of their Sunday Schools more instruction, including in writing and math, because it provided means for upward mobility" (report, PDF p. 8; Brandt's source for this is not given in what I read). That is a sponsor's offer being taken for the learner's own ends, and it belongs next to Johansson and Washington in §2.6.

### 5. "New literacies", digital literacy and its critics

Vee is the main case (entry 4). Weninger and LaDousa (abstract only) apply the ideological model to digital literacy. boyd (read in part) argues the individualising move for media literacy. Graff (2013) says "Literacy" and "Literacy Studies" "at times become promotional labels". I found no work that applies Street's autonomous model to *AI* literacy, but the search was shallow (one Crossref query in Z, one web search here), so this is "I found none", not "none exists".

### 6. What a literacy scholar would say the outline gets wrong or leaves out

1. **Sponsorship is missing.** The outline's "positions" are Brandt's sponsors under another name, partly. A referee will say so first.
2. **The ratchet.** Brandt says sponsors contribute to "the literacy crisis" by raising standards (report, PDF p. 10), and Vee (2013, p. 49) says that easier technologies put "more pressure on individual literacy, ratcheting up the level of skill needed for one to be considered 'literate'". Vee takes the ratchet from Brandt (she cites "diSessa; Brandt" at p. 49), so the two are one source, not two. Both bear on §3.5 and §4 (AI literacy as a moving target) and the outline has nothing like it.
3. **"Illiterate" is a status that institutions assign.** §3.3 describes "illiterate counter-parties". Vee (p. 51) says that when texts became infrastructural, those who could not read "began to be recast as 'illiterate'". Street's school would say the Mi'kmaq signatories had their own record-keeping and were not deficient. The outline should say "non-literate in the instrument's script" or "oral" and not slip into the autonomous model's binary.
4. **The test measures the tester.** Horner (p. 2) and Street (p. 78): partisan versions of literacy aim to "dominate and to marginalize others". §3.1 should say that the registrar's passage was a particular literacy.
5. **"Algorithmacy" is itself a "-acy" noun.** The outline defines it as a competence demanded by a triad (§2.1). Graff's "lure of literacy" (2013, p. 15) and Street's account of the autonomous model both warn against the move. The reply is probably in §2.1's own wording ("relational competence", "institutional positions"), but a Street-trained referee will want it said that the term names a demand placed on people by an arrangement, not a skill people lack.
6. **Local against distant.** Brandt and Clinton: practices serve "larger enterprises that play out away from the immediate scene". This supports §5.2 and §4, and is missing.
7. **Documents and standing.** Vieira on papers and the citizenship test. The outline reaches documents only through treaties.
8. **Who sponsors "AI literacy"?** Brandt's report treats commercial sponsors as central ("commercial sponsorship abounded", PDF p. 8). The outline's "Promise" row could ask who sells and funds AI literacy. I have not searched for this.
9. **Literacy studies' own conflict.** Kynard (p. 64) says the field's founding ethnography so "totally eclipsed discussions of race" at the time of desegregation (the chapter-three argument about Heath is on p. 63). If §2.1 leans on Heath or on "multiple literacies" it should know the critique.

### (iii) Works that cut against the paper, and what they force it to concede

- **Brandt.** The positions have a name and an author; the paper cannot present "who gains a position over learners" as unnamed. Concession: sponsorship is the general frame; the paper's five practices are cases of it, plus an adjudicative and a recording position.
- **Vee.** The comparison is not new for computing, and her version is institutional and favours teaching the skill. Concession in the Introduction: the sentence "current critiques treat it as little more than an overpromised skill" is too strong if Vee's institutional history is counted, and she is the sort of reader who will say so. The paper's reply is that she sees the shift of power as toward the skilled, the paper as toward the position-holders.
- **Street and Brandt and Clinton.** Street warns that treating the "distant" literacies as autonomous concedes too much; the paper should say its triad is an ideological arrangement.
- **Scribner and Cole** (abstract only). Their separation of literacy from schooling would be a ground for a referee to say §3.6 (schooling) and §2.6 (value of learning) are not about literacy at all, which fits T's finding that "imposing" fails as a literacy wrong.
- **Prendergast, Stuckey.** If literacy is rhetorical "right" or violent in itself, §2.6's guardrail ("real value of historical literacy") needs a better defence than "people pursued it".
