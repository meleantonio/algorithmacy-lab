# Facet N1: republican domination, in political theory and in business ethics (2026-10-14)

Facet N1, Round 6, 2026-10-14. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.

## What this facet covers

The paper borrows one concept from republican political theory, domination, and uses it twice: in §2.2 to describe the positions (the gate, the withheld competence, the record) and in §5.1 to say what the operator owes ("rules that are common knowledge, and a share of control for those subject to them"). I checked three things. First, whether the manuscript's attributions to Pettit and Lovett match the texts. Second, what the republican literature on work, platforms and algorithms already says, since a BEQ referee in this area will know it. Third, what the critics of domination as a frame say. M4 already covered Lovett's SEP entry, Muldoon and Raekstad, Hoeksema and the Gädeke abstract; I reopened each of those and went further.

## 1. The manuscript's attributions, checked against the texts

This section comes first because it changes sentences in the draft. Each finding gives the manuscript's sentence, what the source says, and where.

**1a. The definition is Pettit 1999, not Pettit 1997.** §2.2 reads: "On Pettit's account, a person is free to the extent that no one has 'the capacity to interfere in their affairs on an arbitrary basis' (Pettit, 1997, as quoted in Lovett, 2026)". Lovett's entry gives the source of that wording as "(1999, 165; cf. Pettit 1997, 2001, 2012, 2014)" (SEP §1.2), and its bibliography identifies 1999 as "Republican Freedom and Contestatory Democratization," in Shapiro and Hacker-Cordón (eds.), *Democracy's Value*, pp. 163–190. The 1997 book words the definition differently. Two independent sources quote it identically from p. 52: someone dominates another "to the extent that 1. they have the capacity to interfere 2. on an arbitrary basis 3. in certain choices that the other is in a position to make" (O'Shea 2019, p. 3, n. 8, "Pettit, Republicanism, 52"; Gädeke 2020, p. 201, n. 3, "Pettit 1997b, p. 52"). *Correction for the author to decide:* either "(Pettit, 1999, p. 165, as quoted in Lovett, 2026)", with `pettit1999republican` added to the references, or the 1997 p. 52 wording, checked against the book. I have not opened either Pettit text.

**1b. "Procedurally or by the absence of control" misstates Lovett.** §2.2 says republicans "define arbitrary power procedurally or by the absence of control, not as unjust power". Lovett gives two rival definitions, procedural and democratic, and then says that "on either view, arbitrariness simply means uncontrolled and vice versa" (SEP §2.2). Absence of control is common to both; it is not the second option. The sentence would be accurate as "procedurally or democratically, and on either view not as unjust power."

**1c. §5.1 merges two rival accounts and assigns the merged result to "republican theory."** §5.1 reads: "Republican theory says what: rules that are common knowledge, and a share of control for those subject to them (Section 2.2)." In Lovett's entry these are alternatives. The procedural view, power "reliably controlled by effective rules, procedures, or goals that are common knowledge to all persons or groups concerned," is credited to "(Lovett 2010, 2022)". The democratic view, power "directly or indirectly controlled by the concerned persons or groups themselves," is illustrated from "Pettit (2012, 57–58)" and attributed to "Bohman 2008; Laborde 2008; Forst 2013; McCammon 2015" (SEP §2.2). Lovett's own article says his procedural account "diverges from the mainstream view among civic republicans, and from the view of Philip Pettit in particular" (Lovett 2012, abstract). So "common-knowledge rules" is Lovett's criterion, not Pettit's.

Pettit's own criterion, in the one Pettit text I could open, is tracking: "I control the interference of another so far as that interference is forced to track the interests that I am disposed to avow" (Pettit 2006, p. 136). His institutional remedy for discretionary power is contestation. Lovett reports three requirements "outlined by Pettit (1997, 186–7)": decision-makers "must be required to present reasons for their decisions"; contestation "must be equally open to all"; and "there exist institutionalized forums for contestation—impartial 'courts of appeal,' so to speak" (SEP §4.2). Lindblom quotes the same 1997 passages: authorities must "decide on the basis of suitable considerations and to make clear which considerations are moving them" (Pettit 1997, 188), and bodies "should be required to answer for how they behave" (Pettit 1997, 195–196) (Lindblom 2019, pp. 200–201).

*What follows for the draft.* The three duties fit Pettit's contestatory triple more closely than they fit the "common knowledge plus control" pair. The declared operating envelope is a basis for contestation. Counter-delegation is voice plus a forum, and its "independent party able to bind the operator" is Pettit's impartial court of appeal. The author can (a) keep the pair and say that the duties meet both rival accounts, attributing each correctly; or (b) ground the duties in Pettit's contestatory requirements, citing SEP §4.2. Option (b) also removes a second difficulty: on Pettit's account, private parties' power is made non-arbitrary by state regulation, and the shared control belongs to citizens over the state, not to users over a firm (Pettit 2006, p. 146; see entry below). A "share of control for those subject to" an operator is closer to the workplace-democracy wing (González-Ricoy 2014; Breen 2017) than to Pettit.

**1d. Two attributions that hold.** "Just because one is better able to cope with arbitrary power, it does not follow that one's domination is any less" is verbatim in SEP §2.2. The "slave of a kindly master" point is Pettit's own: "the slave of the rich and kindly master may enjoy an enormous range of choice without having any social freedom whatsoever" (Pettit 2006, p. 134). The §5.3 gloss ("predictability does not end domination if the party who declared the rule can redeclare it at will") is consistent with Lovett's requirement that procedural constraints "must be resilient over a wide range of possible changes or modifications in the relevant circumstances" (SEP §2.2); citing that sentence would make the gloss Lovett's rather than the author's.

**1e. Muldoon and Raekstad quotations verified.** "workers' ability both to understand and contest the decisions that are made over them" is at p. 596, and "Workers have had their accounts deleted by an algorithm without warning and with no right to contest the decision" at p. 599, as the draft has them. The draft's "They locate the dominating agent in owners and managers" is accurate (p. 594). But see the entry below on what they say about customers.

## 2. Pettit and Lovett: the primary texts

**Pettit (2006), "Freedom in the Market," `pettit2006freedom`. Part** (pp. 131–143, 146; the author's own copy with journal pagination). The only Pettit primary text I could open. Five passages bear on the paper.

- *Interference includes misinformation.* Interference may mean "misleading the agent about the options available. Misinformation can be a very effective way of rendering the choice of an option effectively impossible or difficult" (p. 135). This supports §4 row 2 (the withheld competence) in Pettit's own terms.
- *Historical consent does not control interference.* The consent view "is not very plausible, however, since it may have been a past, now very alien, self that consented to a form of interference under which I now bristle; that is one reason why we naturally object to the slave contract" (p. 135). Supports §5.6, "The users consented."
- *Control as tracking* (p. 136), quoted in 1c.
- *Coping does not remove control.* Dominated subjects' "self-censorship and self-ingratiation" are measures that "will not remove the control; at best they will help to make it more palatable" (p. 137). This is the primary-text version of the line the draft takes from Lovett in §2.2.
- *Exit, under idealized conditions.* Under "extremely idealistic assumptions," "There is no particular threat to people's freedom as non-domination associated with participation in the market"; in a well-functioning labor market "no one would depend on any particular master and so no one would be at the mercy of a master: he or she could move on to employment elsewhere in the event of suffering arbitrary interference" (p. 142).
- *Private power is regulated by the state.* "Those who can access arbitrary interference will dominate those who are vulnerable to interference, and they will dominate them whether or not that access is ever activated" (p. 146). State regulation is non-arbitrary "so far as it is subject to a suitable degree of control by the interests that members of the community are disposed to avow as common" (p. 146).

*Bearing:* §2.2 (supports, and supplies primary wording for the standing capacity and the kindly master); §5.2 (*sharpens*: Pettit's exit argument assumes no one depends on "any particular master." The shared screening model is a single master behind many employers, so the paper can turn Pettit's own condition against the exit reply); §5.5 (*supports*: Pettit's route to non-domination in private relations runs through regulation, which is where §5.5 says the duties will be discharged).

**Pettit (1997), `pettit1997republicanism` (reused). Record.** Known through three secondary sources, each with page locators: the p. 52 definition (O'Shea; Gädeke); p. 62, "Whether a relationship sprang originally from a contract or not, whether or not it was consensual in origin, the fact that it gives one party the effective capacity to interfere more or less arbitrarily in some other's choices means that one person dominates or subjugates the other" (as quoted in Lindblom 2019, p. 199); pp. 185–196 on contestability (Lindblom, pp. 199–201; SEP §4.2). The p. 62 sentence is the strongest available support for §5.6's "a person may consent to an arrangement and be dominated within it," and it should be checked in the book and cited directly.

**Pettit (1999), `pettit1999republican`. Record** (SEP bibliography). The source of the definition the draft quotes (see 1a).

**Pettit (2012), `pettit2012peoples` (reused). Abstract** (publisher's description via OpenAlex). Its thesis is that state coercion "will not involve individual subjection or domination insofar as we enjoy an equally shared form of control over those in power". Secondary sources quote it: domination as "exposure to another's power of uncontrolled interference" (p. 28, as quoted in O'Shea 2019, p. 3); interference by penalty, which "replaces it with a choice between X, Y and Z-minus" (pp. 51–52, as quoted in Muldoon and Raekstad 2023, p. 590). *Bearing:* the "share of control" in §5.1 is this book's claim about citizens and the state.

**Pettit (2025), "Employment without Domination," `pettit2025employment`. Record** (SSRN). Pettit's most recent statement on work. Not read; a referee may expect it if it is published before submission.

**Lovett, SEP "Republicanism," `lovett2026republicanism` (reused). Part** (§§1.2–2.2, 4.1–4.2, bibliography; revision of 12 August 2026). Beyond 1a–1d: the entry's summary of the program, "not to make our master a better person ... but to render him less of a master" (§2.1), is a compact warrant for the paper's preference for structural duties over operator goodwill. On work it says "in order to prevent the domination workers by their employers, many advocate stronger unions and workplace democracy (Gourevitch 2015)" (§4.1; the missing "of" is in the original), and that markets "when well-ordered might reduce it (Taylor 2017)" (§4.1).

**Lovett (2010), `lovett2010general`. Abstract.** Domination is "a condition experienced by persons or groups to the extent that they are dependent on a social relationship in which some other person or group wields arbitrary power over them"; justice requires "minimizing avoidable domination". *Bearing:* the dependency clause matters for the gate (§3.1, §5.2): a position dominates in proportion to how far the affected party cannot do without the relationship.

**Lovett (2012), `lovett2012what`. Abstract.** Quoted in 1c. *Bearing:* the citation that shows the procedural view is Lovett's own.

**Lovett and Pettit (2009), `lovett2009neorepublicanism`. Abstract.** The standard short citation for the research program: "the conception of a free person as one who does not live under the arbitrary will or domination of others". Optional.

## 3. Republican theory of work and platforms

**Muldoon and Raekstad (2023), `muldoon2023algorithmic` (reused). Part** (pp. 587–601). Beyond what the draft cites, three passages matter.

1. *Definition.* "A is dominated by B if and only if B can interfere with A's choices in ways that A cannot control" (p. 590).
2. *The customer already appears.* Workers "remain subject to the uncontrolled power of consumers and bosses in terms of how they are assessed, rewarded, and punished"; bosses establish "regimes in which they are subject to the arbitrary whims of customers in ways that workers cannot control"; one driver: "you are at the mercy of random people" (p. 598, quoting Lee et al.). These powers "do not inhere in the algorithms in isolation" but "in the socially produced and reproduced corporate entity" (p. 598).
3. *Self-regulation is no remedy.* "Corporate promises to 'do better' through self-regulation are implausible solutions for uncontrolled powers", because acting better voluntarily "amounts to a claim about their intentions to use their uncontrolled power in better ways, not to actions that would remove their uncontrolled power" (p. 600). Their remedy is "democratic ownership and control of productive assets" (p. 601).

*Bearing:* §2.2 *challenges* the sentence "I add the second party." Muldoon and Raekstad already put the second party (the customer) in the picture, as a dominator of the worker whose power the platform builds. What the draft adds is narrower: the intermediary's reading also binds the customer, employer or lender, so neither flank can check it. The sentence should say so. Point 3 *supports* §5.5 ("The duties are worth what their enforcement is worth"). Point 3's ownership remedy is the position Frye argues against (below); the paper's operator duties sit with Frye, and §6 could say so.

**Lindblom (2019), BEQ, `lindblom2019consent`. Part** (pp. 189–191, 199–206). The BEQ precedent the paper most needs. Lindblom argues that employment contracts are incomplete, so "consent to authority has been given under ignorance, and, therefore, ... authority cannot be justified by consent" (abstract, p. 189). Pettit's contestability fills the gap: "It demands transparency, channels for voice, and a forum where contestations can be evaluated" (p. 189). He uses contestability without "the ideal of non-domination as a premise" (p. 200). The separation of powers then requires the forum to stand outside the employer: "Therefore, we should choose to design the checks on employer power outside the domain of the firm" (p. 206).

*Bearing:* §5.4 *already says part of it.* Lindblom's argument for unions over company unions is the argument for counter-delegation's independent party, made in BEQ seven years earlier for employers. §5.6 (*supports*: consent cannot justify acts of authority under incomplete contracts, which describes terms of service that the platform may change). §5.1 (*sharpens*: contestability is the Pettit-derived mechanism the duties actually instantiate; see 1c).

**O'Shea (2019), `oshea2019are`. Part** (pp. 1–6). The standard source for "are workers dominated?" Two moves bear on §2.2's second clarification. On consent: "workers do not possess immunity from the arbitrary power of employers simply by virtue of having agreed to work for them" (p. 5). On law: "Employer power does not, however, have to be entirely unchecked to be arbitrary" (p. 5); legal protections "inevitably leave unregulated a significant subset of the powers of employers" (p. 5). His alternative definition is "the arbitrary power to determine access to capabilities necessary for relationships of equality between citizens" (abstract). *Bearing:* §2.2 *supports* "the concept does not require that the holder be unconstrained in every respect" (O'Shea says it in the workplace case); §5.6 *supports*.

**Frye (2024), `frye2024domination`. Part** (pp. 373–377, 383–386). A critic of the radical wing. "Republicans of all stripes agree that domination at work is a problem. Disagreement mostly surrounds solutions" (p. 373). Against locating domination in ownership, he locates it in "managerial discretion" (abstract, p. 373): "Whether or not firms are privately owned, worker-owned, publicly owned, or owned by no one, managers will retain authority over subordinate workers" (p. 386). He grants that "a formal right of exit is insufficient to check capitalists' power" but holds that exit and competing employers are "an admittedly imperfect check," citing "Pettit 2006, 142" (p. 376). *Bearing:* §2.2 and §5.1 *support* the paper's choice to describe positions and assign duties to whoever holds them, rather than to change ownership. §5.2 *sharpens*: Frye's "imperfect check" through competing employers is exactly what a shared screening model removes.

**Gädeke (2020), `gadeke2020mugger` (reused; upgraded from Abstract to Part, pp. 199–207, Utrecht publisher copy).** Two passages. Domination "is not itself an action. ... Rather, it is a position of power. Whether I welcome the privileges attached to it or resent them, I dominate those subjected to my power simply in virtue of being in this social position" (p. 207). And domination structures involve a "triadic relationship between dominator, dominated, and what Wartenberg calls peripheral agents," who "sustain and reproduce aligned social practices that constitute the power of the dominator" (p. 207, citing Wartenberg 1990, pp. 144–5). *Bearing:* §2.2 *supports* "domination as the description of a position" with a named source; §2.1 *challenges* the novelty of "the triad." Republican theory already has a triad, and a referee will raise it. The paper's triad differs: its third party stands between two parties and binds both, where Wartenberg's peripheral agents sustain one party's power over another. One sentence in §2.2 should mark the difference. M4's note that "existing domination theory is dyadic" is wrong on this point.

**Gädeke (2021), `gadeke2021who`. Part** (abstract; pp. 187–189). Responsibility for domination. She finds Young's model "a promising basis" but says it "fails to fully avoid the problems of the other two models": it is unclear "what exactly a 'social connection' to injustice amounts to" (p. 188). Her own account: "Dominators, dominated and peripheral agents share political responsibility for domination in virtue of reproducing domination by occupying a position within structures of dominating power" (abstract, p. 180). *Bearing:* §5.1 *sharpens* the Young sentence. Gädeke gives the paper a domination-specific account of who else must act (employers that adopt a vendor, lenders that buy a score) without relying on Young's contested "connection."

**Claassen (2024), `claassen2024wealth`. Part** (PMC author manuscript, no pagination). Corporations owe two fiduciary duties to society, wealth creation and "a second fiduciary duty, i.e. not to dominate others in the economy" (abstract). On the multi-party problem: "the political literature on fiduciary relations argues partiality is not at all a necessary feature. Fiduciaries can have fiduciary relations to multiple beneficiaries, who have competing interests. In these cases, they have a duty of 'reasonableness' and 'fairness' in balancing their interests (Fox-Decent, 2011, pp. 34–37)" (section on construals of fiduciary duty). *Bearing:* §5.1 *challenges* "an intermediary between a driver and a rider cannot be loyal to both in the fiduciary's sense." Claassen reports a literature in which fiduciaries serve several beneficiaries with competing interests, and he builds a non-domination duty into corporate fiduciary law. The draft's notary analogy (impartiality toward two parties) is close to Claassen's "reasonableness and fairness"; the sentence should engage him, not dismiss the fiduciary route on loyalty grounds alone.

**Bhargava and Young (2022), BEQ, `bhargava2022ethics`. Part** (pp. 519–526). They grant the republican critique of employment-at-will (Anderson: power "often arbitrary and unaccountable," p. 525) and argue that its concerns "can be mitigated through public policy measures that do not require eliminating EAW" (abstract, p. 519), depending on the jurisdiction's complementary institutions. They quote Anderson (2015, p. 110): "it is the state that establishes the default constitution of workplace governance" (p. 526). *Bearing:* §5.6 "The law already provides" (*sharpens*: BEQ's own literature treats domination remedies as jurisdiction-dependent complements, which supports the paper's claim that EU rules are fragments needing a common rationale). Should cite.

**Ruehle (2023), BEQ, `ruehle2023moral`. Part** (abstract; section on domination). Digital workplace nudges "can expose employees to arbitrary domination"; organizations should "ensure that employees can challenge their implementation whenever necessary through legitimation procedures" (abstract, p. 502). She defines domination through Pettit (2012, 50) and Forst (2014, 7). *Bearing:* §5.4, *supports* (a BEQ article requiring a contestation route for digital influence at work). Optional.

**Hoeksema (2025), `hoeksema2025republican`. Part** (ch. 1 §1.2; ch. 2 §§2.4.2, 2.5.2, 2.6). The dissertation reprints `hoeksema2023digital` as chapter 2 with page numbers, which upgrades M4's fetch-tool reading. On algorithms: agents with "an uncontrolled capacity to use automated profiling" can "dominate individuals in pervasive ways" (p. 58, after Gräf 2017). On exit: "republicans cannot rely on the idea of exit options or of rationally consenting citizens"; "there is little place for reliance on 'notice-and-consent' mechanisms or other exit-strategy based responses" (p. 68). He also notes that domination "is generally thought of as being 'common knowledge' (Pettit 1997, 60)" (p. 64), a different use of "common knowledge" from §5.1's. *Bearing:* §5.2 and §5.6, *support*. Cite the dissertation pages or the 2023 article, not both.

**Umbers (2023), `umbers2023workplace`. Part** (pp. 1417, 1427). Unions "afford workers a form of partial protection from domination" (p. 1417). Gig workers are dominated through dependence rather than managerial authority: dependence "affords such platforms an enormous amount of arbitrary power over their 'workforce'" (p. 1427). *Bearing:* §4.1, *supports*. Optional.

**Bryan (2023), `bryan2023structural`. Part** (abstract and opening, p. 692). "I clarify what it is to have reasonable alternatives in the labor market but show that holding such options is insufficient to avoid structural domination" (p. 692). *Bearing:* §5.2 ("ten exits and one examiner"), *supports*. Optional.

**Workplace-republican canon, Abstract only.** Hsieh (2005), `hsieh2005rawlsian`: justice as fairness "grounds worker participation in the governance of economic enterprises more readily than might have been held in the past, but not necessarily in the form of workplace democracy" (OpenAlex opening paragraph). González-Ricoy (2014), `gonzalezricoy2014republican`: "costless exit is neither possible nor desirable in either perfect or imperfect labor markets, and ... managerial discretion is both desirable and inevitable due to the incompleteness of employment contracts" (abstract). Breen (2015), `breen2015freedom`: non-domination is "insufficient" without autonomy (abstract). Breen (2017), `breen2017non`: Pettit and Lovett hold that "free market exchange backed up with an effective right of exit for employees secured through unconditional basic income policies is sufficient to counter employer domination"; workplace republicans reply that "tight regulation of workplace practices and the institutionalization of worker voice and democratic control within firms" are required (abstract). Kwok (2026), `kwok2026five`: workplace democracy is epistemically superior to workplace republicanism (abstract). *Bearing:* §2.2's "the concept has already reached the workplace" names only Muldoon and Raekstad; a BEQ referee will expect Hsieh, Anderson and González-Ricoy there, and Breen (2017) is the clearest statement that the neo-republican answer to employer power is exit, which §5.2 contests.

**Anderson (2017), `anderson2017private` (reused), and Anderson (2015), `anderson2015equality`. Record.** Known through Bhargava and Young (pp. 525–526) and Frye (p. 373, citing Anderson 2017, 65–70 for worker control). Not opened. The draft does not cite Anderson in §2.2; a BEQ referee will expect *Private Government* there.

**Taylor (2017), *Exit Left*, `taylor2017exit`. Abstract.** "By promoting open and competitive markets and providing the information and financial resources necessary to enable exit, we can empower people's voices and offer them an escape from abuse and exploitation" (publisher abstract). *Bearing:* §5.2, *challenges*. "Exit protects no one when every door leads to the same reader" answers Hirschman, but the republican case for exit is Taylor's and Pettit's (2006, p. 142), and Lovett's entry cites Taylor for markets that reduce domination. The paragraph should name them and say why a shared reader defeats their condition.

## 4. Algorithmic and digital domination

**Sparrow (2026), `sparrow2026slaves`. Abstract.** On government use of AI: "decisions made using AI will often be impossible for citizens to contest because the reasons for the decisions will be inscrutable"; the inability to contest renders them "arbitrary and inimical to liberty on a republican account"; the remedy is to "ensure that decisions made by AI can be publicly contested" (abstract). *Bearing:* §5.4, *supports*, and §4.3 (opacity). It concerns the state, not a private intermediary. Should cite once.

**Maas (2022), `maas2022neo`. Abstract.** AI ethics struggles to build accountability because of "the predominant focus on Mill's harm's principle, rooted in the conception of freedom as non-interference. A strong focus on harm overcasts other moral wrongs, such as potentially problematic power dynamics between those who shape the system and those affected by it" (abstract). *Bearing:* §1 and §5.1, *already says part of it.* The introduction's move from non-maleficence (Jobin et al.) to the positions is Maas's move from harm to domination. Should cite in §1 or §5.1. (M4's `maas2023machine` is a different paper.)

**Aytaç (2024), `aytac2024digital`. Abstract.** Social media companies "can arbitrarily interfere with the choices individuals make qua citizens," and "algorithmic governance entails the capacity to interfere with citizens' choices about what mode of discursive engagement they endorse" (abstract). Optional; the platform-as-public-sphere case is not the paper's triad. (M4's `aytac2024big` is a different article.)

**Christiaens (2025), `christiaens2025platform`, and Hickson (2024), `hickson2024freedom`. Abstract.** Christiaens: unionisation and exit "come with unintended side-effects"; worker-owned cooperative platforms are the republican answer. Hickson: gig workers' "structural vulnerability ... exposes them to extraordinary forms of domination"; the remedy is to "(re)build the rights and protections available to workers". *Bearing:* §5.5; both show the range of remedies (ownership vs. rights) the paper should locate itself in. Optional.

**Danaher (2020), `danaher2020freedom`, and Maynor (2025), `maynor2025flood`. Abstract.** Danaher: algocracy can be "both emancipatory and enslaving"; Hoeksema reports his term "algorithmic masters" (Hoeksema 2025, p. 59). Maynor: regulate platforms "to help protect users from arbitrary interference and empower them to fight back" (abstract). Optional.

## 5. Critics of domination as a frame

**Simpson (2017), `simpson2017impossibility`. Record.** Known only through the reply. Lovett and Pettit summarize his charge: republicanism requires both robust legal protection and popular control of the state, and these are "inconsistent insofar as the ability of the people to control the state entails that they have dominating control over every individual"; more generally, "domination is inescapable" because any set of others "working as a team, could collectively interfere with us" (Lovett and Pettit 2018, abstract, `lovett2018preserving`). Simpson's rejoinder is Simpson (2019), *PPA* 47(4), 412–424 (Crossref record only; not in the bib). *Bearing:* §6, optional. It bears on any use of the democratic "share of control" view in §5.1.

**Kramer (2008), `kramer2008liberty`. Record** (SEP bibliography). Lovett lists him among those who hold that non-domination is "really just another (more obscure) way of talking about security of non-interference (Goodin 2003; Carter 2008; Kramer 2008; Moen 2024)" (SEP §2.1). *Bearing:* the standard liberal objection. If a referee presses it, the answer is SEP §2.1 and Pettit (2006, pp. 136–137): the kindly master lowers expected interference without lowering domination.

**McCammon (2015), `mccammon2015domination`. Abstract.** Power is arbitrary as a function of "its 'deliberative isolation': that it is checked only by the empowered person's (or persons') sense of relevant practical reasons" (abstract). Frye adopts a version of this (p. 373). *Bearing:* §2.2, optional. It gives a third definition of arbitrariness that fits the registrar who "alone judges the answer."

**Thompson (2018), `thompson2018two`. Abstract.** A radical critique of neo-republicanism: "extractive dominance" and "constitutive domination where the norms, institutions, and values of the community shape the rationality of subjects to accept forms of power" (abstract). This is EJPT, not BEQ (the brief queried BEQ). Optional; it bears on §3.5 (the promise), where literacy campaigns shaped what people took to be their own deficit.

## 7. Synthesis

Three findings change the paper. First, the attributions in §2.2 and §5.1 need repair. The definition is quoted from Pettit 1999, not 1997; "common-knowledge rules" is Lovett's procedural criterion, which Lovett says departs from Pettit; and §5.1 presents the two rival accounts as a joint requirement of "republican theory." Pettit's own remedy is contestation (reasons, inclusive voice, an impartial forum), and the three duties fit it better than they fit the pair the draft names. Second, BEQ has already printed the argument for counter-delegation's independent forum in the employment case (Lindblom 2019), and a referee will expect the paper to build on it. Third, two of the paper's distinctive claims need narrowing against named work: "I add the second party" (Muldoon and Raekstad already include the customer) and the novelty of the triad (Gädeke and Wartenberg's triad of dominator, dominated and peripheral agents).

The literature also helps. Pettit's own exit argument (2006, p. 142) assumes that no one depends on "any particular master." A shared screening model is one master behind many employers, so §5.2 can answer the republican exit tradition (Taylor 2017; Breen's summary of Pettit and Lovett) on its own terms. Frye's (2024) relocation of workplace domination from ownership to managerial discretion supports the paper's choice to assign duties to whoever holds a position rather than to call for a change of ownership. And Claassen (2024) undercuts the §5.1 dismissal of the fiduciary route, since he reports that fiduciaries can serve competing beneficiaries under duties of fairness.
