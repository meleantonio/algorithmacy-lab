# Facet C: literacy withheld

Facet C, `C_literacy_withheld`, 2026-10-13. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.

## What this facet covers

Laws and customs that kept reading or writing from particular people, on what stated ground, and who objected at the time. The facet covers anti-literacy statutes for enslaved and free Black people in the Americas (quoted from archival copies), exclusion by sex, caste, class and religion elsewhere, and the practice of teaching the poor to read but not to write.

## The question the synthesis answers

For each documented withholding: what was done, to whom, where and when, on what stated ground, who objected at the time, and what ended it. The synthesis also lists the claims the sources contradict.

### Statutes and official texts

**1. South Carolina, Act of 10 May 1740 ("An Act for the better ordering and governing Negroes and other Slaves in this Province"), s. XLV.**
- Reference: *The Statutes at Large of South Carolina*, vol. 7, ed. David J. McCord (Columbia, S.C., 1840), pp. 397 ff.; s. XLV at p. 413. Bib key: `mccord1840statutes`.
- Verified: archive.org `statutesatlargeo07edit`, https://archive.org/details/statutesatlargeo07edit. The contents list (djvu text) gives "A.D. 1740. 670. An Act for the better ordering and governing Negroes and other Slaves in this Province ... 397". A later act in the same volume (p. 419) revives the act "passed the tenth day of May ... one thousand seven hundred and forty".
- Read status: **read in part** (s. XLV and its page; the contents list; the p. 419 revival clause).
- What it does: fines anyone who teaches a slave to write or uses a slave as a scribe. It says nothing about reading.
- Quotation (vol. 7, p. 413, s. XLV): "And whereas, the having of slaves taught to write, or suffering them to be employed in writing, may be attended with great inconveniences; Be it therefore enacted by the authority aforesaid, That all and every person and persons whatsoever, who shall hereafter teach, or cause any slave or slaves to be taught, to write, or shall use or employ any slave as a scribe in any manner of writing whatsoever, hereafter taught to write, every such person and persons, shall, for every such offence, forfeit the sum of one hundred pounds current money."
- Stated ground: "great inconveniences", unspecified.
- Scale: colony of South Carolina.
- Problems: Problem 2 (supports: the statute states a ground, but a thin one, and names no objector). Problem 3 (national/colonial only).

**2. South Carolina, Act No. 2639, "An Act to amend the Laws in relation to Slaves and Free Persons of Color", 17 December 1834.**
- Reference: same volume, pp. 468-469. Bib key: `mccord1840statutes`.
- Read status: **read in part** (ss. I-III and the enacting date).
- Quotation (vol. 7, p. 468, s. I): "If any person shall hereafter teach any slave to read or write, or shall aid or assist in teaching any slave to read or write, or cause or procure any slave to be taught to read or write, such person, if a free white person, upon conviction thereof, shall, for each and every offence against this Act, be fined not exceeding one hundred dollars, and imprisoned not more than six months; or if a free person of color, shall be whipped, not exceeding fifty lashes, and fined not exceeding fifty dollars ... and if a slave, to be whipped at the discretion of the court, not exceeding fifty lashes". It continues: "And if any free person of color or slave shall keep any school, or other place of instruction, for teaching any slave or free person of color to read or write," the same penalties apply.
- Quotation (p. 468-469, s. II): "If any person shall employ, or keep as a clerk, any slave or free person of color, or shall permit any slave or free person of color to act as a clerk or salesman, in or about any shop, store, or house used for trading, such person shall be liable to be indicted therefor".
- Enacting date: "the seventeenth day of December ... one thousand eight hundred and thirty-four".
- Note: this adds reading to the 1740 prohibition on writing, adds a penalty on the learner's teacher who is Black, and removes Black people from clerical work (a literacy-dependent trade).
- Stated ground: none in the text.
- Scale: state.

**5. North Carolina, *Revised Statutes* (1836-37), ch. XXXIV s. 74 and ch. CXI s. 27.**

**6. Virginia, "An Act to amend the act concerning slaves, free negroes and mulattoes", 7 April 1831, ch. XXXIX, ss. 4-6.**
- Reference: *Acts passed at a General Assembly of the Commonwealth of Virginia* (Richmond, 1831), pp. 107-108. Bib key: `virginia1831acts`.
- Verified: Encyclopedia Virginia transcription, https://encyclopediavirginia.org/primary-documents/an-act-to-amend-the-act-concerning-slaves-free-negroes-and-mulattoes-april-7-1831/ (a secondary transcription of the archival *Acts*; its page marks match the print pages 107-108; I did not see the page image).
- Read status: **read in full** (whole act).
- Quotation (s. 4, p. 107): "all meetings of free negroes or mulattoes, at any school-house, church, meeting-house or other place for teaching them reading or writing, either in the day or night, under whatsoever pretext, shall be deemed and considered as an unlawful assembly ... to inflict corporal punishment on the offender or offenders, at the discretion of any justice of the peace, not exceeding twenty lashes."
- Quotation (s. 5, p. 107): "if any white person or persons assemble with free negroes or mulattoes, at any school-house, church, meeting-house, or other place for the purpose of instructing such free negroes or mulattoes to read or write, such person or persons shall, on conviction thereof, be fined in a sum not exceeding fifty dollars, and moreover may be imprisoned at the discretion of a jury, not exceeding two months."
- Quotation (s. 6, p. 108): "if any white person, for pay or compensation, shall assemble with any slaves for the purpose of teaching, and shall teach any slave to read or write, such person ... shall, for each offence, be fined at the discretion of a jury, in a sum not less than ten, nor exceeding one hundred dollars".
- Reading: the 1831 text bans teaching *in assemblies* of free Black people and *for pay* to slaves. It does not ban a master teaching his own slave or a parent a child. The Virginia act of 2 March 1819 that Goodell and others cite I could not open (gap).
- Scale: state.

**11. Frederick Douglass, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave* (Boston: Anti-Slavery Office, 1845), pp. 33-34.** Bib key: `douglass1845narrative`.

- Problem 2: supports that the rule was stated openly and as policy. Problem 5: the stated aim is social placement, not what the pupils gained.

## (c) Synthesis

### Failure 1. Laws against teaching the enslaved, and the free Black, to write and read (British North America and the United States, 1740-1850s)

- What was done and to whom. South Carolina in 1740 fined anyone who taught a slave to write or used a slave as a scribe (entry 1). Georgia in 1829 made teaching "any other slave, negro or free person of colour, to read or write" an offence for white and Black alike, with a fine up to $500 and jail for a white offender and fine and whipping for a Black one (entry 3). Alabama in 1832 fined $250-500 anyone who tried "to teach any free person of color, or slave, to spell, read, or write" (entry 4). South Carolina in 1834 added reading, penalised Black teachers with up to fifty lashes, and barred Black clerks (entry 2). North Carolina's law, in its 1837 form, indicted any free person who taught a slave to read or write or gave or sold slaves "any books or pamphlets", and flogged a slave who taught another (entry 5). Virginia in 1831 made assemblies of free Black people "for teaching them reading or writing" unlawful and punishable by up to twenty lashes, and fined white teachers (entry 6). The Cherokee Nation in 1841 forbade teaching enslaved or non-Cherokee free Black people, if Goodell's quotation is accurate (entry 8).
- Where and when. Five U.S. states and one Native nation, 1740-1841. The Georgia, Alabama, Virginia and South Carolina acts of 1829-34 cluster within six years. The text of the Georgia act ties its ban to a statute on "printed papers ... calculated to excite disaffection" and to a capital offence for circulating such papers (entry 3).
- Stated ground. Where a ground appears in the statute it is thin: "great inconveniences" (1740), or the pairing with incendiary printed matter (Georgia 1829). The fuller ground is in Auld's reported speech: teaching to read would "forever unfit him to be a slave" and make him "discontented and unhappy" (entry 11). The Berry remark (entry 13) states the aim as a precaution against "light", but I could not verify it.
- Who objected at the time. Douglass named it as the thing that revealed "the white man's power to enslave" (entry 11). Margaret Douglass called it "one of the most inhuman and unjust laws that ever disgraced the statute book of a civilized community" in 1853 (entry 12). Woolman recorded in 1757 that instructing slaves in reading was "not only neglected, but disapproved" among Friends and New Lights (entry 13); this is a report of a lapse, not an objection to a statute. For the legislatures themselves I found no objector in a source I opened.
- What ended it. Not in the sources I opened. The laws were in force when Douglass was tried in 1853 (entry 12).
- Scale. State. No source here covers the Americas as a whole.
- Bearing. Problem 2: met, with named contemporaries (the Douglasses) and a stated standard (their own words). Problem 3: state-level only. Problem 4: not tested here. Problem 5: the withholders and the objectors agree that literacy had power, which complicates the myth.

### Failure 2. The separation of writing, arithmetic and reading in the slave codes, and in schooling for the poor

- What was done. South Carolina 1740 prohibited writing and left reading unmentioned (entry 1). North Carolina allowed "the use of figures" and banned reading and writing (entry 5). Hannah More's Mendip Sunday schools taught Scripture reading and "allow[ed] of no writing for the poor" (entry 14). Davies Giddy argued in the Commons in 1807 that schooling the labouring poor would make them "factious and refractory" and "enable them to read seditious pamphlets" (entry 15).
- To whom, where, when. Enslaved people in South Carolina (1740) and North Carolina (1830s); labouring children in Somerset (1801); the English poor in general in a bill of 1807.
- Stated ground. More: servants and "industry and piety" (entry 14). Giddy: "prejudicial to their morals and happiness"; "despise their lot in life" (entry 15). The two grounds differ: More restricts the skill that would let a pupil change station, Giddy opposes the schooling.
- Who objected at the time. Whitbread moved the bill. The House voted 47 to 13 to go into committee (entry 15). For More's rule I found no objector. Laqueur, who I could not open, is the lead for the debate over writing in Sunday schools (entry 23).
- What ended it. Not found. The fate of the 1807 bill is not in the source.
- Scale. Local (Mendip); colony and state (South Carolina); national bill (UK).

### Failure 3. Exclusion by sex and caste in South Asia, as recorded by colonial officials (Bengal and Madras, 1822-1838)

- What was done. Adam reports a custom, not a law, by which girls in Nattore were "expressly forbidden" to imitate a brother's penmanship (entry 16). Munro's 1822 Madras Minute reports that reading and writing were "unknown" to Hindu women "because the knowledge of them is prohibited" (entry 16).
- Stated ground. Widowhood, "modesty", and "intrigue".
- Who objected. Not in the passages I read. Adam and Munro describe; they do not condemn. Sources I did not find: Phule on the Education Commission in 1882, Savitribai Phule's school, Ambedkar.
- What ended it. Not found.
- Caste. The only caste text I opened (Gautama XII) restricts the hearing and recitation of the Veda by a Sudra, not reading or writing (entry 10).
- Scale. District (Adam), presidency (Munro), and a Sanskrit normative text of unknown date.

### Failure 4. Exclusion of the enslaved from public school (Rio de Janeiro Court, 1854)

- What was done. Decree 1.331-A, art. 69 § 3 barred slaves from enrolling in or attending the primary and secondary schools of the Court municipality (entry 7).
- Stated ground. None. The article groups slaves with children who have contagious illness or no vaccination.
- Who objected. Not found.
- Scale. One municipality of the Empire.

### Failure 5. Religious bar on the laity's possession of Scripture (Toulouse, 1229)

- What was done. The council forbade the laity to hold Old and New Testament books, except a Psalter or Breviary, and forbade any translation (entry 9).
- Stated ground and objectors. The article I read names none. Not found.
- Scale. One provincial council. Tertiary source only.

### Claims the sources contradict

1. The 1740 South Carolina act is sometimes summarised as barring teaching slaves to "read and write". The statute text bars teaching to *write* and using a slave as a scribe, and does not mention reading (entry 1, p. 413). The summary I saw (log #1) credited Encyclopedia.com with the "read and write" version; I did not open that page.
2. Anti-literacy statutes are sometimes pictured as a single block banning all teaching. The Virginia act of 1831 bans *assemblies* of free Black people for reading and writing and *paid* teaching of slaves (entry 6). North Carolina exempts "figures" (entry 5). South Carolina in 1740 left reading alone (entry 1). Alabama reaches both enslaved and free people of colour and includes "spell" (entry 4).
3. Goodell, quoting Kent, dates a Virginia statute to "1830"; the act I opened is dated 7 April 1831 (entries 6, 13).
4. The Mayor of Norfolk gave the penalty as "a fine of one hundred dollars and imprisonment for six months" in 1853 (entry 12). The 1831 act's penalties are different (entry 6). A later codification governed; I did not open it.
5. Douglass reports Auld saying that teaching a slave to read was "unlawful" (entry 11). I did not find a Maryland statute in a source I opened.
6. The caste restriction attached to Gautama XII is on hearing and reciting the Veda (entry 10). It is not a ban on reading or writing, and nothing I opened shows the penalty was applied.
7. Munro calls the bar on women's reading a "prohibition", then says it is "much less attended to in some districts" and that women of "the Rajbundah and some other tribes" are "generally taught" (entry 16). Adam says zemindars "in general instruct their daughters" (entry 16). The custom was uneven.
8. I found no source that links the restrictions to a measured effect. The sources say what the withholders feared literacy would do; they do not show that it did.
