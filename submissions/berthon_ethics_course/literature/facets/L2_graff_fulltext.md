### B1. Kamperman and Graff (2026)

**kamperman2026ai** Kamperman, Sean, and Harvey J. Graff. 2026. "On 'AI Literacy': The Rhetorical and Historical Fallacies of a Movement and Its Marketing." *Change: The Magazine of Higher Learning* 58(4): 34-39. https://doi.org/10.1080/00091383.2026.2706429

- **Verified how.** Crossref (log row 1) and the PDF's own title page.
- **Read status.** *Read in full*, pp. 34-39, including Table 1 (p. 37) and the reference list (p. 39). The PDF's text layer is clean (born-digital, not a scan).
- **Form.** A magazine essay for higher-education administrators and faculty. No footnotes; 17 references, of which two are Graff's books (2023a, 2023b, the WAC Clearinghouse reprints of the 1991 and 2011 Transaction editions) and the rest are policy documents, news items and a handful of composition-studies works. Graff's Literacy Myth is cited by its 1991 edition (p. 39).
- **Scale.** US higher education, with the K-12 level mentioned in passing. Evidence is institutional announcements (University of Florida, Ohio State, DeVry, the University of Kansas institute), two international frameworks (UNESCO 2024, OECD 2025) and two US federal documents (Department of Labor 2026, the 2025 AI Action Plan). No historical data of its own.

**Argument, section by section.**

1. *Front matter and "In Short" (pp. 34-35).* Authors are Kamperman (English, Kansas; co-director of an AI and digital literacy institute) and Graff (Ohio State). Three bullets: AI-literacy initiatives "too often conflate technology skills with reading and writing"; history shows "two myths" they tend to promote, (a) that effective use of generative AI is "a foundational skill that is simple to acquire" and (b) that AI literacy is "a sure path to economic security"; the authors advocate an "AI and Literacy" approach (p. 35).
2. *Opening, unheaded (pp. 35-36).* They survey institutional uptake: UNESCO's 2024 framework "claims 'AI literacy' as a right for all students but barely mentions reading or writing"; the OECD framework never says how its four competencies "overlap with, or contradict, basic literacy skills"; Florida's plan "does not discuss writing at all"; Ohio State has proclaimed all graduates from the class of 2029 "AI fluent"; DeVry will embed "AI literacy" in every course by the end of 2026 (p. 35). They tie the rush to commerce: it "accelerates alongside the major AI companies' widening campaign to encircle higher education", and runs "parallel to the peddling of 'AI literacy' as a sexy solution by technology companies, university presidents, and other marketeers". They then date the pattern: "The same thing happened in the 1980s with personal computers and word processors (computer literacy) and the late 1990s/early 2000s with all forms of digital media (digital and multimodal literacy)" (p. 35). The historical warrant is the citation to Graff 2023a, 2023b, Johnson 2023 and Lewis 2015.
3. *The thesis sentence (pp. 35-36).* "By failing to understand reading, writing, and technology use as socially and historically contextualized, the technological literacy campaigns of the late 20th and early 21st centuries repeat many of the conceptual errors of 19th- and early 20th-century print literacy campaigns, reiterating two harmful myths."
4. *Myth #1: "Literacy involves basic skills that, once acquired, form an unshakeable foundation for future intellectual, creative, and rhetorical work" (p. 36).* Technologies' paths are hard to predict; generative AI is "neither simple nor (yet) foundational"; learning to read and write well is itself not simple. They add that "Computer literacy,' and 'AI literacy' following it, strive to seduce us into thinking the simple and foundational can be easily combined. It is a way for technology companies and, increasingly, state education departments and legislatures to (a) assert the foundational social and educational importance of products deemed central to the nation's economic prosperity and (b) recruit consumers at an early age" (p. 36). The 2025 AI Action Plan is described as enlisting "Americans of all ages in a do-or-die struggle with the Chinese Communist Party for AI dominance, with generous input from major AI companies and investors" (p. 36).
5. *Myth #2: "Literacy is a certain or guaranteed path to economic security" (p. 36).* They concede it "is not baseless" and that the "global AI divide" could harm marginalised groups, then fault the earlier "computer literacy" programmes, which "did no more than thrust laptops and tablets into the hands of marginalized teens", with critics alleging "overmonitoring and increased student surveillance". They say New Literacy Studies "demonstrated that the belief that any form of literacy, from alphabetic to AI, is by itself an independent or transformative factor is a myth (Graff, 2023a, 2023b)", a myth that persists "despite its limited basis in reality" and leads to the belief "that failure to learn to read and write by a certain age is the fault of the individual" (p. 36).
6. *"A Blueprint for an 'AI and Literacy' Approach" (pp. 36-38).* They quote EDUCAUSE's logic that "understanding should precede use" and ask "Is this literacy, or education?" (p. 37). Their remedy has two parts: students and faculty study how people learn to read and write, the history of literacy and the "affordances and limitations" of literacy technologies; and students write with and without AI, using AI "only when developmentally and pedagogically appropriate" (pp. 37-38). Table 1 (p. 37) sets four areas (global and historical context; political and economic context "including corporate interests"; reading and writing with/out AI; research with/out AI), a reflection strand, and an "Ethics and transparency" line: "Explicitly model ethical AI use always. Establish norms for disclosure. Address privacy and do not require students to sign up for private AI accounts."
7. *Benefits and close (p. 38).* Students learn to "see through marketing tricks" ("AI is a marketing term, not an intellectual or technical one"); learn that "AI tools, like all technologies, are never neutral"; and learn that conversations ignoring the cultural, political and economic forces behind literacy technologies "risk ignoring or hiding AI's harms (and, perhaps, underestimating its benefits)". The last line: "The future of AI is uncertain, but literacy's potential to nurture learning and personal enrichment is clear."

**The "fallacies" of the title.** The body never uses the word. The title's "rhetorical and historical fallacies" correspond to the two "harmful myths" and the "conceptual errors" of the thesis sentence (agent's reading). Word search of the article for "fallac" returns the title only.

**What the article does not contain.** Word search and page reading both find no mention of: literacy tests for voting or immigration, anti-literacy statutes, treaties or deeds, documents overriding speech, a print monopoly or licensing, UNESCO's campaigns of the 1960s-2000s (UNESCO appears only as the author of the 2024 teacher framework), colonial or residential schooling, or any third party standing between two people. "Ethical" appears twice, once in the author biography (the Kansas institute's "ethical concerns") and once in Table 1 ("model ethical AI use"); the argument is conceptual and pedagogical, not a history of wrongs.

### B2. Graff, *The Literacy Myth*, "Introduction to the Original Edition: Literacy and History" (1979)

**graff1991literacy** (key shared with the book; see `.bib`). Graff, Harvey J. 1979. *The Literacy Myth: Literacy and Social Structure in the Nineteenth-Century City.* New York: Academic Press. "Introduction to the Original Edition: Literacy and History," pp. 1-19. (The 1991 Transaction edition reprints it.)

- **Read status.** *Read in full*, pp. 1-19.
- **What it argues.** Part I (pp. 2-6) says a "literacy myth" surrounds us, that definitions of literacy are vague, and that research on literacy's correlates is inconsistent, so the myth survives. Part II (pp. 6-10) reviews the modernisation literature (Inkeles, Schuman, Rogers and Herzog) and argues that literacy's apparent effects belong to schooling and to urban-industrial experience. - **Bearing on §4.** *Complicates problem 5.* Graff says benefits existed and the myth is an inflated reading of them (p. 19). *Does not touch* problems 1-4 beyond the general line that schooling carried "social and cultural hegemonic functions" (p. 19).

### B3. Graff, "Introduction to the Transaction Edition" (1991)

**graff1991literacy**. Graff, Harvey J. 1991. "Introduction to the Transaction Edition." In *The Literacy Myth: Cultural Integration and Social Structure in the Nineteenth Century* (Transaction), pp. xiii-xxvii (notes xxvii-xxviii; references xxviii-xxxiii).

- **Read status.** *Read in full*, as supplied (pp. xiii-xxxiii). The text proper ends p. xxvii; the rest is notes and a bibliography, which I scanned but did not audit.
- **What it argues.** It is a historiographical essay on "three generations" of historical literacy studies (pp. xiii-xxii) and an agenda for the third. A short section (pp. xxii-xxvi) turns to policy and says historians have something to tell planners: there are "multiple paths" to mass literacy, with Sweden (p. xxiv) and Prussia (p. xxv) as cases; leaders feared "unbridled, untempered literacy" and then provided it in "carefully controlled" institutions (p. xxv); and schooling's "integrating and hegemony-creating purposes" grew with class society (pp. xxv-xxvi). It closes on quality against quantity (pp. xxvi-xxvii).
- **Scale.** Western history, c. 1500-1990; Sweden, Prussia, North America as cases.
- **Bearing on §4.** *Supports problem 5* (UNESCO quoted as acting on "self-evident axioms and hopes", p. xxiii). *Adjacent to problem 2 (withholding)*: the passage on elites who found literacy dangerous (p. xxv). The 1991 introduction defines the myth nowhere in a single sentence (see (c) Q3).

### B4. Graff, *The Literacy Myth*, chapter 7, "Literacy: Quantity and Quality" (1979)

**graff1991literacy**. Graff, Harvey J. 1979. *The Literacy Myth*, ch. 7, pp. 269-324 (in the Academic Press pagination; the supplied PDF carries the running head "Literacy and Society").

- **Read status.** *Read in full*, all 56 pages, including footnotes. Pages 307-309 are photographs of street signs, with captions.
- **What it argues.** The final chapter asks about the *quality* and *uses* of literacy in a society already 90 per cent literate. Section I (pp. 272-278) reads the Kingston superintendent's reports of 1850-52 and the later Ontario record to show crowded rooms, irregular attendance, poor teachers and failed reading instruction. Section II (pp. 278-292) follows the reading-method debates (letters first against words first) in Massachusetts, Upper Canada and England, finds that oral fluency passed for comprehension, and argues that the schools nonetheless achieved "hegemony" and "socializing". Section III (pp. 292-302) examines what promoters (the religious and labour press) wanted literacy used for and what people actually read. Section IV (pp. 302-321) asks what society and the economy needed, and concludes that imperfect literacy sufficed for most work and daily life. Section V (pp. 321-324) draws the parallel to the twentieth-century "crisis" and says the myth "must be exploded" (p. 324).
- **Scale of evidence.** Kingston school reports 1850-52 (pp. 272-275); Hamilton enrolment 1851 and 1861 (p. 276); a Swedish parish where "only 10%" read with passable comprehension (p. 271); Massachusetts reports of 1839-44; English commission reports of 1851-66; Ontario labour and religious periodicals of 1835-1885; Calhoun (1973), Prentice (1974), Lewis (1953). The 1861-census tabulation and the three-city occupational data live in earlier chapters and appendices, which I did not read; chapter 7 cites their result ("around the 90% mark in each of the cities", p. 271) without re-deriving it.
- **Bearing on §4.** *Complicates problems 5 and 4.* It is the nearest thing in the four texts to a statement of what promoters promised and of the gap. It does not touch problems 1-3.

## (c) Synthesis by question

### Q1. Kamperman and Graff: the argument

*Fallacies named.* None under that name in the body (B1). They name two myths (foundational simplicity; sure path to economic security) and one lapse of method: treating "reading, writing, and technology use" as ahistorical (p. 36). Two further faults appear in passing: using "literacy uncritically as a metaphor to promote technology for technology's sake" (p. 35), and conflating "technology skills with reading and writing" (p. 35).

*History invoked.* Four things. (1) The "literacy myth", cited to Graff, as the claim that literacy is "by itself an independent or transformative factor" (p. 36). (2) "19th- and early 20th-century print literacy campaigns" (p. 36), named in one clause with no example, date, place or figure. (3) The 1980s "computer literacy" and late-1990s "digital and multimodal literacy" waves (p. 35). (4) Table 1, Area 1: "the history of English manuscript writing and typography, exploring, for example, how the introduction of spaces between words helped normalize silent reading" (p. 37); Area 2 has students discuss "the politics and economics of contemporary book publishing" and Area 3 has them "Explore how print alphabetic literacy functions as a technology of communication and thought" (p. 37). They do not invoke tests, campaigns by name, or UNESCO's historical programmes. Deficit framing appears once, as the myth's corollary that failure to learn is "the fault of the individual" (p. 36); marketing appears throughout.

*Do they say AI literacy repeats earlier ethical failures?* They say it repeats "conceptual errors" and "mistakes of the past" (p. 36), and they call the myths "harmful" (p. 36). The harms they name are surveillance and little learning gain for marginalised students in earlier computer-literacy programmes, individual blame, and corporate capture of the agenda (pp. 35-36). They do not call any of this an ethical failure, a wrong done to identifiable people, or a violation of a standard (agent's reading). They mention no voting tests, anti-literacy laws, treaties, print monopoly or UNESCO campaigns, and no third party between two people.

*Remedy.* An "AI and Literacy" curriculum: teach the history and sociology of literacy technologies, have students write with and without AI, and "establish norms for disclosure" (pp. 37-38). The remedy is education of the person, with a disclosure norm and one anti-compulsion line: "do not require students to sign up for private AI accounts" (p. 37).

**What literacy did and did not do, and the qualifications.**

*Individuals: mobility, occupation, wealth.*
- 1979 introduction, p. 19: "Literacy did carry certain benefits to those who possessed it, although its possession often signified attributes other than the abilities of reading and writing." And: "Its value, in fact, depended heavily on other factors, from ascribed social characteristics such as ethnicity, sex, or race, to the institutional, social, economic, and cultural contexts in which it was manifest." And: "Literacy, then, did not universally serve to benefit all who had attained it, but neither did it disadvantage all those who had not."
- Chapter 7, p. 320: "Illiteracy undoubtedly hindered people's advancement culturally, materially, and occupationally (in normative sociological terms), but the level of literacy demanded for survival was not one to block all progress or adjustment. Class and ethnicity primarily determined social position-not literacy or education by themselves." Then: "The majority of Irish Catholic adults, for example, were literate-and selected migrants-but they stood lowest in wealth and occupation, as did laborers and servants. Women and blacks fared little better, regardless of literacy. Possession of literacy was not in itself an achievement that brought material rewards to individuals; it guaranteed neither success nor a rise from poverty."
- Chapter 7, p. 302: at the artisanal level "literacy was more often needed; this appears clearly in the differential access to this occupational level and to its rewards which separated the experiences and attainments of some literate workers from most uneducated ones", though "the evidence presented forces us to evaluate it cautiously".
- Chapter 7, p. 321: "Literacy, it seems certain, was not the benefit to individuals that it was promised to be; nevertheless, it had sufficient impact at the level of skilled work and in its consensual acceptance for its larger limitations and other purposes to be blurred and largely ignored."

Ch. 7, p. 292 says that lacking literacy was "perhaps ... one cause of their restricted occupational distribution and their inability to escape criminal conviction", a hedged remark. The crime analysis itself is in an unread chapter.

*Society.* Chapter 7, p. 321: "On the larger, societal level, literacy even if imperfect was especially important. This related directly to the moral bases of literacy and to the reestablishment and maintenance of social and cultural hegemony. Literacy was more central to the training, discipline, morality, and habits it accompanied and advanced than to the specific skills it represented." And: "literacy was more valuable to the society's goals and needs than to those of most individuals within it." 1979 introduction, p. 19: literacy "contributed regularly as an element of the structure of inequality, reinforcing the steep ridges of stratification, and also as a force for order and integration." 1979 introduction, p. 16, summarising the field: "it is agreed that literacy does make economic contributions to the individual and society." 1991 introduction, p. xxiii: "the connections and disconnections between literacy and commercial development, a generally positive relationships, and literacy and industrial development, often an unfavorable linkage at least in the short run of decades and half-centuries".

### Q4. The require/reward test

**The draft's sentence** (`design_ethics_DRAFT.md:85`): "societies came to *require* literacy for civic, legal, and economic standing; nothing here claims literacy *rewarded* the literate, which is the narrower, defensible half of a distinction Graff's *Literacy Myth* (1991) insists on and does not contest."

**Verdict: not in the parts read, and its first half is qualified by chapter 7 for the case Graff studied: unskilled and semiskilled work seldom required literacy, while skilled, clerical and professional work did require or favour it (pp. 302, 318-321). As an attribution to Graff it is unsupported.** Neither introduction states a distinction between a society requiring literacy and literacy rewarding the literate; nor does chapter 7. Chapters 1-6 and 8 onward remain unread, so "not found" is not "absent from the book".

Evidence, with the nearest sentences.

1. *Nearest in form.* Chapter 7, p. 321: "Consequently, on the basic level of social and economic progress and those who determined it, literacy was more valuable to the society's goals and needs than to those of most individuals within it." The distinction here is between benefit to society (hegemony, order) and benefit to individuals, not between requirement and reward. Also p. 321: "Literacy ... was not the benefit to individuals that it was promised to be." The contrast Graff draws is *promised* against *delivered* to individuals.
2. *On "require", chapter 7 splits by occupation.* P. 303: "Simply, high degrees of literacy were often not required for work or welfare." P. 302, of "an unskilled or semiskilled worker": "Doing his or her job seldom, if ever, required the use of reading and writing"; but "At higher levels of skilled work, such as the artisanal level, literacy was more often needed; this appears clearly in the differential access to this occupational level and to its rewards". P. 318: "A constant use and a high quality of reading and writing were required only by clerical workers and professionals." P. 319: "Few forms of employment required much literacy either, and illiterates were spread throughout the occupational spectrum." P. 320: "the level of literacy demanded for survival was not one to block all progress or adjustment." P. 303: the culture "neither required nor desired" a high level. For civic or legal standing (votes, courts, title), these texts say nothing. The passages nearest to a require/reward pairing are: ch. 7 p. 292, "the level of literacy demanded by society is also relative"; p. 271, literacy "was not a requirement for adaptation or some success"; p. 316, of the middle class, "The achievement of education ... was more than ever before a requirement for the maintenance and transmission of their class and status"; p. 320, "entry into skilled work was more difficult, and even some of the limited demands placed upon literacy skills could not be met by such disadvantaged individuals"; p. 321, "the higher uses of literacy and the corresponding benefits and status were often precluded"; and 1979 introduction p. 16, "Literacy requirements, we now understand, vary among different social and economic groups, regions, and communities." None sets a society that required literacy against literacy failing to reward the literate.
3. *On "reward".* Graff does not say literacy rewarded no one. P. 19 (1979 introduction): "Literacy did carry certain benefits to those who possessed it". Ch. 7, p. 302: access to artisanal work "and to its rewards" separated "some literate workers from most uneducated ones". P. 320 says possession "was not in itself an achievement that brought material rewards to individuals". So "nothing here claims literacy rewarded the literate" is a statement the paper can make about itself, but Graff's finding is *limited* and *conditional* reward, not none.
4. *Where Graff uses "required" in a legal sense.* 1991, p. xxiv, on Sweden: "reading literacy was required under law for all persons", with "regular personal examinations by parish clergy", "without any concomitant development of formal schooling or economic or cultural change that demanded functional or practical employment of literacy skills", and a rationale that "was conservative: piety, civility, orderliness, and military preparedness". Here a church and state required literacy of persons as a duty, independent of economic demand. It is the closest passage to "a society requiring literacy", and it is not about standing and not about reward.

*Agent's reading.* The draft states a distinction Graff does not state; the part of it that rests on his evidence runs against its first half and qualifies its second. Two ways to keep what the paper needs without the attribution: say what Graff's chapter 7 does show (mid-century Ontario work seldom required literacy; literacy brought individuals limited, conditional rewards; society gained more from literacy than most individuals did), or drop the distinction and cite the cases in facets C and D for requirement. The author decides. The same ruling in `RESEARCH_PLAN.md` §3 (third row) and the table at `design_ethics_DRAFT.md:106` ("Comprehension Yields Agency: In Enlightenment literacy, decoding the text grants access to civic standing") rest on a reading Graff's 1861 Ontario finding does not support: "Class and ethnicity primarily determined social position-not literacy or education by themselves" (ch. 7, p. 320), and literacy "contributed regularly as an element of the structure of inequality" (1979, p. 19). The scope caveat is Alexander's, as F reports it: the data are from a society 90 per cent literate in 1861 (ch. 7, p. 271), which says nothing about the earlier stage at which standing might have turned on literacy.

### Q5. Chapter 7: quality, uses, and the schools' purposes

*The argument in his words.* "Common schooling experiences were not in most cases likely to produce more than imperfectly skilled individuals" (p. 276). The ground: "A literate society statistically, this was also a society in which individuals could read only after a fashion" (p. 291). Evidence on how reading was taught:

- The alphabetic ("letters first") method against the "word method". Kingston 1850-52: "The old system of teaching the alphabet first, then advancing to the spelling of syllables of two or three letters remained the rule" (p. 274). Henderson, the local superintendent, warns of "children reading what they evidently do not understand, and hence the habit of what is called school reading" (p. 275).
- Mann (1839) estimated "that fully eleven-twelfths of the reading students in the schools of Massachusetts neither understood the meanings of the words they uttered nor mastered the sense of their lessons nor grasped the ideas or feelings intended by the authors" (p. 281). Graff gives this as Mann's estimate.
- An English inspection "involved hearing each pupil read; 150 were examined in an hour and a half, a rate of one each 36 seconds-hardly a test assuring good reading ability" (p. 282). Promotion between schools also turned on reading: "To advance, fluent or good-sounding reading was required" (p. 282; Graff's own sentence, glossing the school regulations Mann criticised), so fluency could pass for literacy.
- Both methods "were condemned as failing in their goals" (p. 285). Attendance ran at "about 50% of the enrollment levels" from the 1850s to the 1870s, "with common attendance of 100 days or less" (pp. 276-277). In England the Commission of 1861 found "a fair elementary education" attained "by only one-fourth of those who attended, or one-eighth of all children" (p. 286).
- Swedish parish: "Of all those examined in one parish, only 10% read with passable to good comprehension; of those who read orally with 'achievement,' only 23% understood passably or better" (p. 271).

*What literacy was for, in the promoters' eyes.* Moral training, order and hegemony, not skill.
- p. 289: "Failings in instruction, we must recognize, need not signify that the school was not achieving many of its aims: in fostering hegemony and in socializing pupils."
- pp. 289-290: "Respectability, manners, taste, morality, and speech habits would be inculcated in the process of instruction." And, p. 290: "One significant use of training in literacy was to homogenize the speech of the pupils."
- p. 290: "By proscribing differences in speech under that comprehensive term of condemnation 'school reading,' reformers could then justifiably move to Canadianize or Americanize the children of the immigrant and the laborer." Contemporary deficit language, with citations: Thayer, "a coarse style of pronouncing degrades the reader, and gives one a low idea of his breeding and his taste"; Mann, inability to spell "justly stamps the mind with the stigma of illiteracy" (p. 290).
- p. 298: "The expected and desired use of literacy was to support the moral bases of society and order."
- p. 321 (quoted in Q3): literacy "more central to the training, discipline, morality, and habits it accompanied and advanced than to the specific skills it represented."

*Discipline and imposition.* Graff reports that the working-class press blamed the public schools: "Making attendance and reading instruction compulsory, schools gave the children 'dime novels for perusal, having previously given them a taste for such reading'" (p. 296). The classroom is described in Henderson's words as "a prison from which [the child] gladly escapes" (p. 275). Ch. 7 supports the "enforced language standard" reading of ruling 5 for this place and time, on Graff's account of the reformers' purpose; it does not say anyone was deprived of a language.

*Tests.* Chapter 7 treats reading inspection and promotion by fluency, and the English "payment for results" system, which "brought no reports of progress in reading" (p. 287). It does not discuss tests as a gate to civic standing. On the 1970s: "the recent movement toward competency-testing in schools constitutes the first concrete effort at specificity" (p. 323).

*Promoters' promises, and who objected at the time.* Graff identifies the promise only generally: "social realities contradicted the promoted promises of literacy" (pp. 320-321). Promoters seldom promised wealth: the Christian Guardian "could also appeal, if rarely, to economic self-interest", "as Ryerson and Mann had done" (p. 299). The named objectors in these pages are:
- W. B. Hodgson, *Exaggerated Estimates of Reading and Writing as a Means of Education* (London, 1868): "It ought never to be forgotten that the power to read does not in the least determine the use to which it is put" (p. 301).
- Phillips Thompson (1877), whom Graff reads as saying schooling bolstered the existing order: "The whole tendency of modern education ... is to make rascals" (p. 301).
- The labour press: the *Palladium of Labor* and *Ontario Workman* held that a worker "must be a Reader and a Thinker" (p. 300), and the Workman and Palladium "inveighed against recurring illiteracy and the degradation of education" (p. 299).
- Grove, reviewing "the last third of the nineteenth century" (p. 269): "We worship the fetish of reading and writing" (p. 269); and the epigraph from *Palladium of Labor* (1884): "a few weeks schooling in the year is practically of no value" (p. 269).
- Galtung (1976), in an epigraph: "What would happen if the whole world became literate? Answer: not so very much, for the world is by and large structured in such a way that it is capable of absorbing the impact" (p. 322).
