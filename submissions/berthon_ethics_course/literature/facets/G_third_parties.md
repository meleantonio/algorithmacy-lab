# G — Third parties between two people: scribes, notaries, interpreters

*Facet G, 2026-10-13. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Facet A found cases where the party that held the written record bound the party that could not make its own. It found no case of a *distinct third party* who read, wrote or translated for one or both principals and whose text then bound them. This facet looks for that figure in four places: notaries, scriveners and public letter-writers in Europe and Spanish America; the English plea *non est factum* for people who signed what they could not read; interpreters at colonial treaty councils; and the Walking Purchase and Waitangi as test cases. **The question the synthesis answers:** where did an intermediary who was neither principal write, read or translate the instrument that bound them; what is documented about that intermediary serving one side, falsifying, or being distrusted; what safeguards existed; and where did illiterate people use scribes and notaries for their own ends. For each case the synthesis states three facts and no more: (a) two principals plus a distinct intermediary; (b) an intermediary whose text bound both; (c) evidence that the intermediary's interest diverged from one principal's. No parallel to algorithmacy is drawn.

**Legend for read status.** *Read in full* = I read the whole text this session. *Read in part* = I opened the text and read the pages or paragraphs cited. *Abstract only* = a database abstract or the opening text a database returned (here, Consensus); I paraphrase and do not quote beyond it. *Not accessible* = no text and no abstract. ## (b) Entries

### Group 1. English law: the plea of *non est factum*

**foster1869mackinnon** — *Foster v. Mackinnon* (1869) L.R. 4 C.P. 704, Court of Common Pleas, judgment of 5 July 1869 delivered by Byles J. for the Court (Bovill C.J., Byles, Keating, Montague Smith JJ.).
- *Verification:* the Law Reports vol. 4 (1868–69) on archive.org, `law-reports-common-pleas_1868-1869_4`; the case head at p. 704 and the index entry "Foster v. Mackinnon … 704". Connell (2016) cites it at 711–712.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (the headnote, the facts and the judgment, pp. 704–712, in OCR text with some noise). An archival copy of an official report, so a primary source.
- *Facts:* the defendant, "a gentleman far advanced in years", signed the back of a bill of exchange after Callow, "secretary to a company", told him it was a guarantee; Callow "only shewed the defendant the back of the paper" (p. 705). A later holder sued on the bill. The jury found for the defendant; the Court upheld the direction.
- *Quotations (p. 711, Byles J):*
  - "It seems plain, on principle and on authority that, if a blind man, or a man who cannot read, or who for some reason (not implying negligence) forbears to read, has a written contract falsely read over to him, the reader misreading to such a degree that the written contract is of a nature altogether different from the contract pretended to be read from the paper which the blind or illiterate man afterwards signs; then, at least if there be no negligence, the signature so obtained is of no force."
  - "In Thoroughgood's Case (1) it was held that, if an illiterate man have a deed falsely read over to him, and he then seals and delivers the parchment, that parchment is nevertheless not his deed."
  - The Court notes that the rule is "not confined to the condition of an illiterate grantor", and on the Keilwey case it observes that "That, however, was a case where the grantee himself was the defrauding party." (p. 711)
  - Limit (p. 712): for negotiable instruments the principle "must be and is limited in its application … A qualification of the general rule is necessary to protect innocent transferrees for value."
- *Scale:* one case; the doctrine is national (England and Wales) and spread through the common-law world.
- *Bearing:* **Problem 1** — the doctrine is an early statement that a written instrument read falsely to a person who cannot read does not bind him, so a court in 1869 recognised the exact risk: a reader who misreads. **Problem 2** — the standard is stated in the judgment, and it is a contemporary legal one. The judgment names the risk but its facts do not involve a neutral third-party reader: the person who misdescribed the paper was Callow, the acceptor. **Problem 4** — the safeguard is conditional on the signer's non-negligence and on no innocent holder being hurt.

**saunders1971anglia** — *Saunders v Anglia Building Society* (also *Gallie v Lee*), [1970] UKHL 5, [1971] A.C. 1004 (House of Lords).
- *Verification:* the citation and the BAILII address (`bailii.org/uk/cases/UKHL/1970/5.html`) appear in a web search summary and in Connell n. 13.
- *Read status:* **not accessible.** BAILII is behind a bot wall (log row 6). What follows is Connell's quotation of the speeches, so it is second-hand.
- *As quoted by Connell (2016), pp. 249–250 and nn. 18–21:* Lord Reid at 1016 says (in Connell's words) that the extension of the plea to the blind and illiterate logically should also apply to "those who are permanently or temporarily unable through no fault of their own to have without explanation any real understanding of the purport of a particular document, whether that be from defective education, illness or innate incapacity"; and says of a man of full capacity "In general I do not think he can be heard to say that he signed in reliance on someone he trusted." Lord Wilberforce at 1025 speaks of a "residue of difficult cases" including "illiterate or senile persons who cannot read, or apprehend, a legal document". Viscount Dilhorne at 1023 asks whether the same standard of care should be expected of "an elderly spinster who might, if she read the document, be none the wiser".
- *Facts and outcome, from Connell, Part III.A:* a widow, Mrs Gallie, whose spectacles were broken, signed a deed of assignment of her house on Lee's incorrect explanation that she was making a gift to her nephew Parkin; Lee then mortgaged the house to the Anglia Building Society; the Lords, like the Court of Appeal, found she "failed to establish her plea of non est factum", because the document was not radically different from what she thought and because she had been careless. Connell adds that the deed "was drawn up for Lee by a firm of solicitors" (Part III.B).
- *Bearing:* the most-cited modern holding is a refusal. The signer lost. The party who explained the deed (Lee) was the counterparty, and the solicitors drafted it for him.

**connell2016not** — Connell, S. (2016). Not my doctrine? Finding a contract law explanation for non est factum. *Victoria University of Wellington Law Review* 47(2): 245–266. doi:10.26686/vuwlr.v47i2.4807.
- *Verification:* Crossref (title, author, 2016, vol. 47, pp. 245–266); the open PDF's title page matches.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (abstract, Part II pp. 248–250, Part III.A–B). A secondary legal article; the quotations of Saunders and Petelin are his.
- *Quotations:*
  - Abstract, p. 245: "In its early days, non est factum was available only to blind and illiterate persons who, without being careless, relied on another party's seriously flawed explanation of the document."
  - Part II.A, p. 249: "The first major expansion of non est factum occurred at a time when a large proportion of the population was unable to read, and transactions involving deeds were commonplace. The plea was extended to blind and illiterate persons who had relied on someone else's explanation of the deed." (n. 15 cites Thoroughgood's Case.) "Recognising that the expansion … potentially undermined the ability of parties to rely on signed legal documents generally … courts sought to limit its scope."
  - Same page: "In the 19th century, non est factum was extended to cover written contracts as well as deeds. It was also held to be available to literate persons who had not acted carelessly."
- *Scale:* England and the common-law world; says nothing about numbers of people helped or refused.
- *Bearing:* **Problem 1** — the doctrine is the law's own record that people who could not read relied on someone else's explanation and sometimes were bound by a false one. **Problem 2** — he supplies no contemporary 16th-century objector; the plea is a judicial invention and a standard of the courts. **Problem 4** — the explanation of the doctrine is about innocent third-party enforcers, which is a separate triad from the signer-explainer-counterparty one.

**almeida2019firmo** — Almeida Cabrejas, B. (2019). "No firmó porque dijo no saber": alfabetizados y analfabetos en documentos notariales de CODEA de los siglos XVI y XVII. *Philologia hispalensis* 33(1): 21–41. doi:10.12795/ph.2019.v33.i01.02.
- *Verification:* Crossref; the open PDF (Univ. de Sevilla journals) matches.
- *Read status:* **read in part** (pp. 21–24 and 28–29 and the notes; the corpus tables not read). A linguistic study of about 1,000 documents from the CODEA corpus.
- *What it records:* the Pragmática of Alcalá de Henares of 7 June 1503 made notaries enter full deeds in a register and sign-off by the parties. The article quotes it (via Marchant Rivera 2015, p. 335, a secondary quotation of the 1503 text), p. 24: "assí como fueren escritas las tales notas, los dichos escrivanos las lean presentes las partes e los testigos. E sy las partes las otorgaren, las firmen de sus nombres. E sy no supieren firmar, fyrme por ellos qual quiera de los testygos, o otro que sepa escrivir". The notary must say that the witness signed for a party who could not write: "el qual dicho Escrivano haga mencion como el testigo firmò por la parte, que no sabìa escrevir" (p. 24, quoting the *Recopilación*, Madrid 1777, Tít. 25, ley XIII, p. 877).
- *Examples of practice (p. 29):* "la dicha Francisca Fernández, la cual porque no sabe escrevir rogó a los dichos testigos que lo firmasen en mi registro" (CODEA 2274, Ávila, 1510); "E no firmó por no saver. Leyósele su dicho, ratificose en él" (CODEA 1810, Madrid, 1591; the article gives "no firmó por no saber" as a formula for witnesses in depositions and similar participants, not for the grantor of a private deed); the author says the formula for inability to sign is frequent "dada la alta tasa de analfabetismo especialmente entre las mujeres" (p. 29). At p. 23 witnesses' depositions "en general 'les es leída'".
- *Scale:* the Castilian crown; about 1,000 documents, mostly Castile.
- *Bearing:* **Problem 1** — shows the safeguard (reading aloud, a witness who signs, a notary's note), not a wrong. It says nothing about misreading by notaries. **Problem 2** — no contemporary objection in the parts read. The article is also evidence that illiterate parties routinely made binding instruments through a notary (Question 4).

### Q1. Where did a literate intermediary write the binding instrument for parties who could not read it, and what safeguards existed?

The documentary base is thin and unevenly deep. What the sources I opened support is this.

- **A notary was the intermediary by design in Roman-law Europe.** Nussdorfer's opening says even people who could not read or write "went to notaries" and that the notary's instrument counted as "full proof" (2016, opening). In Castile the Pragmática of Alcalá (7 June 1503) required notaries to read the notes ("las lean presentes las partes e los testigos") in the presence of the parties and witnesses and, for a party who could not sign, to have a witness or another literate person sign and to say so in the instrument (Almeida Cabrejas, p. 24, quoting the law via Marchant Rivera). The formulae in the corpus show the signing rule at work: "rogó a los dichos testigos que lo firmasen" (CODEA 2274, Ávila 1510) (p. 29). The reading-back formula "Leyósele su dicho, ratificose en él" (CODEA 1810, Madrid 1591) is, on the article's own account (pp. 23, 29), a witness-deposition formula, not a deed.
- **England chose a different path.** A snippet of Clanchy (PA3) says a statute required each bond to be written by a designated clerk, but "did not require the clerk to put either his name or his scribal signature on the bond like a notary". English law then dealt with the misreading risk after the fact, by a plea: *non est factum*. Byles J in 1869 states it as a rule that a person who cannot read, and has "a written contract falsely read over to him", and signs a paper of "a nature altogether different", is not bound if not negligent (LR 4 CP 704, 711), and traces it to *Thoroughgood's Case* (1582). Connell dates the first extension to "blind and illiterate persons" to a time when "a large proportion of the population was unable to read", and then reports the courts' fear for the "ability of parties to rely on signed legal documents" and the later limits (carelessness; radical difference). In the leading modern case, *Saunders* (1971, via Connell), the signer lost.
- **The leading cases hold the misreader to be a counterparty.** In Foster the man who said the paper was a guarantee was the acceptor of the bill; Byles J remarks of an older case that "the grantee himself was the defrauding party". In Saunders it was Lee, who took the assignment. In none of the three opened or second-hand accounts is the person who wrote or read the instrument a neutral third party, such as a scrivener paid by the signer. I did not read *Thoroughgood* or *Petelin*, so I cannot say the same of them.
- **Seals and reading aloud (Clanchy).** I could not confirm from snippets that a charter had to be read to a grantor or that a seal protected an illiterate grantor. What the snippets show is that "reading continued to be conceived in terms of hearing" (SL16-PA44) and that Italian notaries were "an elite of professional writers" with special status (PP29).

### Q2. Treaty and land-deed interpreters

- **Williams at Waitangi** is the one named interpreter with a documented, contested story. He interpreted for Hobson "sentence by sentence" and had made the Māori text; he held land and had a church superior's letter telling him to support cession; the Tribunal quotes both the historians who call this a conflict of interest and those who reject deceit. Ward, who once took a critical view, wrote in 1988 that he did "not think any of this is true" (p. 414). The Tribunal in the pages read neither convicts nor clears him, though it records "general agreement" that Hobson and his missionary agents "put a positive gloss on the meaning and effect of the treaty to encourage the rangatira to sign" (p. 421) and, at p. 514 (not among the pages read), writes that Williams "may have been honest in his choice of 'kawanatanga'" but "must, however, have known that tino rangatiratanga conveyed more than what was set out in the English text". What it does find, from facet A, is that the rangatira did not cede sovereignty and that Hobson "did not explain" the English intention (p. xxii).
- **Interpreters at Anglo-Iroquois councils.** Hagedorn finds suspicion (greed, ethnicity, intoxication) and checking by a second interpreter; Weiser and Davison settled a disputed rendering with the Indians present (pp. 65–66). Jennings's harsher view (that the question is what the interpreter said the text said) comes only through Ghere's abstract; Hagedorn's n. 8, attached to her sentence calling the "generally unscrupulous" characterisation "too harsh", cites Francis Jennings, *The Ambiguous Iroquois Empire* (1984), p. 63, without saying what he wrote.
- **The Walking Purchase** does not belong in this facet on the sources read: the Court names no intermediary and attributes the fraud to Penn.

### Q3. Did any contemporary name the intermediary's position as a wrong or a danger?

- Colenso, 6 February 1840, asked Hobson whether the Natives understood the articles (Tribunal p. 352 caption). He also wrote shortly afterwards that he did not suppose the chiefs knew they had restrained themselves from selling to whom they will (p. 421, via Ross). That is a contemporary naming a danger; it is aimed at the explanation, not at a named scribe.
- Governor Denny asked in 1757 that Indians give their speeches to the interpreters beforehand (Hagedorn p. 66), and interpreters whose honesty was doubted were checked by others (p. 61).
- The Crown in Spain legislated (the Pragmática, 1503) and, per Andrien, supplied "approved documentary forms and procedures" and self-help manuals; notaries had a reputation as "rogues and scoundrels". These are regulatory and reputational facts, not petitions by illiterate parties.
- Archdeacon Langton wrote to Gregory IX in 1238 that forgery was rife in the church of Canterbury (Clanchy snippet RA1-PR21); the complaint is about a church's seals, not about a scribe serving one lay party.
- I found no petition by an illiterate party against a notary or scrivener, and no statute whose preamble names the harm. Seabra (abstract) refers to Portuguese royal legislation on false notarial documents; I did not see it.

### Q4. Counter-evidence: protection and use by the illiterate

- **Safeguards were real and written into the instruments** (Pragmática 1503; "firmó a su ruego un testigo"; the notary's note), and the corpus shows illiterate parties, women above all, entering such acts routinely (Almeida Cabrejas, p. 29).
- **Illiterate and non-literate people used scribes and notaries for themselves.** Nussdorfer's opening: people "who could not write or read themselves went to notaries". The abstracts for Yannakakis (2015, 2021), Jones (2016) and Premo (2017) (no entry, bib record or DOI check exists in this facet for Yannakakis 2013 or Cunill 2016, which the line also cited before) describe Indigenous communities and litigants using notarial forms and interpreters in colonial Mexico, Guatemala and Peru, including native scribes acting as notaries. Burns, per Andrien, presents notarial offices as sites of negotiation where "the greed and trickery of both notaries and their clients" shaped the record. None of these is read beyond its abstract or opening.
- **Interpreters were checked, not unchecked.** Hagedorn p. 61.
- **The law's own record cuts the other way as well.** The plea of *non est factum* protected a blind or illiterate signer (Foster), and the House of Lords in Saunders still refused it to a widow who relied on her nephew's associate. Both are true.

### Q5. Scale and what the sources do not show

Waitangi is one territory and one week. The notarial evidence is a kingdom's rule (Castile), a city's profession (Rome, Cuzco, Paris) and a corpus of about 1,000 acts. *Non est factum* is a national doctrine with three leading decisions, one of them read first-hand. The interpreter evidence is a thirty-year regional record. No source gives the number of illiterate people bound by a falsified or misread instrument, in any place. No source I opened reports a scribe or notary convicted of falsifying a deed for an illiterate client (Seabra's abstract mentions a Porto case, but of a *negotium non verum* in a true act).

### Claims the sources contradict

1. **"Henry Williams deliberately mistranslated the Treaty."** The Tribunal reports Ward (who did "not think any of this is true", p. 414), Head ("an implausible conspiracy to deceive", p. 414), McKenzie (does "not impute to Williams any will to deceive the Maori by his choice of terms", p. 417, though on pre-emption he "implied" deliberate misleading, p. 422) and Owens (haste and inexperience, p. 418). Belich (p. 415) and Moon and Fenton (pp. 417, 421) hold the opposite. In the pages read the Tribunal does not decide; at p. 514, outside those pages, it writes that "Williams may have been honest in his choice of 'kawanatanga'" but "must, however, have known that tino rangatiratanga conveyed more than what was set out in the English text".
2. **"Interpreters at treaty councils were generally unscrupulous."** Hagedorn (p. 61) calls this "too harsh" while accepting that self-interest and doubts existed. Jennings's contrary view is known from Ghere's abstract; Hagedorn's n. 8 also cites Jennings (1984, p. 63) at that point without quoting him.
3. **"The Walking Purchase turned on a forged or mistranslated document read to Delaware chiefs."** The Court recites a forgery allegation without a source and names no interpreter. Web summaries (not opened) call the 1686 document an unsigned draft or an event that may never have happened. These do not agree, and I have no scholarly account.
4. **"Non est factum was made for people who could not read and still protects them."** Byles J (1869) extends it to a literate man who "forbears to read" without negligence, and Connell says it was extended to written contracts and to literate persons. In Saunders (1971) the signer lost.
5. **"The notary served the powerful against the illiterate."** The reviews of Burns describe notaries serving both litigants and clients as shapers of the record; the Castilian rule put witnesses and a reading aloud between the notary and the illiterate party.
6. **A Consensus attribution error.** Two Consensus records (Trejo, *Journal of Latin American Geography* 10: 220–222; McCulloch 2018 "Linked Data for Cultural Heritage") carry review text about Burns's book although their DOIs resolve to other titles. I did not use that text, including its claim about a 1570s Toledo restriction on Andean native notaries.
