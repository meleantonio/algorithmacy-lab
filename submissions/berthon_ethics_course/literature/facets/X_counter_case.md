Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.

## What this facet covers

The strongest case against the paper's thesis (the global community failed on ethical grounds in the literacy transition and repeats the failure in the transition to algorithmacy). Three questions from the brief: (1) where the literacy transition went well, by the standards of its time and ours; (2) whether "ethical failure" is a later judgment (presentism), and what standard would license the charge; (3) where the literacy/algorithmacy comparison breaks. Per the brief, this facet may discuss the comparison to algorithmacy.

**The question the synthesis answers.** Which parts of the charge survive if a hostile referee presses each of these three points, and which sources the referee would use?

## (c) Synthesis

### Q1. Where the transition went well

1. **The demand came from below in at least two documented cases.** Johansson describes a Lutheran programme in which reading "was everybody's calling and right" (p. 64) which the article places before the industrial revolution; Washington records "a whole race trying to go to school" (p. 30) just after emancipation. Each is a source about a community that took literacy up. A referee would say: the thesis counts the spread of literacy as the occasion of failure, and these sources count it as the achievement.
2. **The same programme coerced.** Johansson himself reports that "worldly compulsion and church discipline" held people "to the book" and that "Christening and the teaching and examination of children, confirmation and Holy Communion opened the road to marriage and other social privileges" (p. 64); the rural "resistance" he reports (p. 63) was to "costly school systems", not to the examinations. On the paper's definition of failure ("making standing conditional on it"), this is an instance, found in the best source for the achievement. So the Swedish case does not clear the thesis. It shows the achievement and the failure in one institution, with no contemporary objection recorded in this article. Who ended it, and when, is **not found** in the source I opened.
3. **Measured benefits exist, with caveats.** Schooling raises earnings about 9 percent per year of schooling on average (Psacharopoulos and Patrinos, abstract) and lowers child mortality, with a world-scale meta-analysis reporting 31.0% lower under-5 mortality for children of mothers with 12 years of schooling than for children of parents with none (Balaj et al., abstract). Quasi-experimental studies in Zimbabwe, Malawi and Uganda find causal effects of schooling reforms (abstracts). All of these measure years of schooling in the late twentieth century, not literacy in the medieval, print or early mass periods the paper treats. The causal evidence is weaker than promoters said (Mensch et al.: 18 of 46 effects significant once endogeneity is handled; Desai and Alva: a causal relationship "far from established"). So the benefit evidence supports the counter-case on direction and the paper on overselling (P5). Political participation: **not found** in this session; I did not search a source for it.
4. **Campaigns.** Cuba 1961 and Kerala are known to me here only through abstracts. Serra: gains in literacy, no political empowerment; Mitra and Singh: top female literacy beside high female suicide and domestic violence. I opened no source that reports a Cuban outcome measure, and I cannot give a figure for either case. Freedpeople's schools: Washington only. Abolitionist schools: **not searched**.

### Q2. Presentism, and what standard licenses the charge

- **Did contemporaries name it?** In one case, yes: Douglass (1845) names the ban on teaching slaves to read as a wrong, and Auld's own words give the ground on which it was done ("It would forever unfit him to be a slave", ch. VI). That is evidence against the presentism objection for withholding. For the other claimed failures (conditioning standing on literacy, imposing it, overselling it) this facet found no contemporary objection. That is absence in my search, not absence in the record; facets C to F carry those cases.
- **What standards are available.** (i) *Contemporary naming* (the plan's test; met by Douglass). (ii) *The actors' own professed standard*: the Lutheran programme claimed reading as every believer's "right", and coerced it (Johansson, p. 64), which is an internal-critique charge needing no present-day morals. (iii) *Present-day morals applied to the past*: Hunt calls the habit "moral complacency and self-congratulation" while allowing that findings need not be "irrelevant" and relativism should not be endorsed; Butterfield holds that the historian is "fitted only to be the detective" (p. 79) while allowing that moral responsibility is not eliminated (p. 90); Vann (abstract) and Bloxham (abstract) argue that historians cannot avoid moral evaluation. (iv) *A split between condemning a practice and blaming persons*: Levy (p. 19): "we should condemn what they did ... We don't need to blame them as well." (v) *Outcome against promise*: the harm is overselling, tested by evidence such as Mensch et al.
- **What I would tell the author.** The thesis survives if it says which of (i) to (v) it uses for each failure, and it is safest on (i), (ii) and (v). Standard (iii) invites Hunt and Butterfield. The word "failed" for a "global community" is a collective charge, and Levy's standing argument and Mason's mixed verdict apply to individual wrongdoers, so the paper has to say whom it holds responsible: states, churches, planters, ministries, or no one. Moody-Adams (1994), the text most likely to support blame under ignorance, was not opened.

### Q3. Where the comparison breaks (disanalogies a careful reader would raise)

1. **Adaptivity.** The page does not respond to the reader; the system does. Yeung (abstract; PDF p. 7) describes continuously updated, feedback-driven systems as unlike "static" nudges. This supports the author's table row on the nature of the medium. Yeung's object is a single user and a choice architect, not a triad.
2. **An owner behind the system.** The author's table says text cannot "observe, react to, or anticipate the reader". A referee would reply that there was always a party behind the page: the draft itself cites Innis ("monopoly of knowledge") and Johns (fixity "as much a product of social actions as of the inherent properties of the press", Johns 2002, p. 121 as quoted by the draft) on this point (`design_ethics_DRAFT.md` line 83). I did not open Innis or Johns, so I quote the draft, not them. If the draft's own sources hold, the "no party behind the page" side of the contrast is overstated for print. The disanalogy then rests on adaptivity and on binding outcomes, not on ownership.
3. **Comprehension and power.** The table (`design_ethics_DRAFT.md` line 106) says that in literacy, "decoding the text grants access to civic standing", and that in algorithmacy comprehension is "decoupled from power". Douglass cuts both ways. In ch. VI, reading gave him "the pathway from slavery to freedom"; in ch. VII it gave "a view of my wretched condition, without the remedy". Comprehension without leverage is therefore documented for literacy, at least for the enslaved, where the law denied the leverage. The row holds for a free reader and is overstated for a withheld one.
4. **Binding outcomes.** The definition of algorithmacy requires an intermediary that "commits outcomes binding on both" parties. Written deeds and charters bound parties too, which the draft's Clanchy line implies (trust moved from persons to documents in England, 1066 to 1307; `design_ethics_DRAFT.md` line 83). I did not open Clanchy, and the draft does not say that a scribe or notary stood between two parties as a reader of both. If one did, the literacy side has a counterpart to the intermediary, and the disanalogy narrows to the intermediary being automated, adaptive and owned. That is a lead for facet A, and it favours the thesis.
5. **Which property carries the comparison.** Qadri et al. say analogies fail when they do not name the property compared, and that governance aimed at the wrong property goes astray. The paper's three failure types (withholding, gating, imposing) each need a stated counterpart property in algorithmacy, or the section reads as a pattern-match.
6. **Who the actor is.** In the literacy cases the doers are identifiable: slaveholders and legislatures (Douglass), churches and states (Johansson). For algorithmacy the actor is firms and platforms. "The global community" is the subject of both halves of the thesis, and the evidence in this facet is regional or national (Sweden, the American South, Cuba, Kerala) or aggregate (Balaj). Scale (P3) is a weakness in both halves.
7. **Competence versus condition.** Literacy was taught in programmes with test endpoints (Johansson: reading examined at confirmation, communion, marriage, migration, p. 64). An endpoint for algorithmacy is not specified in any source I opened, so "failing to teach it" has no yardstick yet. This is my argument, not a sourced finding.
8. **Speed.** Johansson's programme spans roughly 1600-1900. I opened no source that dates the algorithmic transition, so the contrast in speed is unsourced; I leave it out as a claim.

### Claims the sources contradict, or leave unsupported

- **"Literacy rewarded the literate" or "literacy produced health and wage gains"** as a general statement: weakened by Mensch et al. and Desai and Alva, which are about schooling, not literacy.
- **The table's "Comprehension Yields Agency" for literacy**: qualified by Douglass ch. VII (a withheld reader had comprehension without leverage); ch. VI shows the opposite.
- **"Static and passive" text with no party behind it**: contradicted, if the draft's own citations are right (Innis, Johns; not opened by me).
- **A literacy campaign as pure imposition**: contradicted by Johansson (a "right" in the programme's terms) and Washington (demand from the freedpeople).
- **A literacy campaign as pure benefit**: contradicted by Johansson (coercion and legalism, marriage gate), Serra (no political empowerment) and Mitra and Singh (the Kerala paradox).
- **"Contemporaries did not name the wrongs"**: contradicted for withholding (Douglass 1845).

### The four strongest objections (ranked by how much damage each does)

1. **Presentism by the plan's own test.** Failures lacking a contemporary objection or a stated standard drop out (plan §2). Sources: Hunt (read in full), Butterfield (read in part), Levy (read in full). The author's best answer is standards (i), (ii), (v), and the condemn-versus-blame split.
2. **The "global community" is not an agent in the evidence.** Every case here is a region, a state or a church. Sources: Johansson, Washington, Serra, Mitra and Singh (the last two abstracts).
3. **The benefits are real and the demand came from below.** Sources: Johansson (read in full), Washington (read in part), Balaj and Psacharopoulos (read in part). They measure schooling, mostly of the late twentieth century.
4. **The draft's own table overstates the disanalogy**, which helps neither side of the thesis until fixed: Douglass ch. VII (with ch. VI) against row 4; Innis and Johns, as cited by the draft, against row 1. The surviving disanalogies are adaptivity (Yeung) and the owner-run, automated intermediary.
