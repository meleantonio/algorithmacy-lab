# M5 — Cases outside the Anglo-American and settler-colonial record

*Facet M5, 2026-10-13. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.*

**What this facet covers.** Historical cases the paper's §7.1 says it leaves out: examination gates in East Asia; caste, sex and scripture in the dharmaśāstra tradition and its colonial aftermath; language and schooling in Europe (Ukraine, Ireland; France and Prussia were searched and not opened); print and reading matter withheld or licensed outside England (Spanish America, the Ottoman case, Tokugawa Japan); state literacy campaigns (Nicaragua, Tanzania, Cuba; the Soviet and Chinese campaigns were searched and not opened); scripts made from below; literacy franchises in other legal systems (Latin America, the Philippines, Natal, Italy). Facets B–F hold the Anglo-American cases and I do not redo them. I record the history and draw no parallel to algorithmacy.

**The question the synthesis answers.** For each case: what was done, to whom, when, on what stated ground, who objected, what ended it, how large it was, and which of the paper's five practices (gate / withholding / record over voice / monopoly / overselling) it instances, or whether it is a counter-case. Then question 8 of the brief: do the non-Anglophone cases confirm the five practices, add a sixth, or show the pattern is narrower than claimed?

### B1. Latin American and other literacy franchises

**engerman2001evolution** — Engerman, S. L., & Sokoloff, K. L. (2001). *The Evolution of Suffrage Institutions in the New World* (NBER Working Paper 8512). https://doi.org/10.3386/w8512 · PDF https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w8512/w8512.pdf
- *Verification:* Crossref `10.3386/w8512` (title and authors match). Published version: *Journal of Economic History* 65(4), 891–921, doi `10.1017/s0022050705000343` (Crossref matches; **not opened**, so I cannot say the pages below survive into the 2005 text).
- *Read status:* read in part: the franchise section, the Latin American paragraphs and Table 3 (printed pp. 22–25 of the working paper; page numbers read from the extracted text and approximate to a page).
- *What it says:* "the introduction and growing emphasis on a literacy requirement was the major change that occurred after independence in the laws governing the franchise." "Whereas a literacy qualification was not used in the U.S. until the 1890s, when the black population was targeted for disfranchisement in the South (as well as blacks and immigrants in the North), virtually all Latin American countries included a literacy requirement for citizenship (encompassing the right to vote) in their first constitution or soon afterward." Country dates given: Bolivia 1826, "maintained beyond the 1945 constitution"; Chile "between 1833 and 1874, and then later from 1885 through 1970"; Ecuador replaced property with literacy in 1861, "which endured until 1978"; Peru 1826, "largely maintained through 1979"; Uruguay 1830 to 1918; Brazil "replaced [property restrictions] with a literacy qualification in 1891; this restriction endured until 1988." "The only major Latin American countries that did not have literacy requirements at the national level were Argentina and Colombia" (pp. 23–24).
- *Effect:* "after the literacy requirement in Chile was removed by the 1874 constitution (an action reversed in 1885), the proportion of voters in the population more than tripled within a few years" (p. 25). Table 3 (p. 24; its note dates the abolition of Chile's wealth and education requirements to 1878, the text to 1874; the Chile rows give 1.6% in 1869 and 3.1% in 1881, about a doubling, not "more than tripled") lists, by country and year, a "Literacy Requirement" column (Y/N) against "Proportion of the Total Population Voting" (for example Chile 1869: Y, 1.6%; Chile 1881: N, 3.1%; Chile 1920: Y, 4.4%; Ecuador 1888: Y, 2.8%). Note under the table: "After eliminating wealth and education requirements in 1878, Chile instituted a literacy requirement in 1885, which seems to have been responsible for a sharp decline in the proportion of the population who were registered to vote."
- *Stated ground, objector, end:* not in this text. The paper is about participation rates, not about who objected.
- *Scale:* the hemisphere; the authors do not give counts excluded, only the share voting.
- *Discrepancies with the brief and with the paper:* the brief gives Ecuador "until 1979" and Bolivia "until 1952"; this working paper says 1978 and "beyond the 1945 constitution". The paper's §3.1 dates Brazil's literacy rule to the Saraiva law of 1881; this text dates the literacy rule to 1891 (and treats earlier rules as property-based). 1891 is the Republic's constitutional re-enactment, not the start: the first national literacy requirement for enrolment is the Saraiva Law, Decreto 3.029 of 9 January 1881, art. 8, which admits to the roll citizens who prove the elector's qualities "e souberem ler e escrever" (quoted in Leão 2012, Aedos 4(11), p. 611, from Senado, Anais 1881, p. 348); Decreto 6 of 19 November 1889, art. 1, kept it; Constitution of 1891, art. 70 §1 2º bars "os analfabetos" (planalto.gov.br). The Constitution of 1824, arts 91-94, had an income test only. Facet D (F3) is right on 1881. Also: the authors say Latin American "first constitutions" were the origin of the literacy rule, which makes the U.S. test (1890s) the late one, not the model.
- *Bearing:* **gate**, §3.1 and §7.1. It extends the gate from Anglophone settler states to the hemisphere with a table. A referee in comparative political economy would expect it.

**ye2003legal** — Ye Xiaoqing (2003). The Legal and Social Status of Theatrical Performers in Beijing during the Qing. *East Asian History* 25/26, 69–84. https://eah.anu.edu.au/sites/default/files/article-content/25-26/EAH25-26_04.pdf
- *Verification:* the PDF itself (issue 25/26, June/December 2003; contents list gives pp. 69–84). No Crossref record (journal has no DOI). Peer-reviewed ("externally refereed").
- *Read status:* read in part: pp. 69–73.
- *What it says:* "The debased people were deprived of many legal rights, of which the most important was participation in the imperial examinations" (p. 69). Qianlong's 1771 order: the fourth generation "may take part in the examinations if all members of their clan, and all their relatives, remain clean ... The second and third generation will not allowed to do so under any circumstances." His 1788 regulation: "Prostitutes, actors and yamen runners, and their children and descendants, are not allowed to participate in the examinations or purchase a degree. Ye also reports an emperor ordering an actor beaten to death for asking about office: "You actors belong to the debased class, how dare you ask about official positions?" (p. 71–72). She notes Yongzheng's abolition of "jianmin" status reached the *yuehu*, not performers (p. 69).
- *Stated ground:* status ("debased") of the candidate's occupation. Not literacy.
- *Who objected, what ended it:* not in the pages I read. Not found.
- *Scale:* the capital and court; no counts of the barred.
- *Bearing:* **gate by birth, not by reading.** The imperial examination tested literacy to *admit* to office, but a status bar, enforced by flogging and loss of degree, sat in front of the test. This complicates §3.1: the keju is a literacy test that was a route up for those it admitted. Whether historians count it as mobility or exclusion is **not answered here**: Elman 2000 and 2013 and Ho 1962 could not be opened (log 2–4). One search snippet, not checked against any text, attributed to Elman the claim that the content of the exams excluded "over 90 percent" of the population from the first step; I do not use it.

### B4. Language, schooling and print in Europe

**moser2017introduction** — Moser, M. (2017). Introduction [to the special issue on the Valuev Directive]. *East/West: Journal of Ukrainian Studies* 4(2), 3–7. https://doi.org/10.21226/T2DK9B · PDF https://ewjus.com/index.php/ewjus/article/download/320/134/732
- *Verification:* Crossref (title "Introduction", Moser, journal, vol. 4, issue 2, p. 3). *Read status:* read in full.
- *What it says:* on "30 July (Old Style 18 July) 1863" Valuev issued "a secret directive stating that henceforth, 'the approval of books in the Little Russian language that have religious content as well as those of a pedagogical nature or that are intended for mass consumption' was to cease, while 'only those works which are in the category of belles-lettres' were permitted" (p. 3). The directive's ground, as the Valuev Directive itself put it: "a [distinct] Little Russian ... language did not, does not and cannot exist" (Moser cites Miller, pp. 240–41; all quotations are Moser's translations from Russian originals, n. 1). The Ems Ukaz of "30 (Old Style 18) May 1876" was "an even more rigorous ban" that "(with minor amendments) stayed in force until the Russian Revolution of 1905" (p. 3). Effect: "Ukrainians were denied" the development other European nations had in schools, press and courts, and Ukrainian "was doomed to largely remain a 'peasant language'" (p. 3). Moser's own stance: "no one should be allowed to ban, deny, or impose any language or identity" (p. 4). He summarises Remy: censors "frequently prohibited the publication of manuscripts merely because of their Ukrainian language, although they were obviously not 'intended for mass consumption'"; books published in breach in the 1870s resulted from "the corruption of Russian censorship organs"; in the 1880s violations happened when "several Russian officials considered dismissing the imperial language bans"; the restrictions "gradually eroded before they were repealed" (pp. 5–6). Tarnawsky (summarised): the bans strengthened Ukrainian identity across the Russian and Austro-Hungarian borders (p. 6).
- *Bearing:* **withholding** of print and instruction in a language, §3.6 (language imposition) and §3.4. It has all the paper's tests: a dated order, a stated ground, a named contemporary objector (see Encyclopedia entry), an end (1905 relaxation; "gradually eroded"), and a record of leakage.

## (c) Synthesis

### 1. Cases, one by one

For each: *did, to whom, when, ground, objected, ended, scale, practice.* "NF" = not found in any source I opened.

**1. Literacy franchises in Latin America (gate).** Most Latin American republics wrote a literacy requirement into their first constitutions or soon after; the U.S. used one only from the 1890s [engerman2001evolution, pp. 23–24]. Chile: 1833–74 and 1885–1970; Ecuador until 1978; Peru "largely" until 1979; Bolivia "beyond" 1945; Brazil: the 1891 Constitution (to 1988 on this source), but the literacy bar on new enrolment dates from the Saraiva Law of 9 January 1881 (see C1). Ground, objector: NF. Ended: constitutional change, dates as above. Scale: share voting stated in Table 3; counts excluded NF. One measured effect: voters "more than tripled" after Chile dropped the rule in 1874. **Practice: gate.** The authors' point is about participation, not intent.

**2. Natal 1897 (gate, with stated intent).** Passed on the model of the U.S. immigration literacy test; made harder because the persons targeted were "perfectly well able to read and write in their own language", so the test was in a European language [lake2006mississippi]. Objectors: members who said it would keep out good Europeans or that the "wily Hindoo" could pass. Chamberlain recommended it to Australia in 1901. **Practice: gate; internal-incoherence evidence.** Act text NF.

**3. Philippines 1935 (gate).** Reading and writing required of male voters, women's vote conditional on a plebiscite count [philippines1935constitution]. Ground, objector, end NF.

**4. Italy 1882 and 1912 (counter-case to the gate).** Passing the compulsory elementary course qualified a man to vote regardless of property; electors rose from 621,896 to 2,049,461 (6.9% of residents) [ballini0000sistemi]. Objectors: the reform was contested, but the entry gives no voice that attacked the literacy route. Ended: the 1912 law (males over 30 and army-service rule). The same entry reports that the laws of 30 June 1912 (n. 665) and 1913 raised the political electorate from 2,930,473 (1909) to 8,443,205 (8.3% to 23.2% of residents); whether illiterate men over 30 could then vote: NF. The 1882 entry also records a tax qualification of 19.80 lire, so the 1882 rise is not due to the school route alone (65.3% of the 1882 lists were entered "per capacità").

**5. Keju and gwageo (gate by status).** In the Qing, "prostitutes, actors and yamen runners, and their children and descendants" were barred under a 1788 regulation, with a 1771 rule that a debased family's fourth generation might sit if all relatives were "clean" [ye2003legal, p. 72]. Korea: nobi, cheonmin and seoeol "generally barred" [choi2026lifespans, p. 16]. The stated ground is status, not literacy. Objectors, scale, end: NF. Whether the exam was mobility or exclusion: NF (Elman not opened). **Practice: gate** (a status gate before the literacy test).

**6. Caste and sex, Veda and school (withholding).** The dharmaśāstra texts I read fix who may hear, recite and be taught the Veda: women's sacraments are without sacred texts (Manu 2.66); a Śūdra is the model of the uninitiated who may not pronounce Vedic text (2.172) and may not be advised or taught the sacred law (4.80); Gautama XII.4–6 prescribes, for a Śūdra (XII.1), molten tin or lac in the ears for listening to the Veda, the tongue cut out for reciting it, the body split for remembering it. Date, enforcement, scale: NF. Colonial schooling: Phule told the Hunter Commission that "the Mahars, Mangs, and other lower classes are practically excluded from all schools owing to caste prejudices" and asked the Government, "under the promise of the Queen's Proclamation", for separate schools and compulsory primary education [phule1882memorial]. Ended: NF. **Practice: withholding**, by religious law and then by tolerated custom in state schools. Writing is not the thing withheld in any verse I read; hearing, reciting and instruction are.

**7. Ukraine 1863 and 1876 (withholding / language gate).** Valuev's secret circular of 1863 ended licences for Ukrainian books with spiritual content or "intended generally for primary mass reading"; the Ems Ukaz of 1876 forbade original works and translations, imports, plays and readings, and music texts. Ground: that a distinct Little Russian language "did not, does not and cannot exist" (Moser's translation of the directive's reasoning) and, on one account, suspected Polish intrigue [moser2017introduction; danylenko2010ukrainian]. Objectors: Drahomanov at the 1878 Paris literary congress [encyclopediaofukraine0000ems]. Enforcement: leaky: eighteen breaches in 1874–76, ascribed to a corrupt censor, and later violations "at a time when several Russian officials considered dismissing the imperial language bans" [Moser, pp. 5–6]. Ended: the Ems Ukaz "stayed in force until the Russian Revolution of 1905" with minor amendments [Moser, p. 3]; it was never formally revoked [Encyclopedia]. Scale: the Empire's Ukrainian population (count NF). **Practice: withholding** (of print and school use of a language). It bears on §3.6, where the paper's boundary case is a language imposed; here a language was *denied* the school and the press.

**8. Ireland 1695 and after (withholding and licensing).** An act of the Irish Parliament (7 Will. III c. 4, per Parnell) took from Catholics "the means of educating their children either at home or abroad"; Parnell lists elsewhere (pp. 27-28, from the 1697 Commons committee report) an older law under which a Catholic schoolmaster needed the ordinary's licence and the oath of supremacy; that law is not the 1695 act. Burke called the "prohibition of the means of improving our rational nature ... the worst species of tyranny" [parnell1808history, pp. 29–30]. The repealing act of 1782 recited that the education laws "have not answered the desired effect" [p. 121]. Ended: repeal, 1782 and 1793. Scale: NF. **Practice: withholding**; a clear case of §2.3 (contemporary objection plus internal failure). The statute text should be taken from the *Statutes at Large*.

**9. Spanish Indies, 1531–1543 (withholding reading matter; licensing).** Three royal orders (1531, 1536, 1543) stopped "books of fiction in the vernacular" going to the Indies, on the ground that it was "bad practice for the Indians"; the books allowed were those "relating to the Christian religion and morality" [leonard1992books, pp. 81–82]. Enforcement: poor; the repetition shows it, and the Mendoza instruction says so [pp. 82–83]. Objector: NF. Ended: NF in what I read. Inquisition tribunals in Lima (1570) and Mexico (1571) policed only works on the Index [pp. 167–168]. **Practice: withholding** (with a licensing machinery), counter-evidence on effect.

**10. Ottoman print.** The documented fact in the source I could read is that a firman of 1727 permitted printing in Arabic script, and non-Muslim presses ran earlier [ayalon2016arabic, through an extract]. Whether the Ottomans *banned* print: the one article that tests it [schwartz2017did] could not be opened; its abstract (via facet B) says the evidence does not support a ban. **Practice: none established.** Treat as a caution, not a case.

**11. Tokugawa publishing controls.** Existed (1661, 1722, 1790–1800 measures per an exhibition page) [princeton2026banned]. No text, no scale, no objector. **Not usable as a case**; optional footnote only.

**12. State literacy campaigns.** *Nicaragua 1980:* official 50→13%; the stated objectives include politicising; the one independent audit finds about 9% of the population became literate solely through the 1980s interventions, with relapse among women graduates [stansifer1981nicaraguan; sandiford1994nicaraguan (abstract)]. *Tanzania:* official 67%→9.5% (1968→1986), yet only 35% of 13.76 million test-takers passed (1975–86) [kater1994peasants]. *Cuba 1961:* about 707,000 made literate, 250,000 brigadistas, illiteracy to 3.9%; the sources I could open are derivative [mellace2026longterm]. *Soviet, China:* not opened. Coercion: Nicaragua's church groups faced "charges of counterrevolutionaryism" if they stayed out [Stansifer]; otherwise NF. **Practice: overselling, partly; and a counter-case where a campaign delivered something real.**

**13. Scripts from below.** Hangul: a royal script, opposed by Ch'oe Manli's memorial of 1444; the memorial's real target is disputed [mair2025choe]. Cherokee, Vai, N'Ko: not opened. **No finding.**

### 2. Counter-cases (what cuts against the paper)

- **Italy 1882:** literacy as the way onto the roll, widening the electorate [ballini0000sistemi]. Cuts against any claim that a literacy franchise rule narrows the franchise by nature.
- **Chile 1874:** the same hemisphere shows the reverse (voters "more than tripled" when the rule went) [engerman2001evolution, p. 25]. Together with Italy, this means the effect depends on what the rule replaces and who administers it.
- **Tanzania and Cuba:** national campaigns that, by official counts, cut illiteracy from two-thirds to under a tenth (Tanzania) or to 3.9% (Cuba). The Tanzanian report itself shows a 35% pass rate. These are not the "oversold" campaigns of UNESCO's world targets; the paper's §3.5 treats the promise as failed everywhere, and these are cases where the government's count was met, but the Tanzanian report itself says the 9.5% headline and the 35% pass rate are not reconciled and that "actual participation in programmes is far below the official statistics" (Kater et al., §3.3.1), and the Cuban figures come from a preprint that repeats them from sources not opened; neither is yet a clean counter-case to overselling.
- **Spanish Indies and Ukraine:** bans that leaked. A paper that lists withholding as a practice needs to say it records the order and its stated ground, not the effect.
- **Keju:** a literacy test that served as the route into office. The paper's gate is a test that excludes; this one admitted, within a status bar. Historians' verdict unavailable.
- **Ottoman print:** the ban may not have existed.

### 3. Question 8: confirm, add, or narrow?

**Confirm.** The sources I opened confirm **gate** outside the Anglo-American world: Latin America (a hemisphere-wide rule), Natal, the Philippines; and by status in the Qing and Joseon examinations. They confirm **withholding**: the dharmaśāstra texts and the colonial schools that tolerated caste bars (India), Irish education law, Spanish orders on Indian reading, and the Russian ban on Ukrainian print. They confirm **overselling** only in the weaker form that campaign counts come from the campaigners.

**Not confirmed.** I found nothing outside the colonial treaty cases that instances **record over voice**. I did not search Ottoman *kadi* courts, Chinese contract practice or the Spanish notariate (facet A has that for Europe). **Monopoly** is also thin: the Spanish Indies licensing is state control of imports, not a chartered cartel like the Stationers; the Ottoman monopoly is in doubt.

**A sixth practice?** There are two candidates, and neither is a sixth practice. (i) **Designating the language of record.** Natal's "any European language"; the Welsh clause of 1536 (facet B); the Ems Ukaz; the examination's classical Chinese; the Chinese-versus-hangul dispute. The gate or the withholding often runs through a rule about *which* language or script counts. The paper's §3.6 already sees this for schooling; the evidence suggests it belongs inside §3.1 and §3.2 too, because it is how the test is made unpassable by a chosen group. That is a refinement of gate and withholding, not a new item. (ii) **Withholding by omission**: Phule's complaint that state schools tolerated the caste bar and that spending went to the higher classes. That is the state letting a bar stand, which neither the statute cases nor the gate cases cover; it is a variant of withholding where the institution is not the author of the bar.

**Narrower?** Yes, in three respects. (1) **Effect:** the non-Anglophone bans I could check often did not work (Spanish Indies, Ukraine). The paper's claim is about institutional position and stated ground, and should say so. (2) **The literacy test as gate** is not always exclusion: Italy 1882, and the keju for those it admitted. (3) **The Anglophone pattern of a test chosen *because* the targets could pass it** (Natal, Alabama) is the strongest instance of the gate; Latin America shows the rule's breadth but my source says nothing on intent. So "gate" as a *type* generalises; "gate with a stated exclusionary purpose" is documented for Natal and for the U.S. only.

### 4. Claims in the paper or the brief that these sources contradict or leave open

1. **Brazil's literacy rule dated to 1881 (§3.1).** The paper is right. Engerman and Sokoloff's 1891 is the Republic's constitutional date; Decreto 3.029 of 9 January 1881 (art. 8) first required reading and writing for enrolment, and Decreto 6 of 19 November 1889 (art. 1) repeated it before the 1891 Constitution (art. 70 §1 2º). The 1881 law also tightened income proof, so 1881 is not a literacy-only change (Nicolau, via facet D).
2. **Ecuador "until 1979" and Bolivia "until 1952" (brief).** This source gives 1978 and "beyond the 1945 constitution". The final JEH version and a national source are needed.
3. **"Molten lead in the ears".** In the text I read it is "molten tin or lac" (Gautama XII.4), a different text from Manu; Manu 8.272 pours hot oil "into his mouth and into his ears" for a different offence.
4. **Women and the Veda.** Manu 2.66 puts women's sacraments "without ... sacred texts"; I did not find or read 9.18 (the brief's pointer). The OCR of the translation I opened is poor.
5. **A single motive for Valuev.** The sources give at least two (belief that the language did not exist; fear of Polish intrigue); do not state one.
6. **An Ottoman ban on print.** Not supported by any source I could open.
7. **Phule's date.** The reprint's header says 1881; its signature block reads "Poona, 19th October 1882" (Round Table India).
