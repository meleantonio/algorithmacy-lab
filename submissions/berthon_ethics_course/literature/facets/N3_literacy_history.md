## (b) Entries

### 1. The history and sociology of literacy

**Brandt, *Literacy in American Lives* (2001), `brandt2001literacy`. Record.** Crossref confirms the Cambridge monograph and its chapters (Introduction, "The Pursuit of Literacy", pp. 1-24). The book version of the sponsor definition, as Young quotes it (Young 2013, `young2013sponsoring`, Full, p. 10, citing Brandt 2001, p. 19): sponsors are "any agents, local or distant, concrete or abstract, who enable, support, teach, and model, as well as recruit, regulate, suppress, or withhold, literacy—and gain advantage by it in some way." M1 already noted that this wording differs from the 1997 report ("teach, or model ... withhold literacy and gain advantage"). *Bearing:* §2.1, the Brandt paragraph. *Sharpens.* The manuscript quotes the 1998 article with no page; the sentence as printed in the manuscript matches the 1997 report wording, not the 2001 book's. The author should quote from one text he has opened and give its page.

**Brandt, "Sponsors of Literacy" (1998), `brandt1998sponsors` (reused). Record.** No new text opened; NCTE still returns 403 and JSTOR is not open. Secondary sources (a 2022 LSU dissertation abstract and a UT Austin transcript found by the web search) cite the definition to p. 166 of the journal; a Consensus record of the article's abstract cites "Brandt 167". I have not seen the page. A second DOI, 10.2307/358929 (JSTOR), circulates; Crossref does not resolve it, and the bib's 10.58680/ccc19983181 does resolve. *Bearing:* §2.1. The page reference must come from the journal itself.

**Jerasa and Jones, "'I keep getting TT duds': Examining sponsorship using algorithmically driven reading recommendations on #BookTok and Webtoons" (2025), `jerasa2025keep`. Abstract.** *Literacy* 60(1), e70017; CC BY. Grounded "in a literacy sponsorship framework", the authors "trace how material and immaterial sponsors, including platform algorithms, user commentary and engagement metrics, mediate access to literary content", and they report "less visible structures such as algorithmic sorting" that raise "concerns about access, gatekeeping and authority in digitally mediated reading spaces". *Bearing:* §2.1, the sentence "Her sponsor stands between a learner and literacy." *Already says part of it, and challenges the paper's distinction.* Literacy scholars already treat a platform algorithm as a Brandtian sponsor that gatekeeps. The paper's line, that the intermediary "need not teach or withhold anything to hold the test that sorts them", still holds, because Jerasa and Jones describe an algorithm mediating access to texts, not one standing between two parties and committing an outcome. The paper should cite them to show it knows the move has been made and to say what the triad adds.

**Smith, West and McCarthey, "Literacies across sponsorscapes" (2019), `smith2019literacies`. Abstract.** *Literacy* 54(2), 22-30 (Crossref issue date 2019, online). They argue for "mobilising notions of sponsorship beyond fixed conceptions of individual sponsors" and for a lens attending to "the dynamically networked, reciprocal and human‐material dimensions of literacy practices." *Bearing:* §2.1. *Supports* extending sponsorship to non-human sponsors, which is the step Jerasa and Jones take. Optional.

**Graff, *Searching for Literacy* (2022), `graff2022searching`. Record.** Crossref confirms the Springer book (ISBNs 9783030969806, 9783030969813) and chapters including "Historicizing Literacy and Literacy Studies: Axioms and Lessons" (pp. 227-270) and "The New Literacy Studies and the Resurgent Literacy Myth" (pp. 279-285). The latter is held as `graff2022resurgent` (Full, facet F, from the *LiCS* version). *Bearing:* §2.1, §3.5. *Supports.* A referee in literacy studies would expect the book cited beside Graff 1979 when the paper names "the literacy myth"; nothing in it was opened this round, so cite the chapter that was read.

**Goody and Watt, "The Consequences of Literacy" (1963), `goody1963consequences`. Record** (plus the opening paragraph, which OpenAlex carries as its abstract). *CSSH* 5(3), 304-345. The opening sets the "great divide": "add writing, and history proper begins." *Bearing:* §2.1, the Street paragraph. *Challenges, if unanswered.* This is the autonomous-model text Street wrote against. A referee will want the paper to show it is not reviving it; the paper's own sentence ("I do not claim ... that mediation alters cognition") does that work, and citing Goody and Watt as the position disclaimed would make it explicit.

**Halverson, "Goody and the Implosion of the Literacy Thesis" (1992), `halverson1992goody`. Record.** *Man* 27(2), 301 (Crossref gives only the first page). *Bearing:* §2.1. Optional; the title records the standard verdict on Goody's thesis.

**Collins, "Literacy and Literacies" (1995), `collins1995literacy`. Abstract.** *Annual Review of Anthropology* 24, 75-93. The review weighs "universalist vs situated accounts of literacy" and holds that universalist claims "cannot withstand criticism"; it then "explores the relationship among modern state formation, educational systems, and official vs popular literacies", "the role of education in class stratification", and "the volatile relations between oppressed nationalities and official literacies." *Bearing:* §2.1 and §3 introduction. *Supports* the paper's turn from skill to institution: the anthropology of literacy already locates literacy's consequences in the state and the school. Should cite, alongside the held Collins and Blot 2003 (`collins2003literacy`, reused).

**Ong, *Orality and Literacy* (1982), `ong1982orality`. Record.** Crossref holds a Taylor and Francis record (DOI 10.4324/9780203328064, ISBN 9780203328064, issued 1982). Not opened. *Bearing:* §2.1. Ong is the other great-divide text a referee will associate with any "-acy" coinage. The held Finnegan (`finnegan2012oral`, Part) is the classic critic. Cite only if the paper names the great-divide thesis.

**Vincent, *The Rise of Mass Literacy: Reading and Writing in Modern Europe* (2000), `vincent2000rise`. Record.** Open Library: Polity Press, 2000, ISBN 0745614442 / 9780745614441. Crossref has reviews only (the held Sutherland 2002, `sutherland2002vincent`). Not opened. *Bearing:* §3.5 and §3.6. A European-scale survey; a referee may expect it beside Graff when the paper says what mass literacy did and did not deliver. Optional unless the author reads it.

**Jones and Williams, "Technologies of Disenfranchisement: Literacy Tests and Black Voters in the US from 1890 to 1965" (2018), `jones2018technologies`. Abstract** (UCF STARS repository record; no DOI; *Technical Communication* 65(4), 371-386; the record says no full text is held). The authors analysed historical literacy tests "using McCornack's (1992) deceptive messaging criteria" and found "that even though the rhetorical and technical style and tone of the documents appeared to be objective and neutral, the literacy tests served as a technology of disenfranchisement, a way to oppress and marginalize, black voters." Their purpose frames this against present "voting interference and voting technologies". *Bearing:* §3.1 (the gate) and Table 1, row 1. *Already says part of it.* Technical-communication scholars have read the literacy test as a technology whose neutral surface hid an exclusionary purpose, and have connected it to present voting technologies. The paper's claim that "the test measured the examiner's wishes" is consistent with theirs. Cite in §3.1 as the closest prior reading of the test as a designed artefact. Their target is voting technology, not algorithmic intermediaries or AI literacy.

**Dasgupta and Hill, "Designing for Critical Algorithmic Literacies" (2023), `dasgupta2023designing`. Part** (arXiv preprint 2008.01719, pp. 1-3 and the four principles; published in *Algorithmic Rights and Protections for Children*, MIT Press, pp. 59-84, which I could not open). ### 3. "Algorithmic literacy" and "AI literacy", and their critics

**Long and Magerko (2020), `long2020what` (reused). Abstract.** No full text reachable (ACM 403; the author-hosted copy's host no longer resolves). The abstract defines the project as providing "a concrete definition of AI literacy" through "core competencies" and "design considerations to support AI developers and educators in creating learner-centered AI." *Bearing:* §1 and §4.1, the promise row. The paper should cite it as the standard definition it is arguing past. Cite the abstract only.

**Ng, Leung, Chu and Qiao (2021), `ng2021conceptualizing` (reused). Abstract.** Gold OA (CC BY-NC-ND), but ScienceDirect returned 403. The abstract proposes "four aspects (i.e., know and understand, use and apply, evaluate and create, and ethical issues) for fostering AI literacy based on the adaptation of classic literacies." *Bearing:* §2.1. *Sharpens.* "Adaptation of classic literacies" is the analogy at the level of skill, which is the level the paper says the analogy should leave. Cite beside Long and Magerko.

**Kamperman and Graff (2026), `kamperman2026ai` (reused, Full, L2).** Still the closest prior argument from the history of literacy to AI literacy; facet L2 records that it does not treat tests, anti-literacy laws, treaties, the print monopoly or third parties. Nothing in this round changes that.

**Cotter and Reisdorf (2020), `cotter2020algorithmic` (reused; raised from Abstract to Part).** I opened the published *IJoC* PDF (14, 745-765) and read the abstract and pp. 747, 751, 755-759. Note a title discrepancy: the PDF reads "Algorithmic Knowledge Gaps: A New **Dimension** of (Digital) Inequality"; `references.bib` and OpenAlex have "A New Horizon". The PDF should govern. They argue that "knowledge disparities—particularly within the digital realm—are closely related to structural inequities" (p. 751) and find, in a U.S. sample of 2,018, that education predicts algorithmic knowledge (β = .109, p < .001; total R² = .098; p. 755) and that breadth of use predicts it more strongly (p. 756). Their conclusion: "Our findings suggest that more privileged groups are better positioned to benefit from algorithmic knowledge than those with fewer resources" (p. 758). They also note that "even with full transparency, other characteristics of algorithms further constrain knowledge" (p. 747). *Bearing:* §3.5/§4.1, the promise. *Supports, and sharpens.* This is Graff's finding transposed: the competence tracks prior advantage, so promoting it as the remedy rewards those already advantaged. The effect sizes are small, and the paper should not overstate them. Should cite in §4.1's promise paragraph.

**Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (2023), `oeldorf2023what` (reused; raised from Abstract to Part).** I read the authors' OSF preprint ("Updated May 2023"; pp. 2-3, 7-8, 10, 17, 21, 23, 28; preprint pagination). Their research agenda opens with "balancing users' expectations of algorithmic literacy with developers' responsibility for algorithmic transparency" (p. 2). They write that the field must "consider the relationship between users and algorithm creators, particularly in terms of the responsibility that each has in promoting literacy" (p. 21), and concede: "Having proposed that algorithmic literacy should not be solely the responsibility of the social media user, the current reality is that individuals must endure most of effort in improving literacy" (p. 23). Earlier users who knew that content was filtered "did not understand how such filtering worked, or why it was done, which meant they had little power to influence or leverage it" (p. 10). *Bearing:* §4.2 and §5.1. *Sharpens.* The leading review of the field already names the allocation of responsibility between user and developer as an open question; the paper's duties answer it, and its claim that the remedy belongs to "whoever holds the positions" (§5.1) has a foothold in the field's own agenda. Should cite.

**Ridley and Pawlick-Potts (2021, `ridley2021algorithmic`, Full, Z) and Dogruel, Masur and Joeckel (2021, `dogruel2021development`).** Reused. Dogruel et al. remain Abstract (Taylor & Francis 403; the VU repository page linked no PDF). Their abstract measures "awareness of algorithms use" and "knowledge about algorithms". Neither definition contains a second party or a binding decision. Z already recorded this.

**DeVito, "Adaptive Folk Theorization as a Path to Algorithmic Literacy on Changing Platforms" (2021), `devito2021adaptive`. Abstract.** *PACM HCI* 5(CSCW2), 1-38 (ACM 403). "Because platforms constantly change, users must constantly re-evaluate their folk theories"; DeVito proposes "an extensible algorithmic literacy that would support users in ongoing theorization." *Bearing:* §4.3, "the medium answers back". *Supports.* An HCI study that starts from the premise the paper argues for: a literacy aimed at a changing system cannot accumulate as reading does. Should cite in §4.3 beside Yeung (2016); it gives the point an empirical source.

**Swart, "Experiencing Algorithms" (2021), `swart2021experiencing`. Abstract.** *Social Media + Society* 7(2); CC BY; SAGE 403. From 22 interviews with people aged 16-26, she finds that their insights "do not automatically enable young people to verbalize these, nor does having knowledge about algorithms necessarily stimulate users to intervene in algorithmic decisions." *Bearing:* §4.2, comprehension without leverage. *Supports.* Should cite beside Dubal (2023) and Cameron (2024).

**Head, Fister and MacMillan, *Information Literacy in the Age of Algorithms* (Project Information Literacy, 15 January 2020), `head2020information`. Part** (PDF from an institutional repository, eduq.info; read pp. 1-3 and the findings on PDF p. 16). From student focus groups they report "the profound ambivalence — the tangle of resignation and indignation — that almost all students expressed about algorithm-driven platforms that collect data about their personal lives"; students were "nonetheless resigned to using sites like Google and YouTube", and algorithms were "part of the deal" (PDF p. 16). *Bearing:* §4.2. *Supports*: students who understand the systems still describe themselves as unable to act. **Hargittai, Gruber, Djukaric, Fuchs and Brombach (2020), `hargittai2020black`; Gran, Booth and Bucher (2020), `gran2020to`. Gran et al. find "clear demographic differences regarding levels of algorithms awareness" in Norway and ask whether awareness "corresponds to a new reinforced digital divide." *Bearing:* §3.5/§4.1, the promise; §4.1, withheld competence (proprietary details). *Supports.* Optional; Cotter and Reisdorf carry the point better.

**Yu, "The Algorithmic Divide and Equality in the Age of Artificial Intelligence" (2020), `yu2020algorithmic`. Abstract** (Texas A&M repository record; *Florida Law Review* 72(2), 331-389). He extends the digital-divide literature to "an emerging and ever-widening algorithmic divide" with "five attributes": "awareness, access, affordability, availability and adaptability." *Bearing:* §4.1. *Contrast.* The divide framing is the access-and-skill framing the paper argues is incomplete. **Lloyd, "Chasing Frankenstein's monster: information literacy in the black box society" (2019), `lloyd2019chasing`. Abstract** (accepted manuscript, UCL Discovery; structured abstract only). She asks "how do we understand the impact of algorithms on agency and performativity, and, how do we address and plan for it in our educational and instructional practices?" *Bearing:* none directly; it shows the information-literacy field taking up algorithms as an educational problem. **Stamboliev, "Proposing a Postcritical AI Literacy" (2023), `stamboliev2023proposing`. Part** (journal PDF, CC BY-NC-ND; read the abstract, pp. 201-202, and pp. 215-221 and 224-225; journal pagination). Using the Dutch childcare-benefit scandal, she argues that explainable AI "depoliticises transparency" and that "those affected by or discriminated against by AI ... have little tools to deal with the opacity of AI as a system, while those who focus on data opacity are shaping the literacy discussion" (abstract). Of the scandal: "those who are harmed are powerless to stop, alter, or intervene in this code", and "for individuals impacted by this algorithm, XAI would not have altered much (but I am speculating here)" (p. 216). On literacy's own history, she warns that media literacy "may overpromise" (p. 218) and that teaching skills "seems to be the pragmatic solution to a complex problem ... and it is advertised as such" (p. 218). Her conclusion: "Understanding how algorithms work is important, but I have argued that having the power and resources to challenge their use is at least equally important" (p. 225). Her proposals are participatory: "demand that citizens get a seat at the table" (p. 219). *Bearing:* §1 (the transparency fallacy), §4.2, §5.3. *Already says part of it.* She makes the comprehension-is-not-leverage point against XAI and AI literacy and draws on the history of media literacy's overpromising. She does not use the literacy *transition*, the institutional positions, or a triad. Should cite in §4.2 or §1.

**Nichols and LeBlanc, "Media education and the limits of 'literacy': Ecological orientations to performative platforms" (2021), `nichols2021media`. Abstract.** *Curriculum Inquiry* 51(4), 389-412; CC BY-NC-ND; T&F 403. They argue that media-literacy approaches "retain a focus of media pedagogy that centers the actions of individual humans – namely, 'literacies'", and that this "elides more distributive agencies ... computational (algorithms), and regulatory (protocols/defaults) actors with wider networks of institutional governance and political economy"; they propose "ecology" rather than "literacy". *Bearing:* §2.1, the paragraph defending the "-acy" term. *Challenges the term and supports the argument.* This is the closest critique, inside literacy studies, of using "literacy" for platform life at all. A referee from education will know it. The paper agrees with its diagnosis and keeps a literacy-shaped word; it should say why (the triad names a relation, as §2.1 already says) and cite Nichols and LeBlanc as the objection answered. The held Nichols and Stornaiuolo 2019 (`nichols2019assembling`) is the companion genealogy of "digital literacy". Must cite one of the two.

**Cotter, "'Shadowbanning is not a thing': black box gaslighting and the power to independently know and credibly critique algorithms" (2021), `cotter2021shadowbanning`. Abstract.** *Information, Communication & Society* 26(6), 1226-1243; CC BY-NC-ND; T&F 403. "Black box gaslighting captures how platforms may leverage perceptions of their epistemic authority on their algorithms to undermine users' confidence in what they know about algorithms and destabilize credible criticism"; it is "an epistemic contest over the legitimacy of critiques in which platforms hold the upper hand." *Bearing:* §4.1, the record row, and §5.4. *Supports and sharpens.* It is the record over the voice in its platform form: the operator's account of its own system outranks what users say they observed. Should cite in §4.1's record paragraph.

### 4. "Algocracy" and the -acy coinages

**Aneesh, "Global Labor: Algocratic Modes of Organization" (2009), `aneesh2009global`. Abstract.** *Sociological Theory* 27(4), 347-370 (closed). He distinguishes "labor integration through programming code" from "bureaucracy and the market", naming three ruling principles: "bureaucracy (legal-rational), the market (price), and algocracy (programming or algorithm)." *Bearing:* §2.1, the definition of algorithmacy. *A cousin the paper must distinguish.* "Algocracy" names rule *by* algorithm as a mode of organization; "algorithmacy" names the competence demanded of people who coordinate *through* one. A referee who knows the sociology of algorithms will ask about the resemblance. Must cite, in one sentence.

**Danaher, "The Threat of Algocracy: Reality, Resistance and Accommodation" (2016), `danaher2016threat`. Abstract** (Crossref record: *Philosophy & Technology* 29(3), 245-268; the PhilArchive green-OA copy returned 403 to both tools; I read the author's 2015 seminar abstract, saved as `danaher_progcity.txt`, NUI Maynooth Programmable City seminar, 2 November 2015). The abstract defines the threat as "a situation in which algorithm-based systems structure and constrain the opportunities for human participation in, and comprehension of, public decision-making", arising because such systems make decisions "more opaque", and calls it "a significant threat, one that is difficult to accommodate or resist." The 2015 seminar abstract and the published article may differ in wording. *Bearing:* §2.2 (domination), §4.2 (comprehension), §5. *Already says part of it, in political philosophy.* Danaher argues that algorithmic decision-making threatens legitimacy by constraining participation and comprehension, and that accommodation and resistance are both hard. He addresses public decision-making, not a private intermediary between two parties, and he does not use the history of literacy. A BEQ referee in AI ethics would expect it. Must cite, after the author reads the article; it is the most-cited philosophical treatment of the comprehension problem (509 citations in OpenAlex).

**Wilkinson, "The Concept of Oracy" (1965), `wilkinson1965concept`; Balchin and Coleman, "Graphicacy Should Be the Fourth Ace in the Pack" (1966), `balchin1966graphicacy`. Record.** *Educational Review* 17(4), 11-15; *Cartographica* 3(1), 23-28. The titles document that "-acy" coinages for competences beside literacy and numeracy have a sixty-year history in education. *Bearing:* §2.1, "A term ending in '-acy' can smuggle that assumption back in." Optional; one clause would show the coinage has precedent.

**Benjamin, *Race After Technology: Abolitionist Tools for the New Jim Code* (2019), `benjamin2019race`. Record** (Open Library: Polity, 2019, ISBN 9781509526390 / 9781509526406) plus the abstract of the *Social Forces* review (Jipguep-Akhtar 2019, 10.1093/sf/soz162), which summarizes: "automation ... has the potential to hide, speed up, and deepen discrimination while appearing neutral and even benevolent when compared to the racism of a previous era." *Bearing:* §3.1 and Table 1, row 1. *Already says part of it.* The best-known argument that Jim Crow–era exclusion recurs in facially neutral technical systems. It does not single out the literacy test. A referee will expect the paper to place its gate analogy beside the "New Jim Code". Should cite, after the author reads it.

## (c) Synthesis

**Has the paper's term been used before?** I found no use of "algorithmacy" outside the author's own writing in Crossref, OpenAlex (title and abstract), the arXiv API or the extended web search. The term is new in the published record. Its nearest cousin is not "algoracy" (Z's string, a misspelling) but *algocracy*, which has a sociology (Aneesh 2009) and a political philosophy (Danaher 2016) of its own. The two words differ in what they name: algocracy is a mode of rule; the paper's term is a competence demanded inside a three-party relation. The paper must make that distinction in §2.1, because a referee will make it otherwise.

**Has the analogy been made before?** Parts of it, in four places, none of which makes the paper's argument whole.

1. *At the level of skill:* Ng et al. (2021) build AI literacy "based on the adaptation of classic literacies"; Dasgupta and Hill (2023) place algorithmic literacies inside diSessa's computational literacy; Vee (2013) is the paper's acknowledged precedent. These are the uses the paper argues past.
2. *At the level of the promise:* Kamperman and Graff (2026) carry Graff's literacy myth to AI literacy; Cotter and Reisdorf (2020) and Gran et al. (2020) supply evidence that algorithmic knowledge tracks prior advantage, which is Graff's finding in a new medium. Stamboliev (2023) and Nichols and LeBlanc (2021) argue that literacy framings depoliticise a problem of power.
3. *At the level of the sponsor:* Jerasa and Jones (2025) already treat a platform algorithm as a Brandtian literacy sponsor that gatekeeps access to texts. This is the nearest approach to the paper's "positions", and it stops at access to reading matter.
4. *At the level of the gate:* Jones and Williams (2018) read the Jim Crow literacy test as a technology of disenfranchisement with a neutral surface, and Benjamin (2019) argues that Jim Crow exclusion returns in neutral-seeming code. Neither joins the literacy test to algorithmic intermediaries or AI literacy.

I found no work that does what the paper's §4 does: take several institutional positions from the literacy transition (gate, withheld competence, record, monopoly, promise) and map each onto an algorithmic intermediary standing between two parties, in Crossref, OpenAlex, arXiv, Consensus or the web search. That is a documented gap in those sources, not a proof of novelty; the bot walls kept me from full texts of several candidates (Danaher, Nichols and LeBlanc, Cotter 2021, Jerasa and Jones), and their abstracts do not make the move.

**What the literature adds to the paper's claims.**

- *Comprehension without leverage (§4.2)* now has four sources from the algorithmic-literacy field itself: Oeldorf-Hirsch and Neubaum (users "had little power to influence or leverage it", preprint p. 10), Swart (2021, abstract), Head et al. (2020, "resignation and indignation", PDF p. 16) and Stamboliev (2023, p. 225). These are stronger than an analogy because they come from the people who promote the literacy.
- *The medium answers back (§4.3)* has an empirical source in DeVito (2021): users "must constantly re-evaluate their folk theories."
- *The record over the voice (§4.1)* has a platform-specific mechanism in Cotter's (2021) "black box gaslighting".
- *The promise (§3.5, §4.1)* gains quantitative support (Cotter and Reisdorf 2020), with small effects that the paper should report honestly.
- *The term (§2.1)* faces two objections the paper does not yet answer by name: Nichols and LeBlanc's case against "literacy" as the frame for platform life, and the resemblance to "algocracy".

**What cuts against the paper.** Jerasa and Jones weaken the sentence that distinguishes the intermediary from Brandt's sponsor, since literacy scholars already count algorithms as sponsors; the paper's distinction must rest on the triad and the binding outcome, not on the claim that sponsors teach. Nichols and LeBlanc would ask why the paper keeps a literacy-shaped word at all. Danaher's argument shows that the comprehension problem is not new to political philosophy, so §4.2 should present Douglass as the earliest *statement* the paper knows, not as a discovery the field lacks.
