# Facet N4: responsibility, judging the past, and the ethics of intermediaries (2026-10-14)

Facet N4, Round 6, 2026-10-14. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.

## What this facet covers

The brief named four bodies of work: (a) structural responsibility (Young, McKeown, Barry and Ferracioli, Schwenkenbecher, and *BEQ* uses of Young for corporate duty); (b) judging the past (Moody-Adams, Walzer, Calhoun, Isaacs, the moral-luck and anachronism debate); (c) the ethics of intermediaries and platforms in business ethics and organization studies (Martin, Martin and Waldman, Buhmann et al., critics of Zuboff, Rahman, Kellogg et al., Curchod et al., Gal et al., Orlikowski); (d) consumer responsibilization (Giesler and Veresiu, Shamir). It also asked one question: **can Young's social connection model ground an operator's duty?** The reviewers (R2 Finding 5, R3 F6, `review/REVIEW_SUMMARY.md` item 3) said no. Section 1 reports what the texts say; the answer is in the synthesis.

The manuscript (`MANUSCRIPT_v2.md`) already cites Young (2006), Moody-Adams (1994), Walzer (1988), Giesler and Veresiu (2014), Rahman (2021) and Muldoon and Raekstad (2023). In §5.1 it now uses Young "only to say who else must act" and rests the operator's duty "on the simpler ground that it holds a position of power over people who cannot check it and professes not to harm them". Facet M4 already covered Young (2006, abstract), McKeown (2018), Fricker (2010), Rosen (2003) and Walzer; I do not repeat those entries.

## 1. Structural responsibility: Young and her readers

**Young (2005), `young2005responsibility`. Full** (pp. 709–726). This is Young's shortened version of the 2006 essay; footnote 1 (p. 709) calls it "a shortened version of a paper prepared for presentation at a conference on Global Justice … and forthcoming, Philosophy and Social Policy". It lacks the 2006 essay's final section on parameters of reasoning, which it defers: "That step will have to be taken at another time" (p. 726). Six points bear on the operator question.

1. *The definition the manuscript quotes.* "The social connection model of responsibility says that all agents who contribute by their actions to the structural processes that produce injustice have responsibilities to work to remedy these injustices" (p. 709). The manuscript quotes these words from Young (2006) with no page.
2. *The model does not replace liability, and liability reaches corporations.* Under the liability model, the responsible agent "can be a collective entity, such as a corporation, but when it is, that entity can be treated as a single agent for the purposes of assigning responsibility" (p. 717). "By proposing a social connection model of responsibility, I do not aim to replace or reject the liability model of responsibility" (p. 719).
3. *Role responsibility is the model's source.* "We also say, however, that people have certain responsibilities by virtue of their social roles or positions … What I propose as a social connection model of responsibility draws more on the latter usage of the term 'responsibility' than on the liability usage" (pp. 719–720).
4. *Firms are named, and not absolved.* Convicting the thugs and managers "does not absolve the multinational corporations from responsibility for the widespread nature of poor working conditions in the factories producing goods they market" (p. 720).
5. *Discharge is collective.* "The forward looking responsibility can be discharged only by joining with others in collective action … No one of us can do this on our own" (p. 723). "Responsibility from social connection, then, is ultimately political responsibility" (p. 723).
6. *Degrees by power.* "This does not necessarily mean that all who share responsibility have an equal responsibility. The power to influence the processes that produce unjust outcomes is an important factor distinguishing degrees of responsibility" (p. 725). She also makes victims share it: they "can be called to a responsibility they share with others in the structures" (p. 724).

*Bearing:* §5.1, last paragraph. *Supports* the manuscript's present use: on Young's own text the social connection model yields shared, political responsibility discharged with others (p. 723), so it cannot by itself impose a duty on the operator alone. The reviewers were right about that. It also *sharpens* the §5.1 sentence, because Young says three things the paragraph does not use: the liability model runs alongside hers and applies to a corporation as a single agent (pp. 717, 719); positions and roles carry responsibilities (p. 719); and power sets the degree (p. 725). A citation-level note: the manuscript's quotation matches Young's wording here exactly; for the 2006 version it needs a page (the Crossref record gives pp. 102–130; I could not open the 2006 text, so I cannot give the page).

**Young (2006), `young2006responsibility`. Record**, with quotations via Ferguson (2024). Two passages from the 2006 text that the 2005 version lacks. On the powerful: "Because the agents with the greatest power within social structures often have a vested interest in maintaining them as they are, however, external pressure on the powerful is often necessary to move these agents to action, and to prevent them from taking superficial steps rather than making serious changes" (Young 2006, p. 128, via Ferguson p. 5). On privilege: those privileged are "able to adapt to changed circumstances without suffering serious deprivation" (Young 2006, p. 128, via Ferguson p. 6). Ferguson also reports that the corporation-as-single-agent passage recurs at Young (2006, p. 116) and Young (2011, p. 97) (Ferguson p. 5, n. 5). *Bearing:* §5.1 and §5.5 (*supports*: Young's own text expects the powerful to resist, which is a reason the duties need an external enforcer, the point R2 Finding 5 makes about the legislature).

**Young (2004), `young2004responsibility`. Record.** *Journal of Political Philosophy* 12(4): 365–388. Ferguson quotes it for the corporation-as-collective-agent passage (Young 2004, p. 368, via Ferguson p. 5, n. 5). This is the article where Young first set the anti-sweatshop case; a referee in political philosophy may expect it beside 2006 and 2011.

**Young (2011), `young2011responsibility`. Record**, via Ferguson (2024) and Lin and Chen (2022). The four parameters of reasoning are power, privilege, interest and collective ability (Lin and Chen p. 18; Ferguson pp. 5–7, citing Young 2011, pp. 144–147). Victims have a "unique interest" and "share a responsibility" (Young 2011, p. 145, via Ferguson p. 6); without their input "well-meaning outsiders may inadvertently harm them in a different way" (Young 2011, p. 146, via Ferguson p. 6). Young's definition of structural injustice "in contrast to … that which is attributable to the specific actions and policies of states or other powerful institutions" (Young 2011, p. 45, via Wolff 2024, p. 14). *Bearing:* §5.1. The 2011 definition matters: what is attributable to "the specific actions and policies of … powerful institutions" falls outside structural injustice as Young defines it. An operator's design choice is such an action.

**Ferguson (2024), `ferguson2024forward`. Part** (abstract and pp. 1–9: introduction, the SCM, the four parameters, the "integrated approach"). *British Journal of Management* 35(3), CC BY. A management-journal critique of how political-CSR scholars use Young. His thesis: "Although Young's SCM serves as a key reference point in the PCSR literature, engagement with her work tends to be superficial and lacks critical engagement" (abstract, p. 1). He reads Young's power parameter in two ways, as capacity to act and as the duty of weaker actors "to challenge and pressurize more powerful actors" (p. 6). On scope, corporations' "power, capacity and proximity to supply chains" make their expected responsibilities "much greater", and they "have the capacity to implement governance processes such as developing supply chain polices, conducting impact assessments, undertaking due diligence and supplier audits, engaging with rightsholders" (p. 7). His main move, after Neuhäuser (2014) and Gould (2009), is an "integrated approach" that adds blame to the distribution of forward-looking responsibility: power counts "(i) in a positive sense, in terms of capacity to contribute to the amelioration of structural injustice; (ii) in a negative sense, in terms of reflecting a contribution to structural injustice in the first place" (p. 8), so corporations are "'leading agents' in relation to certain forms of structural injustice (Gould, 2009, p. 204)" (p. 8). He reports the critics: Goodin and Barry's "free pass" objection, "the social connection model had better have a backward-looking blame/liability conferring aspect to it for it to be morally coherent" (Goodin and Barry 2021, p. 340, via Ferguson p. 8); and Barry and Ferracioli's charge that Young "seems to rely on an unduly narrow conception of liability, based on an account of causation that is widely regarded as problematic" (Barry and Ferracioli 2013, p. 252, via Ferguson p. 8).

*Bearing:* §5.1. *Sharpens and challenges.* It is the clearest statement that business scholars cite Young "superficially", which a *BEQ* referee may say of the manuscript. It also offers the route the manuscript needs: an integrated account in which the operator's capacity and its contribution together make it a "leading agent" with heavier, firm-specific duties.

**Phillips and Schrempf-Stirling (2021), `phillips2021young`. Abstract.** *Philosophy of Management* 21(3): 315–336. The abstract names the platform case directly: "The rise of outsourcing, sub-contracting, and mobile app-based platforms have dramatically restructured relationships between and among economic actors … these 'not-quite-arm's-length' relationships present difficulties for conceptions of responsibility based on interrogating the past for specifiable actions by blameworthy actors." It finds that business scholars "have invoked Young's model to some good effect, though often superficially and uncritically". Ferguson quotes it: Young's work is taken at "face value" (p. 322) and "what is missing in these discussions is a systematic internal critique" (p. 324) (via Ferguson p. 2). *Bearing:* §5.1, §4 (*sharpens*): the work in the business-ethics literature closest to the manuscript's use of Young for platforms. Springer blocked the text.

**Tempels, Blok and Verweij (2020), `tempels2020injustice`. Part** (abstract, pp. 389–390, 400–406). *Business Ethics Quarterly* 30(3): 388–413. A *BEQ* precedent for using Young to assign responsibility to an industry. The authors argue that "aside from moral duties like not doing harm and respecting consumer autonomy, the food industry also has a responsibility for addressing the structural injustices involved" (abstract, p. 388). They accept that this responsibility "can only be discharged by engaging in collective action with other connected actors (Young 2011)" (p. 400), and then they name the gap the reviewers named: "Young does not clearly substantiate the claim that a connection to structural injustice also implies a responsibility to take action" (p. 401); the connections' "normative relevance … is underspecified" (p. 401, citing Barry and Ferracioli 2013); and the four parameters "are not clearly defined nor do they say anything about what this would entail in corporate practice" (p. 401). Their repair is three criteria, "capacity, benefit, and vulnerability" (abstract), which yield firm-specific actions: producers can reformulate products and improve "transparency concerning the nutritional value of certain products (e.g., labeling, front-of-pack nutrition information)" (p. 404), and responsibility is "more stringent at the moment it is dealing with consumer groups that are more constrained" (p. 405).

*Bearing:* §5.1 and §5.2–5.4. *Sharpens, and already says part of it.* It shows in the target journal how to get from Young to duties on one class of firm: supplement connection with capacity, benefit and vulnerability, and the duties become product-design duties. That is the shape of the manuscript's three duties. It also states the reviewers' objection in *BEQ*'s own pages (p. 401).

**McKeown (2024), `mckeown2024pure`. Part** (pp. 65–66, 78–83: §IV and conclusion). Chapter 4 of Browne and McKeown (eds.), *What Is Structural Injustice?* (OUP, open access). McKeown argues that "Young's conception of structural injustice is ontologically confused" (p. 65) and proposes three types. Structural injustice is "avoidable" when "the unjust outcomes of structural processes are foreseeable and there are agents with the capacity to remedy the injustice but they fail to do so", and "deliberate" when "the unjust outcomes are intended because powerful agents benefit from it so they deliberately perpetuate it, and these agents have the capacity to remedy it but they fail to do so" (p. 82). Her example is Young's own: garment multinationals "deliberately perpetuate sweatshop labour by exercising power within structures through lobbying, through setting industry standards themselves, through lax codes of corporate social responsibility, and through manipulation of consumers" (p. 81). The conclusion: "Young argued that all agents connected to structural injustice share a non-blameworthy political responsibility for it. On my account, agents that deliberately perpetuate structural injustice, or that have the capacity to alleviate it and fail to do so, bear moral responsibility" (p. 83).

*Bearing:* §5.1. *Supports, and is the strongest text for the operator.* It grounds a moral (not merely political) responsibility in the capacity to remedy, which is the property the manuscript attributes to operators ("they hold the positions and can change them"). It uses the same author the manuscript and M4 already rely on (McKeown 2018), so adopting it costs no new framework. The chapter is an edited extract from McKeown's monograph *With Power Comes Responsibility* (Bloomsbury, 2024; p. 65, n. 1), which I could not open.

**Wolff (2024), `wolff2024structural`. Part** (pp. 14–15). Chapter 1 of the same volume. "Now several critics have pointed out that Young's distinction between liability and structural injustice is too stark. It is possible that powerful individuals or corporate actors can deliberately create structures that trap and oppress many others (Powers and Faden 2019) … This is surely correct" (p. 15). *Bearing:* §5.1 (*supports*). A second, independent statement in the same volume that powerful corporate actors fall outside Young's no-blame model.

**Browne (2024), `browne2024untraceability`. Part** (pp. 85–86, 101–102). Chapter 5 of the same volume. Browne defends Young by making untraceability the defining feature of structural injustice, and she adds "a dynamic transition from structural to fault-based injustices which I argue Young's more 'static' account fails to accommodate" (p. 86): once harms can be traced, "we may bring those traceable actions squarely into the realm of moral responsibility" and then into "legal requirements" (p. 102). Her footnote names the reverse risk for algorithms: "the transition moves in the opposite direction, whereby our connections become more complex and less traceable, such as, for example, transference of tasks to the black box of decision-making characteristic of deep neural network operations" (p. 102, n. 21). *Bearing:* §5.1 and §5.3 (*sharpens*). On Browne's reading, the operator's design choice is traceable and so belongs to liability, not to structural injustice; and the operator who makes its system opaque pushes the harm back toward the untraceable, where no one is liable. That is a reason for the declared envelope.

**Lin and Chen (2022), `lin2022artificial`. Part** (abstract, §4.2, §5, §6.1; pp. 13–19). *Feminist Philosophy Quarterly* 8(3/4). The fullest application of Young to AI I found. They argue that "deferring to tech developers and companies for solutions to AI bias is inadequate and risks conferring unearned power to a small group of people" (p. 14) and that "nearly all participants in the social structure are responsible for AI bias … from the CEOs of tech companies and the engineers involved in the development process to the government and ordinary people" (p. 17). Among Young's parameters, "Agents with power are those with a great capacity to shape the social structure and should use this capacity accordingly; in the case of AI bias, leaders of tech companies and governments fall into this category" (p. 18). They also point to Zheng's role-ideal model as an alternative (p. 19). *Bearing:* §5.1 (*challenges*). Read straight, Young's model spreads responsibility to users and warns against putting the remedy in the operator's hands, the opposite emphasis from §5. The manuscript should show it knows this reading.

**Schwenkenbecher (2021), `schwenkenbecher2020structural`. Abstract.** *Journal of Applied Philosophy* 38(1): 23–39 (online 2020). "Ordinary citizens can have massively shared obligations to address structural injustice through distributive action, but, ultimately, such obligations are 'collective' only in a fairly weak sense" (abstract). Lin and Chen cite it for "the lack of specificity on shared responsibility" (p. 17, n. 11). *Bearing:* §5.1 (*challenges* any appeal to diffuse shared duties); optional.

**Barry and Ferracioli (2013), `barry2013young`. Record**, via Ferguson and Tempels et al. (quoted above). *Criminal Justice Ethics* 32(3): 247–257. The standard indeterminacy critique. PhilPapers and Taylor & Francis both returned 403.

**Neuhäuser (2014), `neuhauser2014structural`; Goodin and Barry (2021), `goodin2021responsibility`; Zheng (2018), `zheng2018what`. Record**, with quotations via Ferguson (Neuhäuser: "proving participation in a causal chain" suffices and "negligence … [would] be enough to ground blameworthy complicity – at least in some cases", pp. 243, 248, via Ferguson p. 8) and Lin and Chen (Zheng's role-ideal model, via p. 19). Each is a published route from Young to an agent-specific duty: Neuhäuser by integrating blame, Goodin and Barry by insisting the model needs a blame-conferring aspect, Zheng by grounding duties in social roles. Zheng was blocked at Springer and UNL.

## 2. Judging the past

**Moody-Adams (1994), `moodyadams1994culture`. Part** (pp. 294, 296, 298, 304, re-checked in the arm's text layer). The three quotations in §2.3 are accurate in wording and page: "base hypotheses about what some agent(s) could not do solely on evidence of what the agent(s) did not do" (p. 294); "choosing not to know what one can and should know" (p. 296); the "standards of their own day" phrase is on p. 298. **One correction for the author.** §2.3 quotes: "A culture," she writes, "cannot be an 'agent' of anything" (p. 304). The text reads: "A culture—independent of agents who perpetuate culture—cannot be an 'agent' of anything" (p. 304). The manuscript drops the qualifying phrase without an ellipsis. ## 3. The ethics of intermediaries and platforms

**Martin (2019), `martin2019ethical`. Part** (abstract; introduction, PDF pp. 1–2; "Accountability and Inscrutable Algorithms" and "Why Firms are Responsible for the Algorithms they Develop", PDF pp. 9–10; opening of the conclusion, PDF p. 10). *Journal of Business Ethics* 160(4): 835–850, open access. This is the *JBE* argument for an operator's duty, and it does not use Young. The abstract: "firms developing algorithms are accountable for designing how large a role individual will be permitted to take in the subsequent algorithmic decision … if an algorithm is designed to preclude individuals from taking responsibility within a decision, then the designer of the algorithm should be held accountable for the ethical implications of the algorithm in use" (PDF p. 1 = p. 835). The grounding is two-part: an obligation "created when the firm willingly sells into the decision-making context and based on the unique knowledge and abilities of the firm designing and developing the algorithm" (PDF p. 2 = p. 836). On opacity: "inscrutable algorithms—designed to be difficult to understand and argued to be hard to explain—may force greater accountability on the designer to own the algorithmic decision since their design of the algorithm has precluded anyone else from taking on a larger role in the decision when in use" (PDF p. 10 = p. 844). On entry: "by willingly creating an algorithm that works in a value-laden and particular manner, firms voluntarily become a party to the decision system" (PDF p. 10); and, in social-contract terms (Donaldson and Dunfee), "If a company does not wish to abide by the norms of the decision … then the firm should not be in that business and not sell the algorithm into that particular context" (PDF p. 10).

*Bearing:* §5.1, §5.3, §5.4. *Supports, and already says part of it.* Martin gives the operator's duty the ground §5.1 gestures at ("holds a position of power … and professes not to harm them") in a form *BEQ* and *JBE* readers know: voluntary entry into a decision context plus unique knowledge. Her inscrutability argument is the declared envelope's logic, since making the system opaque increases the operator's accountability. Her "who-does-what" delegation is close to counter-delegation. The manuscript does not cite her, and a business-ethics referee would expect it to.

**Martin (2019b), `martin2019designing`. Abstract** (summary box, p. 129). *MIS Quarterly Executive*, pp. 129–142, 2019 (Crossref gives pages but no volume or issue; I have not verified them). "By creating inscrutable algorithms, which are difficult to understand or govern in use, developers may voluntarily take on accountability for the role of the algorithm in a decision" (p. 129). Optional: the practitioner version of the above.

**Martin and Waldman (2022), `martin2022algorithmic`. Abstract.** *JBE* 183(3): 653–670. A factorial-vignette study: "many of the procedural governance mechanisms in practice today, such as notices and impact statements, do not lead to algorithmic decisions being perceived as more legitimate in general … Yet, robust governance, such as offering an appeal process, can create a legitimacy dividend for decisions with bad outcomes" (abstract). *Bearing:* §5.3 (*supports*: notices do not help, which matches the manuscript's case against disclosure) and §5.4 (*supports* a contestable route). Empirical, not normative; one sentence.

**Buhmann, Paßmann and Fieseler (2020), `buhmann2019managing`. Abstract** (RePEc). *JBE* 163(2): 265–280. Accountability for algorithms "remains an elusive ideal due to the opacity and fluidity of algorithms"; organizations' engagement "may be manipulative, adaptive, or moral", and under heavy opacity "the emphasis of engagement should shift to a rational communication process", judged by "participation, comprehension, multivocality, and responsiveness" (abstract). *Bearing:* §5.3 (*challenges*): a discourse-ethics answer to opacity that puts the remedy in communication rather than in a binding envelope. A referee from the Habermasian PCSR school would raise it; the manuscript's reply is Lovett's point that predictability without control does not end domination.

**Helberger, Pierson and Poell (2018), `helberger2018governing`. Part** (pp. 1–3). *The Information Society* 34(1): 1–14. "The power relationship between users and platforms is unequal, not least because of the opacity of platform operations" (p. 2). "Neither the platform nor the users can be held fully responsible. The solution, we will argue, lies in shared responsibility and a division of labor between the platform and users, or what we will refer to as cooperative responsibility" (p. 3). But "platforms have an obligation to create the conditions that allow individual users to comply with their responsibilities" (p. 3), and they quote Fahlquist: "The greater the extent to which institutional agents have taken their forward-looking responsibility, the greater the extent to which it is reasonable to ascribe both backward-looking and forward-looking responsibility to individuals" (Fahlquist 2009, p. 119, via p. 3). *Bearing:* §5.1 and §3.5/§4 row 5 (*sharpens*). The Fahlquist principle orders the duties: the operator's duty comes first, and only once it is met may a user be held responsible. That gives the manuscript a principled reply to responsibilization.

**Orlikowski and Scott (2014), `orlikowski2014what`. Part** (abstract; discussion, accepted-manuscript pp. 31–34). *Organization Science* 25(3): 868–891. A comparison of the AA hotel inspectorate and TripAdvisor. Under the AA, hoteliers "expect a professional relationship with inspectors who are accountable for the assessments they make … hoteliers interact with named and responsible assessors as well as an established and reputable institution that stands behind the valuation" (AM p. 32). "Unlike the AA, which publishes its formula (e.g., the publicly available 65-page Quality Standards), the details of TripAdvisor's Popularity Index remain obscure. The company declines to discuss its ranking algorithm and fraud detection tools, arguing this will prompt 'gaming'" (AM p. 34). *Bearing:* §4.4, §5.3 (*supports, and already says part of it*). It is an organization-theory case of the manuscript's contrast between a named, accountable third party with a published standard and an intermediary with an undisclosed one, and a triad (hotel, guest, rater). It is the closest empirical parallel I found to the declared envelope.

**Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen and Neysen (2020), `curchod2019working`. Abstract.** *ASQ* 65(3): 644–676. From 77 eBay sellers: power asymmetries "at two levels: the transactional level between sellers and customers and the governance level between sellers and eBay"; customer evaluations create monitoring "through the construction of visibility gaps between buyers and sellers and through an implicit coalition between buyers and the platform owner, who join together in the evaluation procedures"; and "by mediating and objectifying relations, algorithms reproduce power asymmetries among the different categories of actors" (abstract). *Bearing:* §2.2 and §4 (*sharpens*, and challenges one claim). The manuscript says the intermediary's reading "binds the rider, the employer, or the lender as well, and neither flank can check it" (§2.2). Curchod et al. find the platform in "an implicit coalition" with one flank. The triad may be asymmetric in a way the manuscript does not yet say.

**Rahman (2021), `rahman2021invisible`. Abstract** (existing key). The abstract supports §4.2's account: freelancers "experienced this evaluation as a form of control but could not align their actions with its criteria because they could not clearly identify those criteria", an "invisible cage" in which "the criteria for success and changes to those criteria are unpredictable". **A check for the author.** §4.2 says the platform "replaced a visible rating with an opaque evaluation". The abstract says the platform "implemented an opaque evaluation algorithm to meaningfully differentiate between freelancers' rating scores". "Replaced" may be right, but the abstract does not show it; please check against the article.

**Kellogg, Valentine and Christin (2020), `kellogg2020algorithms`. Abstract** (existing key; OpenAlex abstract). The "6 Rs": "employers can use algorithms to direct workers by restricting and recommending, evaluate workers by recording and rating, and discipline workers by replacing and rewarding"; and "workers are individually and collectively resisting algorithmic control through a set of emerging tactics we call algoactivism" (abstract). This verifies the term "algoactivism", which `M2_algorithm_studies.bib` marked as unverified. *Bearing:* §4 (*supports*; the standard review a management referee expects).

**Gal, Jensen and Stein (2020), `gal2020breaking`. Abstract** (Semantic Scholar). People analytics "can create a vicious cycle of ethical challenges - algorithmic opacity, datafication, and nudging - which limit people's ability to cultivate their virtue and flourish"; remedies include "reframing people analytics as a fallible companion technology, introducing new organizational roles and practices, and adopting alternative technology design principles" (abstract). *Bearing:* §5 (optional; a virtue-ethics neighbour whose remedies are organizational design, like the manuscript's).

**Bhargava and Assadi (2023), `bhargava2023hiring`. Part** (abstract and introduction, pp. 201–203). *BEQ* 34(2): 201–230 (online 2023). Building on Scanlon's value of choice, they argue that interviews "generate commonly overlooked kinds of noninstrumental value" and "should thus not be entirely replaced by algorithms, however sophisticated algorithms ultimately become at predicting performance and fit" (p. 201). *Bearing:* §5.2 (*supports*). A *BEQ* argument for a non-algorithmic route in hiring, which is the manuscript's example of structural refusal. Not in `references.bib` or the manuscript (the earlier draft cited it; R1 lists it).

**Scherer, Neesham, Schoeneborn and Scholz (2023), `scherer2023guest`. Part** (abstract, pp. 409, 414, 432). *BEQ* 33(3): 409–439, the guest editors' introduction to the special issue on "organized immaturity". They define immaturity as what "arises when individuals defer or delegate autonomous reasoning to external authorities, including the authority of sociotechnological systems" (p. 414), take Zuboff's surveillance capitalism as a recent statement (p. 409), and propose "disorganizing immaturity" and "organizing maturity" as counters (p. 409). Among the countermeasures they invite study of "the capacities of state and nonstate actors to educate individuals and collectives to critically deal with digital technologies—which is sometimes also referred to as digital literacy or data literacy" (p. 432). *Bearing:* §1, §3.5 and §5 (*challenges and sharpens*). This is *BEQ*'s own recent frame for the paper's subject, and it lists literacy among the remedies, which the manuscript's fifth practice argues against. A *BEQ* referee is likely to know this special issue. The manuscript should place itself against it in a sentence: the special issue treats immaturity as a condition to be cured by maturity and literacy; the manuscript treats the positions held by the institution as the problem.

**Couldry and Mejias (2018), `couldry2018data`. Part** (accepted manuscript pp. 1–9). *Television & New Media* 20(4): 336–349. They use colonialism "not as a mere metaphor, nor as an echo or simple continuation of historic forms of territorial colonialism", but for "a new form of colonialism distinctive of the 21st century" (AM p. 1), and they disclaim "loose analogies to the content or form, let alone the physical violence, of historical colonialism" (AM p. 5). Their central parallel is a document: the Spanish *Requerimiento*, "read in Spanish by conquistadors to a non-Spanish speaking audience … to demand their simple acceptance", set beside "similarly incomprehensible documents called Terms of Service, which contain outlandish appropriative claims by corporations" (AM p. 8). *Bearing:* §3.3 and §4 row 3, and §1's framing of the analogy (*already says part of it*). The paper's record-over-voice practice includes a treaty read across a language gap (Waitangi), and Couldry and Mejias already draw the colonial-text-to-terms-of-service parallel. Their care about the analogy's limits (AM p. 5) is the move §1 makes ("a bounded analogy"). A referee in critical data studies would expect the citation. Their book (`couldry2020costs`, Record) is the fuller statement; I did not open it.

**Critics of Zuboff.** I looked for peer-reviewed critiques of *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* in OpenAlex and Crossref and found reviews and an interview, not a standard critique I could verify. Couldry and Mejias (AM p. 1) place Zuboff (2015) among accounts that miss "a wider frame"; Scherer et al. (p. 409) adopt her. The manuscript does not cite Zuboff, and I do not recommend adding her unless a referee asks.

## 4. Consumer responsibilization

**Giesler and Veresiu (2014), `giesler2014creating`. Part** (pp. 840–841, 851, in the M4 copy). The manuscript's claim is accurate: the "financially literate consumer" is the fourth of their cases ("A fourth responsible-consumer type cultivated at the WEF is the financially literate consumer", p. 851). They take responsibilization from Shamir: a "technique of the self that sets into action a reflexive subjectivity deemed suitable to partake in the deployment of horizontal authority and one which willingly bears the consequences of its actions" (Shamir 2008, p. 4, via Giesler and Veresiu p. 841), and their mechanism is the P.A.C.T. routine, "personalization, authorization, capabilization, and transformation" (p. 840).

**Shamir (2008), `shamir2008age`. Record** (existing key), quoted via Giesler and Veresiu above. Giesler and Veresiu also note that Shamir "describes corporations as one target of responsibilization" (p. 841), a point the manuscript could use: responsibilization runs at firms too, through voluntary codes.

*Bearing of section 4:* §3.5 and §4 row 5 (*supports*; already cited). Helberger et al.'s Fahlquist principle (section 3) is the normative counterpart: individuals may be held responsible only after the institution has met its own forward-looking responsibility.

## Synthesis

**Can Young's social connection model ground an operator's duty?** On Young's own text, not by itself. The model assigns responsibility that is shared, political and "can be discharged only by joining with others in collective action" (Young 2005, p. 723), and it reaches users and victims too (p. 724). Lin and Chen apply it to AI and conclude that "nearly all participants" bear responsibility and that deferring to companies "risks conferring unearned power to a small group of people" (pp. 14, 17). Tempels et al., in *BEQ*, say that Young "does not clearly substantiate the claim that a connection to structural injustice also implies a responsibility to take action" and that her parameters say nothing about "what this would entail in corporate practice" (p. 401). The reviewers' "no" stands for the model taken alone.

The texts also show three things the reviewers did not say.

1. **Young keeps the liability model for corporations.** She does not replace liability (p. 719), she treats a corporation as a single agent under it (p. 717), and she defines structural injustice against harms "attributable to the specific actions and policies of … powerful institutions" (2011, p. 45, via Wolff p. 14). An operator's design choice is such an action. On her own terms the operator's duty belongs mostly to the liability and role side, not to the social connection side. Browne's traceability reading (pp. 86, 102) points the same way.
2. **The literature since Young supplies agent-specific duties.** McKeown (2024) gives moral responsibility to "agents that deliberately perpetuate structural injustice, or that have the capacity to alleviate it and fail to do so" (p. 83). Ferguson (2024) and Neuhäuser (2014) make powerful corporations "leading agents" by combining capacity and contribution (Ferguson p. 8). Tempels et al. (2020) derive firm-specific product and labelling duties from capacity, benefit and vulnerability (pp. 404–405). Wolff (2024) says Young's line is "too stark" for corporate actors who "deliberately create structures" (p. 15).
3. **The business-ethics ground for the operator already exists and is not Young.** Martin (2019) grounds the developer's accountability in voluntary entry into a decision context and unique knowledge, and holds that designing users out of the decision, or making the system inscrutable, increases the designer's accountability (PDF pp. 2, 10). That is close to §5.1's "simpler ground", and it is in *JBE*.

So the manuscript's present §5.1 is defensible but thin. It keeps Young for "who else must act", which is the use Young's text supports, and it needs a named ground for the operator, which Martin (2019) and McKeown (2024) supply. Martin argues from the decision context and McKeown from capacity to remedy; together they meet R2's request to "name the duty-bearer".

**Two empirical findings bear on the triad.** Curchod et al. find the platform in "an implicit coalition" with buyers against sellers; Orlikowski and Scott find that an accountable, named third party with a published standard (the AA) gave way to an intermediary that will not disclose its formula. The first complicates §2.2's claim that the reading binds both flanks equally. The second is a worked case of the absent independent party in §4.4.

**Prior work that already makes part of the argument.** Couldry and Mejias (2018) draw the colonial-document-to-terms-of-service parallel. Scherer et al. (2023) frame delegated judgment as organized immaturity in *BEQ*, but recommend literacy, which the manuscript argues against. Martin (2019) makes the operator-accountability argument from design. Orlikowski and Scott (2014) document the accountable-assessor versus opaque-algorithm contrast. I found no work, in Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar or the web search, that uses Young's model, or a successor such as McKeown's typology, to ground the duties of an operator whose system binds two parties at once.

## Recommendations

### Must engage (a referee would object otherwise)

1. **Martin (2019)**, §5.1, the sentence "The operator's duty rests on the simpler ground that it holds a position of power over people who cannot check it and professes not to harm them." Add the business-ethics ground: the operator entered the decision context voluntarily and alone knows the design, and on Martin's argument a system designed to keep users from taking responsibility, or made inscrutable, places the accountability on its designer (PDF pp. 2, 10). Cite again at §5.3 for the envelope (inscrutability increases accountability).
2. **McKeown (2024)** (and, if space allows, Ferguson 2024), §5.1, the sentence "Young's model is forward-looking and shared; it assigns no blame, and I use it only to say who else must act." Add that later work in the same tradition assigns moral responsibility to agents "that have the capacity to alleviate it and fail to do so" (McKeown 2024, p. 83), which is the operator's position. This answers R3 F6 and R2 Finding 5 with the literature the paper already uses.
3. **Young's own text on liability and corporations**, same paragraph. Cite Young's statement that her model does not replace the liability model (2005, p. 719; for 2006, check the page in the SPP text) so that a reader does not take the paper to say that Young rules out an operator's own duty. Add the page to the existing Young (2006) quotation.
4. **Scherer et al. (2023)**, §1 (the opening paragraph on AI literacy as the prescribed safeguard) or §3.5. One sentence placing the paper against *BEQ*'s organized-immaturity special issue, which proposes "digital literacy or data literacy" among the countermeasures (p. 432).
5. **The Moody-Adams quotation, §2.3** ("A culture," she writes, "cannot be an 'agent' of anything" (p. 304)). Restore "independent of agents who perpetuate culture" or mark the omission. This is a correction, not a new source.

### Should engage

6. **Tempels et al. (2020)**, §5.1, after the Young sentence: *BEQ* precedent for deriving firm-specific duties (capacity, benefit, vulnerability) from a structural-injustice starting point (pp. 401, 404–405). It also states the reviewers' objection in the venue's own pages.
7. **Orlikowski and Scott (2014)**, §4.4, where the paper says no independent party with binding authority stands at the point of decision; and §5.3 on declared envelopes: the AA's published 65-page standard and named, accountable inspectors against TripAdvisor's undisclosed algorithm (AM pp. 32, 34).
8. **Curchod et al. (2020)**, §2.2, the sentence "the intermediary's reading binds the rider, the employer, or the lender as well, and neither flank can check it." Qualify it: the platform may form "an implicit coalition" with one flank (abstract).
9. **Couldry and Mejias (2018)**, §3.3 or §4 row 3 (the record): they already pair a colonial document read in a language its hearers could not understand with terms of service (AM p. 8). Cite as prior work and note the difference: the manuscript asks what the record-holder owes, not what is extracted.
10. **Helberger et al. (2018)** with Fahlquist's principle, §3.5/§4 row 5 or §6 ("Users should learn"): individuals may fairly be held responsible only after the institution has met its own forward-looking responsibility (p. 3).
11. **Bhargava and Assadi (2023)**, §5.2, after "For hiring, it means a human assessment available on request and weighed on the same footing": a *BEQ* argument that interviews "should thus not be entirely replaced by algorithms" (p. 201).
12. **Lin and Chen (2022)**, §5.1 or §6 (objections): the strongest statement that Young's model spreads responsibility to users and warns against relying on companies (pp. 14, 17). Name it and answer it with McKeown or Martin.
13. **Rahman (2021)**, §4.2: check "replaced a visible rating" against the article.
