### 2. Comparisons of the literacy or print transition with AI

**Qadri, Prabhakaran and Denton (2026), `qadri2026ai` (existing key; upgraded from abstract). Full** (arXiv:2609.31482v1, 25 Sept 2026, 4 pp. plus references; DataCite record matches title and authors). ICML 2026 workshop paper (Culture × AI). They argue that scholars reach for the printing press, electricity or steam as "gestalt analogies" that "fail to isolate the precise technical and social properties that carry the comparison" (PDF p. 1). The abstract states the method the paper uses in §4.1 almost word for word: "these comparisons are typically imprecise about which property of the technology carries the comparison, and imprecise analogies produce imprecise governance by designing interventions against the wrong property of the system" (PDF p. 1). They split consequences into "structural" and "contingent" (p. 2), and on accountability write that under generative synthesis "authority is decoupled from accountability" and "recovering something like contestability requires sustained institutional effort against a system that produces none of it on its own" (p. 4). Their cases are discovery and synthesis (index against LLM, encyclopedia against LLM), not standing, literacy or third parties.
*Bearing:* §4.1, first paragraph. **Already says it** for the method sentence: "The property matters more than the resemblance: an analogy that does not say what carries it invites governance aimed at the wrong feature of a system." The manuscript does not cite them. Facet X (entry 20) found the abstract and drew the same test; the full text adds the structural/contingent split, which fits the "Where the comparison is weak" column of Table 1.

**Posada, Weller and Wong (2021), `posada2021we`. Part** (arXiv:2104.12731v1; abstract, §1, §3.2 to §3.3 and conclusion, PDF pp. 1-7). AIES 2021, pp. 864-872. They argue that print, like datafication, "disrupted extant power arrangements, leading to decentralization, and triggered a recentralization of power by new actors better adapted to leveraging the new technology" (PDF p. 1). On the present: "As in the case of the printing press, the initial euphoria of a technology capable of breaking up powers in the status quo shifted the distribution of power to a new equilibrium" (p. 6), and "the corporations have seized power by controlling the currency of power: data" (p. 7). They close by warning against "thinking about datafication as just a faster printing press" (p. 7). Their print history is Eisenstein, Anderson and Russian printers in 1905; they do not discuss licensing monopolies, literacy tests, anti-literacy law or records, and they make no argument from internal standards.
*Bearing:* §4.1, the fourth row ("The means") and the paragraph that leaves it open. *Sharpens:* they claim the recentralization the paper declines to establish, so the open row should cite them as a claim the paper does not adopt. Also §1, contribution 2: a print-to-AI comparison on power grounds already exists, at the level of the medium, not the positions.

**Hänska (2021), `hanska2021communication`. Abstract** (the *European Journal of Communication* book note, 36(5): 541, read through its Consensus record, which reproduces the note) and Record (Routledge book, DOI 10.4324/9780429280795; chapters "Communications Against Domination", pp. 63-80, "Print Against Domination", pp. 83-93, and "Platforms and Networked Non-domination", pp. 94-107, in Crossref). Not open access; LSE's repository holds no full text. The note says Part II applies a normative framework to "broadcast communication, the Internet and networked communications, and the increasing integration of AI technologies into our communications systems" (quoting the blurb) and lists chapters on "print against domination; platforms and networked non-domination; and machine intelligence and discursive control".
*Bearing:* §2.2 and §1. This is the only work I found whose title joins domination, the printing press and algorithmic media. From the note, its subject is justice in communication (who may speak and be heard), not literacy as a condition of standing or an intermediary between two parties. **Unread.** The paper cannot claim the domination-plus-print-plus-algorithms move is new until someone reads chapters 6 to 8.

**Wheeler (2024), `wheeler2024from`. Record.** *From Gutenberg to Google and on to AI* (Brookings Institution Press), DOI 10.5040/9780815752806; chapters include "The Biggest Thing Since Gutenberg". Qadri et al. cite Wheeler's Brookings essay as an example of the Gutenberg analogy (PDF p. 5). *Bearing:* §4.1, optional; a policy-world instance of the gestalt analogy.

**Burrell (2024), `burrell2024automated`. Part** (First Monday 29(4); abstract, §1 to §3.1, PDF pp. 1-11 of the journal's PDF). Not a literacy comparison, but the nearest domination account of automated decision-making outside Muldoon and Raekstad. She proposes "the domination of humans by algorithms, i.e., the denial of self-determination and control, and specifically limitations on opportunities to voice concerns and have them be heard" as the definition of algorithmic injustice (PDF p. 2), drawing on Young, not Pettit. Two passages bear directly on the paper. On literacy as remedy, she lists "a mass effort to train humans in computational literacy" as one response and warns that ensuring self-determination "should not be simply a way that system designers and business firms avoid their own responsibility by delegating it to consumers" (p. 11). On refusal, she reports that the US Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights "includes 'human alternatives, consideration, and fallback' as one of five rights that should be preserved for people subject to AI decision-making" (p. 11).
*Bearing:* §2.2 third clarification ("the concept has already reached the workplace and the platform"): **supports**; a second domination account to cite beside Muldoon and Raekstad, with a different lineage (Young). §5.2 ("The idea has antecedents"): **already says part of it**: the Blueprint's "human alternatives ... and fallback" is a published antecedent of structural refusal that the manuscript does not name. I did not open the Blueprint; the author should cite it from the primary text.

### 3. Business ethics and management journals, 2018-2026

#### 3a. The BEQ special issue on organized immaturity (BEQ 33:3, July 2023)

**Scherer, Neesham, Schoeneborn and Scholz (2023), `scherer2023guest`. Part** (abstract; pp. 409-416, 424-427, 432). The guest editors define organized immaturity as "the reduction of individual capacities for public use of reason constrained by sociotechnological systems" (p. 409) and, after Kant, as a state "when individuals defer or delegate autonomous reasoning to external authorities, including the authority of sociotechnological systems" (p. 414). Their two remedies are "disorganizing immaturity", which protects negative freedom through resistance (p. 424), and "organizing maturity", which strengthens positive freedom (p. 426). Under the state's role they list first "strengthening basic, higher, and continuing education by informing citizens of all ages and preparing them to confront and manage the dark aspects of social media and ICT influences", then regulation including "effective appeal processes", data property rights, support for watchdog NGOs, and public forums (p. 427). Their research agenda asks for studies of "the capacities of state and nonstate actors to educate individuals and collectives to critically deal with digital technologies—which is sometimes also referred to as digital literacy or data literacy" (p. 432).
*Bearing:* §1 (the third paragraph's last two sentences) and §5.1. **Challenges and sharpens.** BEQ's own recent program frames the harm as a loss of the person's capacity and puts education first among the state's remedies, beside regulation. The paper's claim that "an ethics that educates the user addresses the wrong party" is a direct position against "organizing maturity" as education, and agrees with the special issue's regulatory list. A BEQ referee will expect the paper to say so.

**Harracá, Castelló and Gawer (2023), `harraca2023how`. Part** (City accepted manuscript; abstract, introduction, the section on platforms as organizations and on power accumulation, AAM pp. 1-9 and 18). BEQ 33(3): 440-472. Platform owners "act as intermediaries to bring together (match) and facilitate exchanges between different groups of users" (AAM p. 7), and once a market tips the role "provides the platform owner with a position from which they can charge supra-monopoly prices and define the rules of the market, including who can access it and how the transactions occur", so that platforms operate "as gatekeepers" (AAM p. 8). On competence: "the algorithmic logic becomes a norm that everyone needs to learn to play by or with ... it is somehow defined behind closed doors by the platform owners" (AAM p. 18).
*Bearing:* §2.1 (the intermediary standing between two parties) and §4.3 (the rule that learners chase is set by the owner). **Supports.** It gives the paper a BEQ citation for the multi-sided intermediary and for the owner-defined norm that users must learn. Its framework is Bourdieu, not domination, and its harm is autonomy erosion, not a binding decision between two parties.

**Schade (2023), `schade2023dark`. Abstract** (OpenAlex; CBS repository file blocked). BEQ 33(3): 473-501. Argues from GDPR-mandated transparency practice that transparency "itself risks producing new forms of organized immaturity by facilitating user ignorance, manipulation, and loss of control of personal data" (abstract).
*Bearing:* §5.3 first paragraph ("disclosure fails"). **Supports**, from inside BEQ, with GDPR evidence. Must be read before it is quoted beyond the abstract.

**Scherer and Neesham (2023), `scherer2023organized`. Abstract** (SAGE blocked). *Organization Theory* 4(3). Names "infantilization, reductionism and totalization" as the patterns that organize immaturity and sets out "individual abilities and public deliberation principles" as the counter (abstract). *Bearing:* optional; the theory behind the special issue.

#### 3b. Explanation, transparency and legitimacy

**Kim and Routledge (2022), `kim2022why`. Part** (abstract, pp. 75-76). BEQ 32(1): 75-102. Cited by the manuscript (§1, §4.2) but **absent from `references.bib`**; this entry supplies it. They argue that informed consent understood "as an assurance of trust for incomplete transactions" means "the rationale of informed consent already entails a right to ex post explanation" (p. 75).

**Cordasco and Véliz (2026), `cordasco2026accuracy`. Abstract** (Springer blocked). JBE, online 13 April 2026. Explanations that serve self-advocacy "must be reliable (truth-tracking) and verifiable (open to independent check)"; in open-ended decisions these "conflict with accuracy", so "the self-advocacy interest cannot by itself ground a general right" (abstract).
*Bearing:* §4.2 ("Kim and Routledge ... ground a right to explanation"). **Sharpens.** A 2026 JBE paper argues the right to explanation is weaker than Kim and Routledge hold, on different grounds from the paper's. The phrase "open to independent check" is close to the paper's demand for an independent party.

**Martin and Waldman (2022), `martin2022are`. Abstract** (Springer blocked). JBE 183(3): 653-670. A factorial vignette study finds that "notices and impact statements, do not lead to algorithmic decisions being perceived as more legitimate", while "robust governance, such as offering an appeal process, can create a legitimacy dividend for decisions with bad outcomes" (abstract).
*Bearing:* §5.3 (disclosure) and §5.4 (a body to hear disputes). **Supports**, with evidence the paper does not have. Perceived legitimacy is not the paper's standard, which the citation should say.

**Martin (2019), `martin2019ethical`. Abstract** (Springer blocked). JBE 160(4): 835-850. "Firms developing algorithms are accountable for designing how large a role individuals will be permitted to take in the subsequent algorithmic decision", and "if an algorithm is designed to preclude individuals from taking responsibility within a decision, then the designer of the algorithm should be held accountable" (abstract).
*Bearing:* §5.1, "The duties fall first on operators—the firms that design and run the intermediary". **Already says part of it.** The most-cited JBE paper on algorithmic accountability places the duty on the firm that designs the decision's division of roles. The paper's duties extend Martin's "who-does-what" to the affected parties' roles; it must cite her.

**Buhmann, Paßmann and Fieseler (2020), `buhmann2020managing`. Record.** JBE 163(2): 265-280. Optional; algorithmic accountability as reputation management and discourse.

#### 3c. Hiring, contestability, separation of powers

**Bhargava and Assadi (2024), `bhargava2024hiring`. Part** (abstract, pp. 201-202, §3 at pp. 214-217). BEQ 34(2): 201-230 (online 2023). Against the "algorithmic threat" to interviews, they argue from Scanlon's value of choice that "interviews should thus not be entirely replaced by algorithms, however sophisticated algorithms ultimately become at predicting performance and fit" (p. 201). The noninstrumental loss is symbolic: "the symbolic value of choice is what is lost when a person for whom it would be appropriate (in a given social context) to make a certain decision is precluded from making that decision" (p. 216). Their chooser is mainly the organization and its members choosing colleagues; the candidate's standing is secondary.
*Bearing:* §5.2, "For hiring, it means a human assessment available on request and weighed on the same footing." **Supports**, from BEQ, on a different ground (value of choice, not domination). The paper should cite them and note that their argument protects the employer's choice, while structural refusal protects the applicant's route.

**Lindblom (2019), `lindblom2019consent`. Part** (abstract, pp. 189-191, 199-206). BEQ 29(2): 189-211 (online 2018). Because employment contracts are incomplete, "consent to authority has been given under ignorance, and, therefore, ... authority cannot be justified by consent"; contestability, after Pettit, "demands transparency, channels for voice, and a forum where contestations can be evaluated" (p. 189). From the separation of powers he argues that unions are "uniquely suited to implement contestability, since they are organized outside of the employer's domain of authority" (p. 189). Against the company union: "it would seem foolhardy to attempt to handle this problem in a way that makes us dependent on the power of the employer" (p. 205). He says his argument "does not use the ideal of non-domination as a premise" (p. 200).
*Bearing:* three places. §4.4, "independence at the first instance" and "the separation of roles": **already says it** for employment; the platform's own first reviewer is Lindblom's company union. §5.4, "a body that can bind the operator": **supports**. §5.6, "The users consented": **supports**; consent to incomplete terms is consent under ignorance. This is the BEQ paper a referee is likeliest to say the paper should have found.

**Lyons, Velloso and Miller (2021), `lyons2021conceptualising` (existing key; upgraded from abstract and introduction). Part** (arXiv:2103.01774v1, PDF pp. 9-16). Submissions to Australia's AI Ethics Framework asked for more than contest: one proposed "a ninth principle covering independent oversight. Contestability and accountability require an independent third party to ensure a fair and just outcome" (PDF p. 9, quoting the Australian Library and Information Association). The authors warn that contestability alone "would place too much of a burden on decision subjects, who need to initiate contestation" (p. 9). Submissions also "highlight the need for general education about AI to empower individuals to contest", as the government's role (p. 14).
*Bearing:* §4.4, "no independent party with binding authority stands at the point where an algorithmic intermediary's reading becomes a decision". **Already says part of it**: the demand for an independent third party is on the public record (and is evidence for benchmark 1, contemporary objection, in §5.1). The education passage is another instance of the promise (§3.5, §4.1 row 5).

**Alfrink, Keller, Kortuem and Doorn (2023), `alfrink2023contestable` (existing key; upgraded from record). Part** (TU Delft repository copy of the online-first version; §2 and §6.1.4, PDF pp. 1-12). They define contestable AI as "responsive to human intervention throughout the system lifecycle" (abstract) and list "tools for scrutiny by subjects or third parties" among the features (PDF p. 3). On appeals: "A risk of appeal procedures is that burdens are shifted to individual subjects", met by synchronous contact "or to have third parties represent subjects"; and "Mechanisms for contestability should allow for collective action" (PDF p. 12).
*Bearing:* §5.4. **Supports and already says part of it.** Third-party representation and collective contest are in the contestability-by-design literature; the binding, independent adjudicator is not among its features in the sections I read.

**Cohen and Suzor (2024), `cohen2024contesting`. Part** (abstract; §3 headings and §3.2, PDF pp. 1-19). *Internet Policy Review* 13(3). Public contestability needs "(1) capabilities to organise; (2) separation of powers; and (3) access to alternative and independent information" (abstract), and they propose "semi-autonomous" internal and external actors as a corporate analogue of the separation of powers (PDF p. 16).
*Bearing:* §5.4 and §6, last paragraph ("what control by the affected means for a system with millions of users"). **Supports**; it begins the institutional question the paper defers.

#### 3e. Meaningful human control, model cards, datasheets, user-side agents

**Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven (2018), `santonidesio2018meaningful`. Part** (abstract and introduction, Frontiers PDF p. 1). *Frontiers in Robotics and AI* 5: 15. Meaningful human control needs a "tracking" condition, that the system respond to "the relevant moral reasons of the humans designing and deploying the system", and a "tracing" condition, that outcomes trace back "to at least one human along the chain of design and operation" (p. 1).
*Bearing:* §5.1 and Table 2. **Sharpens.** The control the concept secures belongs to designers and deployers; the person decided about is not in it. The paper's duties give a share of control to the affected party (the republican "democratic view" of §2.2), and a sentence saying so would pre-empt a referee who reads the duties as meaningful human control renamed.

**Mitchell et al. (2019), `mitchell2019model`. Part** (arXiv:1810.03993; abstract, §1 to §3, PDF pp. 1-3). FAT* 2019, pp. 220-229. Model cards "disclose the context in which models are intended to be used, details of the performance evaluation procedures" (p. 1), with headings for "Primary intended uses" and "Out-of-scope use cases" (p. 3). Among stakeholders: "Impacted individuals who may experience effects from a model can better understand how it works or use information in the card to pursue remedies" (p. 3).
*Bearing:* §5.3, second paragraph ("A declared operating envelope is a different instrument"). **Must be distinguished.** A model card is the closest existing artifact to an envelope: it declares scope in advance. It differs on three counts the paper can state: it is voluntary documentation, it describes method and measured performance, and it binds no one to a limit on what the system may do to a person. The paper should say this in one sentence.

**Gebru et al. (2021), `gebru2021datasheets`. Part** (arXiv:1803.09010; abstract and §1). *Communications of the ACM* 64(12): 86-92. The analogy to "the electronics industry, every component ... is accompanied with a datasheet describing its operating characteristics, test results, recommended usage" (PDF p. 1). The audience is "dataset creators and dataset consumers" (p. 2). *Bearing:* §5.3, optional beside Mitchell. "Operating characteristics" is the nearest phrase in the literature to "operating envelope", and it describes, it does not bind.

**Priyanshu, Vijay, Jabarian and Mireshghallah (2026), `priyanshu2026et`. Abstract** (arXiv:2609.24927; DataCite verified). In 325,000 trials across 13 agents, 8 "systematically choose more expensive options for wealthier users when requests are identical", even when told to find the cheapest; they call it "adversarial delegation", where "the very conditions that make a personal AI agent useful - access to personal information - enable it to act against the user's interests" (abstract).
*Bearing:* §5.4, third paragraph ("it is itself a new third party whose supplier has interests of its own"). **Supports**, with experimental evidence; the claim currently rests on Kolt's legal analysis. Preprint, unrefereed.

**Jonker (2025), `jonker2025is`. Part** (abstract, pp. 153-154, 168-169). BEQ 35(2): 153-186. Data-transferring interactions share the "bargaining dynamics" of labour markets, so data unions and data strikes have "a promising functional similarity" to labour regulation (p. 153). The distinction he draws: "information asymmetries are best mitigated by disclosure while bargaining power asymmetries are best mitigated by strengthening bargaining power" (p. 168).
*Bearing:* §5.3 and §5.4. **Sharpens.** Jonker's split states, in BEQ, why disclosure cannot answer a power asymmetry; the paper's envelope and counter-delegation are bargaining-side remedies in his terms. His collective data union is the network version of counter-delegation that M2 asked the paper to address.
