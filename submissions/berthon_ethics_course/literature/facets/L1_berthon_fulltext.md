# Facet L1: Berthon's four full texts

Facet L1, 2026-10-13. Agent-drafted. The author has not reviewed it, and none of the text is the author's.

## What this facet covers

The author supplied the full text of four Berthon papers. Facet H read three of them (or the abstracts) without the paper: Berthon, Pitt and Campbell (2019, "Addictive De-Vices"), Berthon, Yalcin, Pehlivan and Rabinovich (2024, "Trajectories of AI technologies") and Keaney and Berthon (2025), and it read the fourth, Berthon and Pitt (2019, "Types of mindfulness"), only in the Elsevier corrected proof. This facet reads all four in full and answers six questions: (1) what the policy paper means by informing, guiding and restricting, and which remedies it lists, for whom; (2) what it names as the sources of digital addiction, and whether it uses historical analogy; (3) the ethical position of the two AI papers; (4) whether Berthon places the remedy on the individual, the firm or the regulator; (5) which statements in `H_berthon_record.md` and `BERTHON_NOTES.md` the full texts confirm, correct or contradict; (6) whether any of the four mentions literacy, reading, writing, print or media history. The reader of the eventual paper is Berthon himself, so I report the texts fairly in both directions. Every reading that is mine and not the source's is marked "agent's reading".

### L1.1. Berthon, Pitt and Campbell (2019), "Addictive De-Vices: A Public Policy Analysis of Sources and Solutions to Digital Addiction"

*Journal of Public Policy & Marketing* 38(4): 451–468. DOI 10.1177/0743915619859852. Key `berthon2019addictive`. Published online 28 July 2019 (Crossref). Section label on p. 451: "Use and Misuse". **Read in full**, all 18 pages (references on pp. 463–468 skimmed for titles, not checked entry by entry). Guest editors Kristen Walker, George Milne and Bruce Weinberg (p. 463). Scale: consumer policy, with the nation-state as the acting level (US throughout) and examples from the UK, Norway, Japan, South Korea, China, Germany and the EU.

**Argument in brief.** Digital addiction is "both akratic (we are aware of the negative consequences) and engineered (designed on purpose)" (p. 451). Product design "is at the heart of addiction" (p. 454, p. 452); advertising, distribution and price "enhance and deepen" it (p. 452). The industry's own response (Apple and Google usage dashboards) is "minimal" (p. 457). So "public policy solutions are required" (p. 457), organised as informing, guiding and restricting across the four Ps (Table 2, pp. 458–459). The paper closes by listing research questions for marketing scholars (pp. 461–463) and concludes that "public policy intervention is inevitable" (p. 463).

**What the paper says "informing", "guiding" and "restricting" mean.** Three statements, two in the text and one by implication in Table 2:

- p. 452: "For all 4 Ps, we present policy solutions in terms of three broad strategies: informing (educate and disclose), guiding (shape and incentivize), and restricting (ban and remove)."
- p. 457: "each of the elements of marketing strategy (product design, place and distribution, advertising and promotion, and price and cost) are addressed by three groupings of public policy strategy: inform (educate and disclose), guide (shape and incentivize), and restrict (ban and remove) (cf. Moorman and Price 1989)."
- Table 2 (pp. 458–459) assigns each remedy to one of the three, in a grid of four Ps by three strategies, with a column of "Related Questions for Future Research".

Agent's reading: the paper treats the three as a ladder of intrusiveness. Mandatory labelling, the paper's first example of informing, is "one of the least controversial public policy strategies" (p. 457); restricting is "The most extreme public policy intervention" (p. 459). Guiding sits between.

**The remedies.** Table of every specific remedy, with printed page, who acts, and the paper's own stance. "Stance" uses three values: *recommended* (the paper says it argues for it or that it should be done), *option* (listed with "could", "might", "would" and no verdict), *option with objection* (the paper itself names a difficulty). The paper never says "we recommend" a specific remedy; it calls them "our suggestions" (p. 457) and "public policy ideas we propose" (p. 461), and its only unconditional recommendation is public policy intervention in general (pp. 457, 461, 463). Every specific remedy is therefore an *option* at least, and I mark where the paper adds a qualifier. The strategy column gives the paper's own assignment in Table 2; "T2" in the page column means Table 2.

**Does the paper weigh the remedies against each other?** Yes, in four places, though never as a ranking of the three strategies.

1. Against information alone (p. 457), on the Apple and Google dashboards: "experts are split as to the effectiveness of these changes. As one notes, 'There's literally no evidence that knowing how much you consume changes how you consume' (Tsukayama 2018). Indeed, empirical research suggests that information is necessary, but not sufficient to change behavior (e.g., Worsley 2002)."
2. Against information campaigns alone (p. 460), quoted in row 8.
3. Against the labels themselves (p. 457), row 1: "would not entirely prevent addiction". The paper also notes that time-out messages "can sometimes have the opposite effect, challenging the user to play for longer or instilling feelings of guilt" (p. 457).
4. Against self-regulation (p. 457): "Although there is always the possibility that self-regulation may achieve some changes, a pressing need remains for public policy intervention." And "it is in these tech giants' financial interest to keep consumers hooked on the experiences they offer. If they did indeed act aggressively to 'unhook' consumers, there are plenty of other competitors to take their place."

The paper also says interventions "should be compared on the basis of both their cost and efficacy... Some may even exacerbate the problem" (p. 462), and "Consumers may prefer more user control rather than blanket mandates" (p. 462). It does not say that education alone is insufficient in so many words about the schools-and-specialists route (rows 10 to 12); it gives that route a supporting citation instead (row 10). Agent's reading: the paper treats information as a necessary first step ("awareness and knowledge are first steps", p. 460) that must be paired with design changes and restrictions, and its fullest argument that individuals cannot carry the load is at p. 463: "Consumers on their own struggle to maintain equanimity with regard to their digital experiences, and companies are competitively incentivized to learn how to make their offerings as addictive as possible. Thus, public policy intervention is likely to be both unavoidable and beneficial to society."

**Consumer education, "literacy", mindfulness.** Education: yes, as the first of the three strategies ("informing (educate and disclose)", pp. 452, 457) and in rows 8, 10, 11, 12. The text speaks of "educational campaigns" and "educating consumers of the dangers of digital experiences" (p. 460). "Literacy": **not found** (the only hit for "litera" is "literally", p. 457, and "literature"). Mindfulness: yes, twice, both as a user-side educational measure delivered through schools and specialists (p. 460, Table 2 p. 458: "Teach mindfulness when using digital experiences"). The paper does not cite Berthon and Pitt's mindfulness column (reference list checked for "Berthon" and "Pitt": none). It cites Bahl et al. 2013 and Kim, Milne and Bahl 2018 for mindfulness.

**Does the press-release account in H match the paper?** The facts match in most particulars; the sorting under the three verbs does not. Details are under Question 5.

**Sources of digital addiction (Question 2).** The paper names product design as the core and the other three Ps as amplifiers: "while product design is at the heart of addiction, each of the other factors works to enhance and deepen its effects" (p. 452).

- *Design.* "We begin by discussing the heart of addiction to digital experiences: design. Tech companies have received criticism for marketing offerings that are purposefully designed to be habit forming and ultimately addictive (Harris 2017)." (p. 454). Notifications as "variable-ratio reinforcement, which has long been used and perfected in the gambling industry" (p. 454); Snapchat streaks (p. 454); continuous scroll: "Similar to how casinos avoid natural light and clocks, by removing discrete pages and loading content into an infinite stream, natural stopping points are taken away" (p. 454); "The removal of natural cues to end a behavior such as chapters or other end points has the effect of inducing immersion" (p. 454). "Tech companies do not want users satisfied; they want to keep them in a state of dissatisfaction" (p. 454).
- *Big data and experimentation.* The marketer's role is wider than for sugar or tobacco: "its largest impact was exposure... In contrast, with digital experiences, in addition to exposure, marketers play a much more active role in designing the products themselves" (p. 454); "experiments are easier and almost costless to run; and, thanks to programming, the cycle of testing and redesign can operate almost instantaneously" (p. 454). "Moreover, companies are increasingly conducting clandestine, real-world experiments on their customers... unencumbered by institutional review boards (IRBs)... As it stands, tech companies have little or no oversight (ethical or legal) as to what they do with their customer interactions." (p. 455). Facebook and OkCupid are named (p. 455).
- *Advertising and promotion.* "advertising and promotions are not the core cause of addiction... they help both initiate and sustain such addictions" (p. 455). Ads act as cues; native advertising blends ads into the feed; data allows precise targeting; "With advertising as their primary source of revenue... tech companies are incentivized to know as much as possible about a person" (p. 455).
- *Place and distribution.* "While availability alone does not cause addiction, the presence of cues can initiate behaviors that deepen addiction" (p. 455); ubiquitous networks and free Wi-Fi (pp. 455–456); children targeted through school Wi-Fi and buses (p. 455); bandwidth and VR as "the equivalent of heroin in the digital world" (p. 456, reporting Kotler 2014).
- *"Free" pricing.* "the cost to the consumer of many digital experiences is essentially zero, making onboarding use of new tools frictionless" (p. 456); freemium microtransactions and "pay-to-win" (p. 456); and "a major cost of digital experiences is that of attention... 'If you're not paying for it, you become the product'... So, users pay with their attention, time, and all the associated psychological consequences" (p. 456). "The absence of warnings is likely to make consumers prone to use or overuse digital experiences" (p. 456).
- *The individual's side.* The paper also lists individual causes of addiction from the literature: genetics, conditioning, discounting, and "Addictive behavior is more likely if people are not aware of the full ramifications of their actions" (p. 453). That sentence cuts against the akratic framing of the abstract; the paper does not reconcile the two.

**Historical analogy (Question 2).** Yes, by analogy to other regulated goods, not to media history.

- p. 453: "Marketers have innocently, then knowingly, promoted addictive offerings for as long as commerce has existed (Martin et al. 2013). Examples include sugar, tobacco, cocaine, opioid pain medication, and gambling". Then p. 454: "we are in the midst of the transition between consumer innocence and knowledge of their addictive nature."
- Gambling: variable rewards (pp. 453–454), casinos (p. 454), problem gambling and online gambling (pp. 454, 457, 462), casino self-exclusion (p. 461), "Gamblers Anonymous" (p. 463).
- Food and nutrition: the food industry's ubiquity, portion sizes and pricing of high-fat, high-sugar food (pp. 455–456); "Thus, we argue that public policy solutions are required to address digital addiction, just as in other areas, from nutrition to gambling" (p. 457); labelling of calories and fuel economy (p. 457); school nutrition (p. 460).
- Tobacco and alcohol: tax precedent and lobbying (p. 461); "existing bans on minors accessing alcohol and tobacco" (p. 460); Alcoholics Anonymous and court-mandated attendance (p. 463); the cigarette is cited in the reference list (Brandt 2007).
- Prescription drugs: advertising disclosure (pp. 460–461).
- Print and television: only as media or comparisons. "books have chapters" (p. 457); "chapters or other end points" (p. 454); campaigns "could use broadcast media such as TV, radio, and print" (p. 460); "prime-time TV" (p. 461); the phrase "If you're not paying for it, you become the product" "actually predates the internet and emerged from the advertising-funded media offerings of the 1970s" (p. 456). No argument about the history of literacy, reading or print.
- A national-history remark: "The United States has a long history of framing such behaviors as a moral weakness and a matter of willpower (Bernhard 2007). Even when a problem is acknowledged, there is a tendency for endless debate as to who is responsible: the federal government looks to the states, the states look to the industry, and the industry points to the consumer's freedom of choice" (p. 462).

**Bearing on the thesis.** Complicates in both directions. It refutes any reading of Berthon as an author who leaves the remedy with the user (see Q4), and it also shows that the same authors endorse user-side mindfulness education and employer digital-hygiene programmes (rows 11, 12, 21) as a layer inside a regulatory package.

### L1.2. Berthon, Yalcin, Pehlivan and Rabinovich (2024), "Trajectories of AI technologies: Insights for managers"

*Business Horizons* 67(5): 461–470. DOI 10.1016/j.bushor.2024.03.002. Key `berthon2024trajectories`. Open access, CC BY (stated p. 461). **Read in full**, all 10 pages (pp. 469–470 are acknowledgement and references). A footnote reads "Authors contributed equally" (p. 461). Scale: firm and market, with society as the larger system the framework studies.

**The abstract differs from the one Facet H quoted.** H4 quoted an OpenAlex abstract ("With the release of the chatbot ChatGPT 4 there is emerging consensus that the future may have begun... promises a cornucopia of benefits to humankind"). The published abstract (p. 461) reads: "Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has long been considered a technology for the future. With the release of the chatbot ChatGPT 4, many now feel the future has arrived. Long in gestation, this new technology promises many benefits to humankind, but worries persist that as AI technology scales and comes to rival or exceed human intelligence, the servant may become the master. Amid such hyperbole, the more nuanced trajectories of this technology have been neglected. In this article, we use the Trajectories of Technology (ToT) framework developed by Berthon and colleagues to explore the disparate paths that AI has taken and will take in the coming years, especially in the form of chatbots. This framework provides managers with a conceptual tool to strategically plan for the enormous promises and perils of AI in general and of chatbots specifically." The OpenAlex text is an earlier wording. Quote the published one.

**What it argues.** Technology is not "inert tools but active forces in our lives... even as we possess our creations, they in turn possess us" (p. 461). It applies the ToT model of Berthon, Hulbert and Pitt (2005) to GenAI: four trajectories (subversion, diversion, emersion, aspersion), on two axes (whether people or technology act first; positive or negative feedback) (p. 463). Regulation enters as a trajectory, not a recommendation: "Diversion is an intentional trajectory characterized by negative feedback. Here, businesses and other third parties seek to control or regulate a technology such that aspects of its trajectory are attenuated." (p. 464). Aspersion includes an observation about self-regulation: "the market optics of AI bias has proved a major incentive for companies to self-regulate the training of their AI systems" (p. 464).

**Ethical position.** Stated in one paragraph, the fourth managerial implication (p. 468): "Fourth, consider the ethics: Human communication, broadly conceived, especially as it is routed through social media channels, will change greatly as AI is adopted for information and content generation. AI really is where design ethics will become crucial. If technology is about epistemology, then AI is about ethics, for it is a fundamentally human undertaking. What we design shapes who we will become. For example, children may prefer AI chatbots to interacting with other children... But kids need to grapple with the challenges of coming to know other kids in order to grow into socially adept adults. The AI technology that we develop and sell will shape the next generation with global repercussions." Table 1 (p. 468): "Consider the ethics. What the consumer wants may differ from what they need." The closing section (p. 469) is a general warning: "enthralled by our magical devices, we overlook the price we pay"; AI "will be able to free humans from their embodiment, at ever increasing social, psychological, and planetary cost"; "The technologies that we create are like mirrors: They reflect and amplify the best and worst of humanity." Agent's reading: the position is a designer's ethic ("what we design shapes who we will become") addressed to the people who "develop and sell" AI, not a rule-based or regulatory ethic.

**Who is asked to do what.**

- *Managers.* All four recommendations are for managers: "expect the unexpected", "plan for various scenarios", "overestimate AI, underestimate AI, and then balance the two", "consider the ethics" (pp. 467–468; Table 1, p. 468). The title and abstract say the same.
- *Designers.* Only the sentence above: "AI really is where design ethics will become crucial" (p. 468).
- *Regulators.* Not addressed. They appear as actors inside the model: "industry leaders urging a pause or slowdown in this area so regulations can catch up" (p. 467); "Some unionized groups have called for regulation" (p. 465); the open letter, calls for a ban, and the Frontier Model Forum are reported (p. 466). The paper takes no side: "Others argue that banning AI development is untenable, and that regulating its alignment is a solution" (p. 466).
- *Users.* Never asked to do anything. They are the party whose behaviour the technology shapes: "Through this feedback loop of user training, whereby the technology and the user shape each other's behavior over time" (p. 465); "humans tend to accept the wrong answers" because the system's responses "are written to sound so pleasant and authoritative" (p. 467).

**Explainability, transparency, user understanding, education, literacy.** "Transparency": not found. "Explainability": not found; the word "explain" occurs only for the Duolingo feature "Explain My Answer" (p. 465) and as "fails to explain the effect" (p. 467). "Literacy": not found. Education appears as a use case, not a remedy: students using ChatGPT to write essays, and institutions that "seem to have capitulated to this trend" (p. 464); Duolingo and Khanmigo (pp. 465–466); "this evolution is highly likely to change how we learn as human beings. In the history of humanity, only a select few have had the luxury of a reliable tutor available at any time of the day." (p. 466). The nearest thing to user understanding is a diagnosis: "the facticity or trustworthiness of material on the web is likely to decline over time, perhaps precipitously", and "The current solution of using humans to police fake content is likely to run into capacity issues: AI scales up much faster than humans." (pp. 466–467).

**Language, media, history.** "our understanding of the world, and of our social selves, is shaped by the language we use. What shapes our language therefore affects our conceptual worlds. AI's fast adoption and its methods of training algorithms through human interaction only serve to increase its effects on society. AI is therefore uniquely poised to rewire our linguistics and to reshape how we conceptualize our experiences." (p. 465). Earlier media appear as examples of technology that changes perception: "TV and social media change not only how we see the world both materially and conceptually but also how we see one another and ourselves" (p. 462); the telescope (p. 462). On the internet and social media: "from great forces for democracy, redistribution of wealth, and safeguarding truth, these tools have been used to promote control, inequality, and falsehood. The same is likely to be true of AI." (p. 467). And on attention, a paragraph that links to the 2019 work (p. 469): "Humanity's first encounter with the widespread use of AI was in social media (see Harari et al., 2023). ### L1.3. Keaney and Berthon (2025), "Balancing explainability and privacy in AI systems: A strategic imperative for managers"

*Business Horizons*, online October 2025 (Crossref: 2025-10). DOI 10.1016/j.bushor.2025.10.004. Key `keaney2025balancing`. **In-press proof, 9 pages, own pagination 1–9** (page 1 headed "Business Horizons (xxxx) xxx, xxx"; page 9 is references). **Read in full.** Authors' affiliation as printed (p. 1): both at "Bentley University, 175 Forest Avenue, Waltham, MA 02454, USA". Scale: the firm.

**Abstract (published wording, proof p. 1).** "The rapid embedding of artificial intelligence (AI) into core enterprise operations presents managers with a critical strategic dilemma: balancing explainability and data privacy. On the one hand, explainable AI (XAI) is essential for regulatory compliance and building stakeholder confidence. On the other hand, the methods used to open the AI black box risk exposing sensitive customer data and proprietary information, contravening privacy obligations and creating new attack vectors. In this article, we provide a strategic framework for managers to address these challenges... These recommendations include adopting a tiered explainability framework, integrating specific privacy-preserving XAI techniques, and promoting interdisciplinary AI governance teams to ensure a secure, compliant, and responsible AI strategy."

**What it argues.** Explainability (what the regulator and customer want) and privacy (what the same regulator requires) pull against each other, and managers must balance them. The article is a "conceptual synthesis with managerial guidance" (p. 2). Its framework (Figure 1, p. 3) treats XAI through "the progressive lenses of transparency, explainability, and interpretability" (p. 2). The seven recommendations in Section 5 (Table 1, p. 6) are: a tiered explainability framework; privacy-preserving XAI techniques; mandatory trade-off assessments; model cards and datasheets; an interdisciplinary governance team; proactively educating stakeholders on the limits of XAI; and ethics embedded in procurement and design.

**Ethical position.** Ethics as a managerial design principle, held against compliance: "This treats ethics as a foundational design principle, not as a reactive compliance checklist, leading to more robust, trusted, and sustainable AI systems." (p. 8). And: "Balancing explainability and privacy is an ongoing commitment, not a one-time solution. It requires a proactive, interdisciplinary approach that embeds ethical considerations into the fabric of an organization's AI development lifecycle." (p. 8). It also frames the task as strategic, not technical: "The tensions between explainability and privacy are not purely technical problems to be delegated to data scientists. These tensions are a core strategic dilemma that requires managerial oversight." (p. 5). The text is also candid that the motive is partly commercial: privacy investment can be reframed "as a strategic R&D investment in future-proofing the business" (p. 7).

**Who is asked to do what.**

- *Managers.* Everything in Section 5. Examples: "For managers, the immediate action is to convene stakeholders and begin mapping out these specific tiers and access rules before a regulator demands it." (p. 6); the governance team must be "granted genuine authority to approve, delay, or halt projects that fail to meet the organization's ethical and risk standards" (p. 7).
- *Designers.* Addressed as the AI development lifecycle: "these principles must be integrated by design from the very first stage of the product lifecycle" (p. 8).
- *Regulators.* Not addressed. Regulation is taken as a present and growing external constraint, and the authors do not argue for or against it: "Regulators, citing their authority under frameworks like the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), are demanding a full explanation" (p. 1); "Over time, AI regulations will continue to mature and solidify. Vague principles are being replaced by concrete enforcement mechanisms, as seen with the EU's AI Act. This landmark legislation will mandate risk assessments, transparency reports, and clear explanations for high-risk AI systems" (p. 5).
- *Users and customers.* Recipients, not actors. On the paper's account the customer holds a legal right (its reference list also carries Wachter, Mittelstadt and Floridi 2017, "Why a right to explanation of automated decision-making does not exist in the General Data Protection Regulation", and the text does not discuss the dispute): "Frameworks like the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) grant individuals the right to explanation for automated decisions" (p. 3), and the paper tells managers to write vendor contracts that guarantee "rights to explanation" (p. 8). What the customer gets is a tier: "For an external customer, a high-level summary of the reasons for a decision may suffice (e.g., 'Your loan was denied due to a high debt-to-income ratio')." (p. 6).

**Explainability, transparency, user understanding, education, literacy.** Transparency and explainability are the subject. Both are framed as duties of the firm to stakeholders and regulators: "Stakeholders, from customers to auditors, increasingly expect this level of transparency to build trust and ensure accountability." (p. 3). The paper also uses a label analogy that echoes the 2019 policy paper: "A model card acts like a nutrition label for an AI model, outlining its intended use, performance limitations, and ethical considerations." (p. 7). Education: Section 5.6, "Proactively educate stakeholders on the limits of XAI" (p. 7): "It is crucial to develop training and clear disclaimers that communicate that AI explanations are often probabilistic approximations, not absolute certainties... Employees, especially those in customer-facing roles, must be trained on how to frame these explanations correctly to avoid implying a level of certainty that does not exist. This manages stakeholder expectations, reduces the risk of overreliance on imperfect explanations, and fosters a more sophisticated understanding of AI capabilities across the organization." (p. 7). Agent's reading: this is the closest the four papers come to user understanding of a system, but the educated party is the firm's own staff, and the aim is to manage expectations and limit liability, not to build a user competence. "Literacy": **not found** (the only "litera" hit is "literature", p. 2). Reading, writing, print, media history: not found.

**Bearing.** Strongly supports the claim that Berthon's recent work addresses managers. Complicates H's statement that regulation is "largely absent" from his later work: here it is the premise of the whole article, though never argued for.

### Q1. Addictive De-Vices: informing, guiding, restricting

The three verbs are defined once, by parenthesis, on pp. 452 and 457: informing is "educate and disclose", guiding is "shape and incentivize", restricting is "ban and remove". Table 2 (pp. 458–459) is the authoritative list; the remedies and their actors are in the table above (28 rows). The pattern in the table:

- *Regulator acts:* rows 1 to 7, 13, 15 to 17, 19 to 23, 25. The paper's verbs are "mandate", "required", "forced", "might be required" and "legislators might consider"; in rows 15, 16, 20, 21 and 22 they are softer still ("government intervention could shape behavior"; "Legislators could go further, mandating"; "could be forced to show"; "regulators could structure tax incentives"; "regulators could impose taxes").
- *Firm acts:* as the party mandated to label, redesign, disclose and submit to oversight; and, unprompted, as gatekeeper (row 27) and as maker of weak self-regulation (row 28).
- *User acts:* as the reader of labels and disclosures (rows 1, 9, 14, 19, 20), as a self-excluder (row 18) and as a participant in support groups (row 26). Never as the sole actor.
- *Educator acts:* rows 10 to 12. The paper puts these under *guiding*, not *informing*. It reserves *informing* for the population-level campaign and the disclosure.
- *Recommended, option, objection:* no specific remedy is "recommended" in so many words. The paper recommends public policy intervention as a class (pp. 457, 461, 463), then lists options. The paper marks as controversial or difficult: row 2 ("more controversially"), row 17 ("more controversial"; "might seem extreme"), row 22 ("most controversially"; "only a long-term political possibility"), row 24 (lock-in), row 25 ("unlikely to be popular"). ### Q4. Individual, firm or regulator?

This section reads all four texts with H's findings on the two HICSS papers (Su, Wang and Berthon 2023; Wang, Berthon and Su 2025). Quotations for the HICSS papers are H's, with H's locators; I did not reopen them.

**The texts, by venue.**

- *Regulator.* Addictive De-Vices, a *Journal of Public Policy & Marketing* article, puts the weight on the regulator. "Thus, we argue that public policy solutions are required to address digital addiction" (p. 457). "In many instances there is an urgent need for regulators to take control of a phenomenon that confronts society with several serious problems." (p. 461). "Thus, at a meta-level, public policy intervention is inevitable." (p. 463). The reason is structural: "the negative consequences of addiction to digital experiences are unlikely to be addressed on the market level at which they occur" (p. 463).
- *Firm.* The *Business Horizons* and HICSS texts address the manager. The mindfulness column: "it is incumbent upon managers in general and marketers in particular to understand the phenomenon" (pp. 131–132); the firm should "innovate to develop new business models that do not run on attention" (p. 136). Trajectories (2024): the four recommendations (pp. 467–468). Keaney and Berthon (2025): "a core strategic dilemma that requires managerial oversight" (p. 5). Wang, Berthon and Su (2025), per H: "places the ethical responsibility squarely on their shoulders" (p. 5191).
- *Individual.* No text makes the individual the sole or main bearer. The mindfulness column sets out four modes of attention that an individual practises, and says the thin popular mode "may be of little help" against these platforms (p. 136). The policy paper lists user-side measures (rows 10 to 12, 18, 26) and the AA-style support groups (p. 463), all inside a regulatory package.

**Sentences that cut against each reading.**

- Against the regulator reading: the AI papers do not call for regulation. Wang, Berthon and Su (2025), per H, leave regulation as "Future research" (p. 5192). Trajectories reports the call for a pause "so regulations can catch up" without endorsing it (p. 467). Keaney and Berthon take regulation as given and counsel managers to get ahead of it (p. 6). The policy paper itself concedes the political difficulty: "only a long-term political possibility" (p. 461), and that the industry "is likely to be the biggest opponent of the political process of intervention" (p. 462). It also says regulators may be late and gatekeepers may act first: "It may ultimately be gatekeepers such as Google and Apple that force app developers to abide by best practices" (p. 462).
- Against the firm reading: the policy paper rejects self-regulation as the main route (p. 457) and writes that "companies are competitively incentivized to learn how to make their offerings as addictive as possible" (p. 463). Trajectories, by contrast, allows that firms self-regulate when it pays: "the market optics of AI bias has proved a major incentive for companies to self-regulate" (p. 464).
- Against the individual reading: "Consumers on their own struggle to maintain equanimity" (p. 463); "information is necessary, but not sufficient to change behavior" (p. 457); "the industry points to the consumer's freedom of choice" (p. 462, reporting the pattern of blame-shifting). In the column: "Increasingly, our attention is not under our own control" (p. 136).
- For the individual reading: the column's typology is an individual-level practice; the policy paper endorses mindfulness teaching and digital hygiene (pp. 458, 460) and tax relief for employers' digital-hygiene programmes (p. 461); and the column's own recommendation is that "wise mindfulness or mindfulness that leads to insight is essential" (p. 136).

**Three-sentence answer (agent's reading).** Berthon's remedy follows the venue and the reader: where he writes for policy-makers (Addictive De-Vices, 2019) he puts the main weight on the regulator, with firms as the regulated party and users as the people informed, guided or restricted, while in the *Business Horizons* and HICSS papers (2019 to 2025) he addresses the manager and treats regulation as an external condition or as future research. The individual is never the sole bearer in any of the four texts, and the policy paper says that information is "necessary, but not sufficient to change behavior" (p. 457, citing Worsley 2002), that "Informational campaigns alone have had limited impact" (p. 460), and that "Consumers on their own struggle to maintain equanimity with regard to their digital experiences" (p. 463). The work therefore does not run from individual to structural; the structural claim is present in 2019 and the later work shifts the addressee, not the diagnosis.
