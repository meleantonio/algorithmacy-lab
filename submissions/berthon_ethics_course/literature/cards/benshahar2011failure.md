## Summary
A long law-review article arguing that mandated disclosure, "the single most common technique for protecting personal autonomy in modern society", chronically fails (p. 647). The article has four parts: a survey of disclosure mandates in credit, informed consent, contract boilerplate, Miranda warnings and much else; a survey of empirical evidence that the mandates neither inform people nor improve decisions; an account of why they fail, at the level of lawmakers, disclosers and disclosees (including illiteracy and innumeracy); and an argument that disclosure has unintended harms, among them inequity, since "the poor have harder problems that require more information and experience, but they have less ability to use the information" (pp. 647–649, 742). On boilerplate, they report that roughly one person in a thousand opens online terms and that such readers spend a median 29 seconds, and quote Marotta-Wurgler's conclusion that license agreements "are almost always ignored" (pp. 671–672). The conclusion declines to prescribe a single replacement and points toward simpler information and advice (pp. 742–743). This card rests on the pages listed; the middle of the article was not read this session.

## Key facts it relies on
- Mandated disclosure is "the single most common technique for protecting personal autonomy in modern society" (abstract, p. 647).
- Four parts: pervasiveness, documented failure, reasons for failure, unintended effects (p. 647; contents pp. 647–649).
- "it chronically fails to accomplish its purpose. Even where it seems to succeed, its costs in money, effort, and time generally swamp its benefits" (p. 651).
- "roughly 1 in 1000 people actually scrolls through online boilerplate when it is disclosed prior to the agreement" (p. 671, n. 127 citing Bakos et al.).
- That reader "spent a median time of twenty-nine seconds on the webpage" (p. 671).
- Marotta-Wurgler found terms accessed in 8 of 11,184 visits with a click-to-agree box and 40 of 120,545 visits without one (p. 671, n. 129).
- "Marrota-Wurgler writes, 'The general conclusion is clear: No matter how prominently [end-user license agreements] are disclosed, they are almost always ignored.'" (p. 672, n. 130: "Id. (manuscript at 16)", referring to n. 129).
- n. 129 is Marotta-Wurgler, "Will Increased Disclosure Help? Evaluating the Recommendations of the ALI's Principles of the Law of Software Contracts", 78 U. Chi. L. Rev. (forthcoming) (p. 671).
- Disclosure is inequitable: it helps the sophisticated more than the poor (p. 742).
