## Summary
McDonald and Cranor ask: "if website users were to read the privacy policy for each site they visit just once a year, what would their time be worth?" (pre-press p. 1). They estimate reading time two ways: from the word counts of the 75 most popular sites at 250 words per minute (about 10 minutes per policy), and from an online study of 212 participants who skimmed policies and answered simple comprehension questions (p. 1). With Nielsen data on the number of unique sites visited (lower bound 119 a year) and a value of time set at 25% of hourly wage for leisure and twice wages for work time, they put the national opportunity cost "on the order of $781 billion" (p. 1). The conclusion gives about 201 hours a year per person, worth about $3,534 (p. 19), and sets the total against an online advertising market of about $21 billion in 2007 (p. 20). The figures are a model of what reading *would* cost if people read every policy word for word; they are not observations of what people do. The card rests on the author's pre-press, whose pages differ from the journal's; Solove (2013, n. 41) cites the $781 billion to journal p. 564.

## Key facts it relies on
- p. 1 (abstract): "if website users were to read the privacy policy for each site they visit just once a year, what would their time be worth?"
- p. 1: 75 popular sites, 250 words per minute, about 10 minutes per policy; 212-participant skimming study; lower bound of 119 unique sites a year.
- p. 1: "the national opportunity cost for just the time to read policies is on the order of $781 billion."
- p. 18: "using the point estimate of 244 hours per year to read privacy policies per person means an average of 40 minutes a day."
- p. 19: "reading privacy policies carries costs in time of approximately 201 hours a year, worth about $3,534 annually per American Internet user."
- pp. 19-20: comparison shopping across sites, which the FTC's self-regulation vision presumes, would make the true cost "perhaps on the order of double the costs we estimate".
- p. 20: online advertising worth "about $21 billion in 2007"; users "should give up an estimated $781 billion of their time to protect themselves from an industry worth substantially less."
