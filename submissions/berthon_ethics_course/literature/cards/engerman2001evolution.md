## Summary
Engerman and Sokoloff ask where political institutions come from. They trace how the rules extending the franchise evolved in the United States and across the Americas. Their prior argument holds that factor endowments produced very different levels of initial inequality among New World colonies. Here they show that the franchise followed suit: "where there was greater inequality, the proportion of the population that had the right to vote was generally lower", and the right was extended later (abstract). In the Latin American section I read (pp. 22-25), they report that after independence property qualifications were loosened. The main change, though, was the introduction of literacy requirements, which spread "rapidly throughout Latin America" while the U.S. used none "until the 1890s". They list national examples with dates of adoption and repeal: Bolivia, Costa Rica, Chile, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guatemala, Mexico, Peru, Uruguay and Brazil. Argentina and Colombia are the major exceptions at the national level. Given very low literacy rates, they argue, these requirements depressed participation. Table 3 tabulates secrecy, wealth and literacy requirements alongside the share of the population voting, 1840-1940. When Chile removed its literacy requirement in 1874, the share voting "more than tripled within a few years" (p. 25).

## Key facts it relies on
- Abstract: "where there was greater inequality, the proportion of the population that had the right to vote was generally lower, and the timing of the extensions of this right ... generally later".
- p. 23: "the introduction and growing emphasis on a literacy requirement was the major change that occurred after independence in the laws governing the franchise."
- p. 23: "Whereas a literacy qualification was not used in the U.S. until the 1890s ... virtually all Latin American countries included a literacy requirement for citizenship (encompassing the right to vote) in their first constitution or soon afterward."
- pp. 23-24, dates: Bolivia 1826 to beyond 1945; Costa Rica 1844-1913; Chile 1833-1874 and 1885-1970; Ecuador 1861-1978; El Salvador 1864-c.1945; Guatemala 1879-1945; Mexico 1835-1857; Peru 1826 to c.1979; Uruguay 1830-1918.
- p. 24: Brazil "replaced [property restrictions] with a literacy qualification in 1891; this restriction endured until 1988." Argentina and Colombia had no national literacy requirement.
- p. 24 and Table 3: the countries with the most progressive suffrage laws (Argentina, Costa Rica, Uruguay) had "markedly higher rates of the population voting".
- p. 25: after Chile's 1874 constitution removed the literacy requirement, "the proportion of voters in the population more than tripled within a few years". Table 3 shows Chile at 1.6% in 1869 and 3.1% in 1881.

## Critical notes from the literature
- AUDIT_05: the authors' own Table 3 shows Chile at 1.6% (1869) and 3.1% (1881), about a doubling, while their text says "more than tripled" (`audit/AUDIT_05.md`:26). The Table 3 figures confirm this.
- Brazil's date. Engerman and Sokoloff give 1891 (the Republic's constitution) and say nothing of the Saraiva Law. AUDIT_05 and facet M5 hold that 1881 is right for the first literacy rule for enrolment (Decreto 3.029 of 9 January 1881, art. 8, "souberem ler e escrever"). The 1881 law also tightened proof of income (`audit/AUDIT_05.md`:24, :758-759; `facets/M5_wider_cases.md`:260).
- End date for Brazil. They give 1988. The manuscript says 1985 (Emenda Constitucional 25/1985, via Aleixo and Kramer). The 1985 amendment made enrolment optional for illiterates while their ineligibility for office remained (ledger P3-020; facet D:495).
- The published version (`engerman2005evolution`, *Journal of Economic History*) was not opened. Facet M5 asks for it to be checked first (`facets/M5_wider_cases.md`:274).
- Facet M5 lists Chile 1874 (p. 25) as a counter-case to name in the paper (`facets/M5_wider_cases.md`:286).
