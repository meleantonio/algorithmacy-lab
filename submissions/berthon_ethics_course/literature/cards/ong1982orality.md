## Summary
Ong's book synthesises the orality-literacy research of Parry, Lord, Havelock, Goody and others. The pages read
here define "secondary orality", the orality of telephone, radio and television. Ong defines it three times and
each time ties it to writing: it "depends on writing and print for its existence" (p. 2); it is sustained by
"electronic devices that depend for their existence and functioning on writing and print" (pp. 10-11); and it is
"a more deliberate and self-conscious orality, based permanently on the use of writing and print, which are
essential for the manufacture and operation of the equipment and for its use as well" (p. 133). In the same
passage he says the computer "maximizes commitment of the word to space" and intensifies the sequential
processing begun by writing (p. 133). Secondary orality resembles the old in "its participatory mystique, its
fostering of a communal sense, its concentration on the present moment, and even its use of formulas" (p. 133),
but "is both remarkably like and remarkably unlike primary orality" (p. 133): "We plan our happenings carefully
to be sure that they are thoroughly spontaneous" (p. 134).

## Key facts it relies on
- "The electronic age is also an age of 'secondary orality', the orality of telephones, radio, and television, which depends on writing and print for its existence" (p. 2).
- Primary orality is "'primary' by contrast with the 'secondary orality' of present-day high-technology culture, in which a new orality is sustained by telephone, radio, television, and other electronic devices that depend for their existence and functioning on writing and print" (pp. 10-11).
- "The electronic transformation of verbal expression has both deepened the commitment of the word to space initiated by writing and intensified by print and has brought consciousness to a new age of secondary orality" (p. 132).
- The computer "maximizes commitment of the word to space and to (electronic) local motion and optimizes analytic sequentiality by making it virtually instantaneous" (p. 133).
- Secondary orality is "essentially a more deliberate and self-conscious orality, based permanently on the use of writing and print" (p. 133).
- "Secondary orality is both remarkably like and remarkably unlike primary orality" (p. 133).

## Critical notes from the literature
- Ong's cognitive "great divide" between oral and literate minds is contested (Street 1984, Finnegan 1988, Halverson 1992, Scribner and Cole 1981); see the sister-arm card's Caution. The passages used here are definitional and do not need the cognitive thesis.
- Ong wrote before networked computing; "secondary orality" names broadcast media. Heyd (2021) and Arató and Balázs (2024) extend it (facet V6, Q2). Marz (2026) reports a 1996 interview in which Ong said online writing "is not directly oral at all" (second-hand).
