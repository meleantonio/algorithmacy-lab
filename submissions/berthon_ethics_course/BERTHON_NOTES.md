## 2. What Berthon's texts say

**Berthon and Pitt (2019), "Types of mindfulness in an age of digital distraction", *Business Horizons*
62(2): 131–137.** Read in full, in the corrected proof. The proof's pages run 1–7; the issue page 136
for the quotation below is inferred and unchecked.

- The quotation the draft uses is verbatim, less its first word: "Increasingly, our attention is not
  under our own control. Choices are made for us not necessarily in our interest but in the interest
  of social media companies and the advertisers they serve. In short, business models are designed to
  make us mindless." (proof p. 6)
- The next paragraph qualifies it: "Now before we get all hot and bothered about nasty companies
  commandeering our minds, it is worthwhile to reflect on how we got to where we are now." (proof p. 6)
- The paper addresses managers: "it is incumbent upon managers in general and marketers in particular
  to understand the phenomenon" (proof pp. 1–2). It gives consumers no instruction.
- On which mindfulness suffices: "evaluative (right) mindfulness is needed", and "wise mindfulness or
  mindfulness that leads to insight is essential if we are to see how our minds are undermined by the
  technologies and content we interact with" (proof p. 6).
- On firms: the typology "allows marketers to rethink the 'attention capturing' and 'more consumption
  is better' business models and innovate so as to develop more mindful modes of business practice"
  (proof p. 6). The paper names no mechanism, regulator or law.

**Berthon, Pitt and Campbell (2019), "Addictive De-Vices", *Journal of Public Policy & Marketing*
38(4): 451–468.** Abstract only; the paper was not opened.

- "This addiction is both akratic (we are aware of the negative consequences) and engineered (designed
  on purpose). Marketers are complicit in this engineering".
- "The authors then turn to policy solutions and discuss how informing, guiding, and restricting can
  help ameliorate the problem and promote the public good."
- A university press release (3 October 2019, quoting Pitt) reports "mandatory labeling for apps", a
  proposal to "mandate natural 'stopping points' in digital offerings", and that "regulators could
  impose taxes directly on the most addictive offerings". This is secondary. Do not quote it as the
  paper.

**Wang, Berthon and Su (2025), HICSS-58, pp. 5184–5193.** Read in full.

- "a sensemaking perspective puts control over big data and AI firmly back in managers hands; and
  places the ethical responsibility squarely on their shoulders." (p. 5191)
- "Future research should develop and assess regulations that promote both ethical and sustainable
  practices" (p. 5192). The paper proposes no rule.

**Su, Wang and Berthon (2023), HICSS-56, pp. 4933–4942.** Read in full. A literature review of AI
ethics in marketing; no remedy placed on users.

**Read at abstract depth only:** Berthon et al. (2024), "Trajectories of AI technologies"; Keaney and
Berthon (2025); the three 2026 papers; the fake-news papers of 2018–2022. None of the texts opened,
in full or as an abstract, mentions literacy, print or the history of media.

## 3. What Williams says about user-side remedies

All from the list of "things we must not do in response to the challenges of the attention economy"
(p. 100). Read: pp. 100–102 only.

- "we must not reply that if someone doesn't like the choices on technology's menu, their only option
  is to 'unplug' or 'detox.'" (p. 100)
- "we must reject the impulse to ask users to 'just adapt' to distraction" (p. 101)
- "education is necessary – but not sufficient – for transcending this problem. Nor will 'media
  literacy' alone lead us out of this forest." (p. 101)
- "We also can't expect companies to self-regulate, or voluntarily refrain from producing the full
  effects they're organizationally structured and financially incentivized to produce." (p. 101)
