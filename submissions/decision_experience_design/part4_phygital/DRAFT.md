# Decision Experience Design, Part 4: Where the Ruling Meets the Body

*The Phygital Context*

A passenger stands on a rain-slicked curb in downtown Chicago watching an illuminated pin on a mobile screen. Two blocks away, a driver circles an intersection, bound to a turn-by-turn navigation overlay that permits no external map application. A countdown timer ticks down from sixty seconds. Neither the driver nor the rider selected the corner where their bodies are scheduled to meet; neither negotiated the fare; neither determined the penalty that accrues if one of them abandons the encounter (Cameron, 2024; Möhlmann et al., 2021). The transaction was computed in the cloud, but its execution takes place on asphalt, where two physical bodies must resolve the frictions of an algorithmic match that neither party set.

Contrast that curb with the classic archetype of behavioral economics: a cafeteria line where fruit is placed at eye level before the desserts (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). In the cafeteria, the architecture is static, material, and indifferent. The counter does not extract telemetry from the tray; it does not reorder the salad bowls as a diner approaches; it applies an identical layout to every person who walks through the turnstile.

Parts 1 and 2 of this series defined Decision Experience Design (DXD) through a precise computational and structural relation: an adaptive, opaque algorithmic intermediary that reads multiple parties simultaneously and commits binding determinations that neither party fully controls. Part 3 examined this structure when applied to retail prediction markets, where collective coordination is visually displayed while an extractive, zero-sum wager is executed. In each case, the discussion assumed what the technology sector routinely assumes: that the interface is a glass screen and the user is a disembodied pair of eyes.

This essay tests whether Decision Experience Design extends into physical space. The corporate discourse of retail, hospitality, and urban planning insists that it does, celebrating the arrival of the "phygital"—the seamless fusion of physical environments and digital workflows. But when we strip away the marketing jargon, what actually changes when an algorithmic intermediary acquires a physical footprint? 

I argue that physical decision experience design exists, but only within narrow boundaries that the prevailing literature routinely misidentifies. Most physical and hybrid environments are not decision experience design at all; they are choice architecture, governed by the traditional principles of environmental psychology and servicescapes. True physical DXD occurs only where an adaptive intermediary reads multiple parties and commits an outcome that the parties must subsequently honor with their physical bodies during the task. Furthermore, the physical layer serves as the testing ground where the ethical claims of digital design are either kept or broken: it is the site where algorithmic rulings harden into material facts, where the cost of refusal is priced, and where the human who might answer for the machine is systematically displaced.

## 1. Opening: One Decision Point, Two Bodies

### 1.1 The Kerb
A driver and a rider meet at a place neither of them chose. The app committed the match. The rider walks to the designated pin. The driver waits out a timer. Each is bound by operational terms the other did not set: the rider to an upfront price and a cancellation fee, the driver to a fare hidden until acceptance and a route locked into the platform’s proprietary navigation (Cameron, 2024; Möhlmann et al., 2021; Rosenblat & Stark, 2016). Cameron (2024) calls the labor process "a three-way interaction between the driver, the rider, and the app" (p. 468). The curbside pickup is the cleanest physical instantiation of an algorithmic match in the contemporary economy. The ruling arrives as a digital notification on a handheld screen, but what follows is entirely physical: two human bodies must navigate traffic, weather, and physical space to execute a determination neither of them configured.

### 1.2 The Cafeteria
In Thaler and Sunstein's (2008) canonical cafeteria, the food director rearranges the display to place carrots and apples at eye level and desserts in less accessible corners. The intervention works "without changing any menus" (p. 3). No one in the queue is monitored; no biometric or transactional telemetry is ingested in real time; the identical spatial arrangement confronts every diner who picks up a tray. In the public-health typology of behavioral nudges (TIPPME), Hollands et al. (2017) classify this class of intervention as "not designed to be interactive or tailored to specific individuals," explicitly excluding systems that read individual transaction histories ("Focus"). The physical cafeteria arranges an option set for an individual chooser, but it does not mediate an ongoing relationship between counterparties.

### 1.3 The Question
Part 1 of this series filed the arranged environment among the predecessors of Decision Experience Design: an identifiable designer, a static interface, and an individual chooser. For the past decade, researchers studying hybrid physical-digital spaces—under the banner of the "phygital"—have documented rooms in which digital layers penetrate physical services. The central question this essay poses is one that the phygital literature systematically evades: Which physical arrangements belong to Decision Experience Design, and which belong to the classical design of a choice?

To answer this, we apply four operational marks drawn from Part 1’s definition:
1. **A distinct intermediary:** A third party standing between counterparties who each possess an interest in the outcome.
2. **Reads both parties:** The system actively ingests data from both parties *in the encounter*, rather than relying on a static record.
3. **Binds both parties:** The system commits an outcome that neither party can unilaterally or costlessly undo.
4. **Does not hold still:** The rules, matching weights, or pricing parameters adapt dynamically across encounters, rather than presenting a fixed rule to all participants.

As we will see, when subjected to these four criteria, the vast majority of physical and hybrid environments fail to qualify.

## 2. What Phygital Researchers Say

### 2.1 A Term Its Reviewers Call Undefined
The term "phygital" did not emerge from computer science or sociology; it originated as an advertising buzzword in the late 2000s. Academic scholarship subsequently imported the concept, but its theoretical standing remains remarkably precarious by its own researchers' admission. In a comprehensive systematic review, Mele, Russo Spena, Marzullo, and Di Bernardo (2023) write that the term "lacks a clear definition," that "vagueness in using the term characterises most of the studies," and that the construct represents an unresolved ontological "mess," utilized primarily by commercial practitioners (§1, §3.1, §4). Johnson and Barlow (2021) similarly conclude that the field "lacks a coherent literature."

Origin accounts in the literature actively conflict. Marketing scholars often attribute the coinage to an agency campaign around 2013, whereas Mele and colleagues' historical bibliography identifies a 2007 ubiquitous computing workshop paper on "phygital maps" as an earlier academic appearance. Claims on trade blogs attributing the term to specific agency executives remain unverified in the peer-reviewed record.

Moving beyond terminology, Mele and Russo-Spena (2025) assert that the real-digital dichotomy is "no longer clearly sustainable," attributing "intelligence and autonomy" to hybrid materiality (pp. 217, 224). Borrowing from Luciano Floridi’s (2015) concept of the "onlife" hyperconnected world, they argue that digital and physical spaces have fused into an inseparable agencement. Yet for decision experience design, the boundary between physical and digital is not an ontological metaphysical divide; it is a concrete design question: Where is the ruling enacted, and where does leaving have a price?

### 2.2 What They Are Trying to Design
In the conceptual frameworks proposed by marketing scholars, the phygital experience is framed around consumer movement between physical and virtual settings, seeking to forge a "coherent continuum" and a "seamless delivery of value" (Batat, 2024, as cited in Alexander & Varley, 2025). The governing ideal is the total elimination of friction. Johnson and Barlow (2021) identify the disappearance of visible payment mechanisms as the cornerstone of the "phygital advantage": when payment becomes imperceptible, conscious attention to spending evaporates, and transaction volume increases.

The defense of friction exists almost entirely outside the phygital canon. Padigar, Li, and Manjunath (2025) argue for "constructive friction" in payment and consent architectures, demonstrating that frictionless checkouts induce impulsive overspending and allow automated assistants to steer consumers into suboptimal purchases. Gustafsson (2026) observes that unstaffed, automated retail stores heighten customer anxiety, noting that algorithmic nudges deployed in the absence of human trust are perceived as coercive surveillance. Pusceddu et al. (2025) deliberately reintroduce human slowness into automated service encounters to restore relational dignity. Yet within mainstream phygital research, friction is treated as a mechanical pathology to be excised.

### 2.3 Problems the Field Identifies
While celebrating seamlessness, empirical phygital studies document severe human distortions:
- **Captivity:** Wilson-Nash, Goode, and Veloutsou (2026) introduce the construct of *digital technology captivity* to describe the condition wherein consumers are compelled to use digital interfaces to access basic physical services. "Captivity is different: the consumer cannot walk away" (§2.1). Mobile-only stadium tickets, QR-code restaurant menus, and cashierless grocery stores enforce participation by dismantling physical alternatives. Lee and Kim (2026) term this *coercive service design*: "the systematic withdrawal of viable human-staffed alternatives."
- **Algorithmic Mediation:** Shabnam et al. (2026) document the "algorithmic mediation of agency" and "digitally enforced rules" in smart service environments, observing that these computational constraints are "non-negotiable and often opaque."
- **The Missing Employee:** Service scholarship rarely examines the customer and frontline employee as an interdependent system (Mulcahy et al., 2026). When automated systems enter the room, the employee is either erased from the model or relegated to a human sensor maintaining the equipment (Gustafsson, 2026; Pusceddu et al., 2025).
- **The Seamless Cult:** A literature audit reveals 118 scholarly works coupling "phygital" directly with "seamless," and only a single paper coupling it with "seamful."

### 2.4 What Phygital Research Fails to Model
Across the entire corpus of phygital marketing research, not a single full-text study models an intermediary that simultaneously reads two human parties and commits a binding determination upon both. The universal framework remains dyadic: a commercial firm designs a channel through which an isolated customer journeys. Authors deploy Bruno Latour's vocabulary to describe smart artifacts as "mediators" (Mele & Russo-Spena, 2025), but they stop short of analyzing the triadic encounter where an algorithm binds two human counterparts at once.

Furthermore, the remedies proposed by phygital researchers are legibility remedies: provide clearer explanatory signage, improve screen transparency, and disclose data practices. Under the governing thesis of this series—algorithmacy is not legibility—those transparency remedies fail. They provide awareness without operational capacity.

## 3. The Predecessor: A Room Arranged Once

### 3.1 Atmospherics
Designing physical spaces to influence human behavior has possessed an academic home for over half a century. Philip Kotler (1973) introduced "atmospherics" as "the conscious designing of space to create certain effects in buyers" (p. 50). Operating through sensory dimensions—visual, auditory, olfactory, and tactile—atmospherics manipulates buyer affect to elevate the probability of purchase. Crucially, Kotler's framework presumes costless exit: the shopper remains an autonomous economic actor browsing an environment they are free to leave. Atmospherics alters the sensory ambiance, but it does not alter the underlying contract or read the individual consumer.

### 3.2 The Servicescape's Two Users
Mary Jo Bitner (1992) developed the "servicescape" framework specifically because marketing had ignored service employees and organizational sociology had ignored customers. Bitner's foundational typology categorizes physical spaces by who acts within them: the customer alone (self-service), the employee alone (remote service), or both simultaneously (interpersonal servicescapes). 

The servicescape explicitly recognizes that physical surroundings affect two distinct user groups with conflicting objectives. A layout optimized for employee task speed may degrade customer comfort, and vice versa; the designer’s job is to settle this structural conflict. However, in Bitner's model, that settlement is made *once, at design time*, through architectural planning and interior layout. The dimensions are "objective physical factors that can be controlled by the firm" (p. 58). The physical shelf, partition, or reception counter remains exactly where the firm positioned it until physical remodeling occurs. 

This finding corrects an assertion in Part 1 of this series. The classical paradigm of physical design does not assume a "single chooser." It assumes two users whose interactions are mediated by a physical space that holds completely still.

### 3.3 The TIPPME Boundary
When behavioral economists codified choice architecture for health and environmental policy, they formalized this stability. In their TIPPME typology, Hollands et al. (2017) classify environmental interventions—placement, sizing, and sensory availability—defining them as "not designed to be interactive or tailored to specific individuals" ("Focus"). Physical choice architecture deliberately draws its perimeter to exclude responsive, tailored systems. That exclusion marks the exact boundary where Decision Experience Design begins.

### 3.4 Correcting Part 1
Part 1 claimed that classical choice architecture relies on a "visible human designer." But as Thaler and Sunstein (2008) emphasize, most choice architects act "without realizing it," and "some nudges are unintentional" (pp. 3, 10). The hospital administrator who lays out hallway tiles or the grocery stocker who fills eye-level shelves rarely conceives of themselves as behavioral engineers. The distinction between choice architecture and DXD does not turn on the visibility or intent of the architect; it turns on whether the mechanism holding the choice is static or adaptive.

## 4. Things That Bind: Scripts, Delegation, and Pre-Digital Devices

### 4.1 Prescriptive Artifacts
If static physical space can structure choice, can a non-digital physical object act as an intermediary? Science and Technology Studies (STS) established long ago that human obligations are routinely delegated to mechanical objects. 

Bruno Latour (1992) famously analyzed how physical artifacts embody social prescriptions. A hydraulic door-closer replaces the unreliable human porter, enforcing the civic rule to keep the door shut; a seat belt alarm forces the driver to click the buckle; a concrete speed bump—the "sleeping policeman"—compels cars to slow down. The artifact carries a script: it physically closes off non-compliant actions on behalf of an absent designer. 

Yet Latour’s mundane artifacts lack the operational marks of Decision Experience Design. A speed bump or a door-closer carries a prescription, but it reads no one. Its resistance is uniform, mechanical, and blind. It does not calibrate its height based on the driver's license or adjust its spring tension based on who approaches the threshold. It embodies an authoritarian script, but it holds completely still.

### 4.2 The Pre-Digital Two-Party Device
A much closer historical precursor to an intermediary is found in Madeleine Akrich's (1987) study of the electricity meter in Abidjan, Côte d'Ivoire. The mechanical meter stood as an arbiter between the state utility company and the household subscriber. To prevent the utility from making fabricated billing claims and to prevent the consumer from stealing power, the physical mechanism required mutual participation: "il faut « l'accord » des deux pour le faire tourner" (§26). 

Akrich’s meter carries two of the four marks: it is an intermediary distinct from the subjective claims of either party, and its mechanical action binds both parties to a legally enforceable measurement. If the consumer cuts the circuit, billing halts; if the utility cuts the supply, the wheel stops. Yet the mechanical meter holds completely still. Its internal gears execute a fixed ratio governed by the laws of electromagnetism, remaining entirely unadaptive across time and use.

### 4.3 The Limits of Built Form
A pervasive trope in design ethics attributes sovereign political authority to built form, exemplified by Langdon Winner's (1980) claim that Robert Moses designed low stone overpasses on Long Island parkways to prevent twelve-foot public transit buses from carrying poor and minority passengers to Jones Beach. 

Historical scholarship has dismantled this myth. As Bernward Joerges (1999) documented, commercial buses were legally prohibited from all New York parkways by administrative regulation; low bridge clearances were standard aesthetic guidelines for suburban parkways nationwide; and thousands of chartered buses traveled directly to Jones Beach via surface avenues. Joerges’s critique cuts directly to the core of physical governance: built form rarely binds on its own; "control rights" and statutory authority carry the actual power (p. 20). Concrete and steel do not govern without an institutional apparatus policing the perimeter.

## 5. Computing in the Room: What HCI Already Knew

### 5.1 Seamful Ubicomp
When Human-Computer Interaction moved computation from desktop screens into physical rooms during the 1990s, it did not worship seamlessness. Mark Weiser (1991), the originator of ubiquitous computing at Xerox PARC, envisioned computation embedded invisibly into everyday physical artifacts. But Weiser quickly recognized that physical environments are fundamentally fragmented. In an unpublished 1994 lecture, Weiser explicitly warned against cosmetic invisibility, advocating instead for "seamful systems, with beautiful seams" (Weiser, 1994, slide 19).

### 5.2 Seams as Affordances
Matthew Chalmers and his colleagues developed this into the paradigm of *seamful design* (Chalmers, 2003; Chalmers & Galani, 2004). Chalmers recognized that physical-computational infrastructures inevitably exhibit seams: wireless networks experience dead zones, positioning sensors drift, and battery levels decay. Rather than concealing these boundaries behind fabricated status bars, seamful design argues that physical and technical seams should be made visible and interactively exploitable. In mobile mixed-reality games, players quickly discovered that "GPS shadows" cast by tall buildings could be utilized as tactical hiding zones (Chalmers, 2003). Seams are not system failures; they are the material edges of computational systems.

### 5.3 The Seamless Chimera
Genevieve Bell and Paul Dourish (2007) launched a decisive critique of ubiquitous computing's promotional claims, characterizing the seamless vision as an ahistorical "chimera." They observed that real-world computing is permanently messy, incomplete, and unevenly maintained, continually entangled with legacy physical infrastructures that resist clean administrative control. 

Modern phygital marketing has revived the exact seamless delusion that HCI dismantled twenty years ago. By treating friction as an intolerable defect, commercial phygital design systematically conceals the seams where algorithmic authority is exercised.

### 5.4 Situated Action and Repair
Decades before the smart environment debates, Lucy Suchman (1985) articulated the fundamental cognitive boundary of automated interaction in her studies of photocopying machines at Xerox PARC. Suchman demonstrated that an interactive machine reads human action only through an impoverished sensory aperture—projecting "a restricted set of effects... onto a prescribed template" (p. 124). The machine cannot grasp the situated physical reality of the human user; it can only track whether designated document switches are opened or closed. 

Confronting this epistemic gulf, Suchman did not recommend that designers bombard users with explanatory diagrams of the machine's internal software states. Instead, she argued for engineering concrete, situated opportunities for *repair*—giving the user the immediate, tangible affordances required to halt the cycle, correct the mechanical error, and restore alignment. Suchman’s insight provides the core historical precedent for Decision Experience Design: algorithmacy is not legibility. When computation enters the physical room, human agency depends not on reading the system's software code, but on possessing the material affordances to contest, halt, and repair its rulings.

### 5.5 Correcting Part 1
Part 1 of this series glossed seamful design as an explanatory disclosure mechanism that allows users to understand system limits. That formulation reflects recent work in Explainable AI (Ehsan et al., 2024), but it mischaracterizes Chalmers’s foundational insight. For Chalmers, seamful design was never about reading an explanation; it was an operational property: designing interfaces so that material discontinuities could be directly felt, understood, and navigated in action.

### 6.1 Ride-Hail Kerbside Matching
Ride-hailing curbside matching represents the textbook case of physical Decision Experience Design, satisfying all four marks completely. The platform is an economically distinct intermediary standing between independent gig workers and retail passengers (M1). It ingests continuous telemetry from both sides: GPS locations, acceleration metrics, driver acceptance rates, passenger ratings, and cancel histories (M2). The match commits an unyielding mutual obligation: the driver is bound to a locked route, a blind fare, and a countdown timer; the rider is bound to a predetermined price, a physical pickup pin, and cancellation penalties (M3). Finally, the operational rules do not hold still: matching parameters, surge multipliers, and compensation structures shift continuously across retraining cycles and unannounced software updates (M4) (Cameron, 2024; Möhlmann et al., 2021; Rosenblat & Stark, 2016; Lee et al., 2015). When driver and passenger meet at the curb, their physical bodies are executing a determination committed by an opaque algorithm.

### 6.2 The Airport Biometric Gate
At the international boarding gate, the traveler encounters the Department of Homeland Security’s Traveler Verification Service (TVS) (CBP, 2018; TSA, 2026). The system is a distinct third-party intermediary: it belongs to the federal sovereign, operating between the commercial airline and the passenger (M1). It reads both parties: the carrier provides the flight manifest and identity records, while the camera captures the traveler’s live facial geometry at the stanchion (M2). The match commits an unyielding outcome: permission to board the aircraft, while a non-match or "Selectee" flag halts boarding and alerts armed enforcement (M3). The underlying galleries and threat assessment models update dynamically per flight and per intelligence cycle (M4) (Adey, 2004; Kitchin & Dodge, 2009).

Crucially, the airport gate reveals an operational hybrid: **the algorithm proposes, but a human commits**. As Kitchin and Dodge (2009) document, at physical border gates, automated code is constantly verified, negotiated, or manually overridden by gate agents and border officers (ms. p. 13). Furthermore, the airport reveals how exit is priced in physical space. While federal regulations state that facial recognition is voluntary for U.S. citizens, the Government Accountability Office (GAO, 2022) found that required opt-out signs were frequently missing, outdated, or positioned where passengers could not read them (p. 11). In regulatory rulemaking, the Department of Homeland Security acknowledged that citizens attempting to exercise an opt-out were repeatedly threatened with denied boarding or subjected to punitive secondary inspections (DHS, 2025). The physical layer is where the theoretical right to refuse is priced out of reach.

### 6.3 Warehouse Pick Paths and Rate Systems
Inside automated fulfillment centers, the scanner directs the warehouse picker along an optimized physical trajectory, presenting the target shelf and counting down the seconds allocated for item extraction (Cheon & Erickson, 2025; Delfanti, 2021). The system calculates an instantaneous "rate," tracking every pause; if inactivity exceeds a rolling threshold of three to five minutes, it logs "Time Off Task" (TOT), with two cumulative hours triggering automatic discharge (Cheon & Erickson, 2025; Gutelius & Theodore, 2019).

Does the warehouse rate system qualify as DXD? Under a strict reading of Mark 1, it fails: the system is the exclusive property of the employer. But under a functional reading, it operates as an intermediary standing between the worker and the frontline manager: the manager serves merely as a "context prober" who is summoned to confirm or excuse algorithmic flags (Cheon & Erickson, 2025). Mark 2 holds only partially: the worker's physical body is read exhaustively, but the second party—the retail customer—is present only as a static order queue. Mark 3 is entirely asymmetric: the rate binds the worker under threat of termination, but binds the customer to nothing. Mark 4 holds strongly: packing rates and TOT thresholds fluctuate dynamically based on seasonal volume and algorithmic recalculations (Cheon & Erickson, 2025; U.S. Senate HELP Committee, 2024).

The warehouse represents Form 4: **the physical floor is where the algorithmic number becomes a physical reality**. When California enacted Assembly Bill 701 (2021) and New York passed the Warehouse Worker Protection Act (2022), the law intervened precisely at the physical interface: mandating that employers disclose written quota descriptions and forbidding metrics that penalize workers for bathroom breaks. When the French Conseil d'État (2025) adjudicated Amazon’s warehouse metrics, it reduced the CNIL’s penalty to €15 million and reinstated three scanner-rate indicators, but affirmed that retaining real-time scanner intervals violated data protection law by turning continuous physical movement into disciplinary evidence.

### 6.4 Hotel Housekeeping Dispatch
In modern hotels, paper room lists have been replaced by housekeeping management platforms like UpKeep (Spektor et al., 2023). Guest Room Attendants (GRAs) receive dynamically ordered cleaning queues on mobile devices. If a room requests express service, the app reshuffles the sequence, forcing the worker to transport heavy linen carts across multiple floors and breaking up the physical efficiency of a consolidated station. Attendants cannot reject assignments without risking discipline (Spektor et al., 2023). Like the warehouse, this is a functionally asymmetric intermediary: it reads the worker's timestamps and the guest's check-out status, binding the worker's physical exertion while preserving the guest's seamless comfort.

### 6.5 Tracked Casino Floors
Natasha Dow Schüll (2005, 2012) documented the evolution of player tracking across Las Vegas casinos. By inserting a magnetic loyalty card, the player’s every action is ingested: bet size, button speed, game duration, and coin-in velocity. Server-based slot machines adjust dealing speeds and game themes in real time, while floor managers re-zone slot banks based on heat maps of player density. The casino represents the physical perfection of behavioral extraction; however, it fails Mark 1. The casino floor is not an intermediary arbitrating between two parties; the house designs, reads, and monetizes. It is choice architecture weaponized through continuous real-time telemetry.

### 6.6 SFpark: The Declared Operating Envelope
San Francisco’s municipal parking pilot, SFpark, installed wireless sensors in roadway pavement to track parking occupancy block by block, adjusting meter rates periodically to maintain a target vacancy of one to two spaces per block (Pierce & Shoup, 2013). SFpark adapts its pricing based on behavioral demand, but it does so through a published, predictable schedule: rates change by discrete, pre-announced increments (typically $0.25) across six-week evaluation cycles. SFpark demonstrates what a **declared operating envelope** looks like in the physical streetscape. The rules drift, but the drift is bounded, public, and slow enough for human cognitive navigation.

### 6.7 App-Required Physical Access and Cashless Counters
In retail environments where physical entry requires scanning an app (such as automated convenience stores), or where counters reject cash payments, the system holds completely still. There is no real-time algorithmic adaptation; a fixed digital rule is enforced at the door or register. Yet these environments represent the sharpest edge of digital technology captivity (Wilson-Nash et al., 2026). When local governments in New York and Massachusetts enacted statutes mandating that physical retail establishments accept cash, they intervened against a structural lockout: preventing platforms from making possession of a digital banking credential a mandatory prerequisite for basic physical subsistence.

### 6.8 Electronic Shelf Labels (ESLs)
The Federal Trade Commission (FTC, 2024, 2025) and congressional inquiries (Warren & Casey, 2024) have raised urgent concerns that digital price tags on retail shelves could enable surveillance pricing—adjusting food and commodity prices based on facial recognition, personal browsing history, or time of day. New York responded with legislation requiring retailers to post explicit disclosures whenever an in-store price is generated by an algorithmic calculation utilizing personal data (New York State Assembly, 2025).

Yet when we examine actual empirical deployments, a stark division appears between regulatory capability and observed practice. Retailers utilize electronic shelf labels almost exclusively for operational efficiency—reducing the labor cost of changing paper tags and executing periodic, store-wide price reductions (Stamatopoulos et al., 2021). No documented commercial deployment in the United States currently uses shelf labels to read the shopper standing in front of the shelf and quote an individual price. The ESL represents an architectural capacity for physical DXD, but one that remains largely unactivated in the field.

### 6.9 Hostile Built Architecture
Anti-homeless spikes, divided benches, and curved window sills enforce an exclusionary social script (Petty, 2016; Rosenberger, 2023). But hostile architecture carries none of the marks of DXD: it is an unyielding, un-sensed physical barrier designed to expel an unwanted population. It represents the coercive authority of built form in its purest, most primitive pre-digital state.

## 7. What Physical Decision Experience Design Is, and Is Not

Subjecting these arrangements to empirical and conceptual testing allows us to evaluate the four potential forms of the thesis outlined at the start of this investigation:

1. **Form 1 (The Algorithmic Intermediary Reaching into Physical Space):** This claim holds, but only within a tightly circumscribed domain. Ride-hail kerbside coordination and the biometric airport gate demonstrate that an algorithmic intermediary can indeed reach into physical space, simultaneously reading and binding two parties who must execute the match with their physical bodies during the task (Wood et al., 2019). Outside transit and border security, however, arrangements that satisfy all four marks are remarkably rare.
2. **Form 2 (Physical or Human Arrangements as Independent Intermediaries):** This claim is refuted. Non-digital physical devices can enforce scripts (Latour's door-closer) or bind reciprocal contracts (Akrich's electric meter), but they hold completely still. Human gatekeepers (such as a restaurant maître d' or an immigration officer) read, adapt, and bind, but treating human agents as "intermediaries" dissolves the boundary of Decision Experience Design entirely, collapsing DXD into the general sociology of human bureaucracy.
3. **Form 3 (Physical Design as Choice Architecture):** This is the baseline reality. The overwhelming majority of what marketing scholars label "phygital"—from automated kiosks and digital signage to ambient retail lighting—does not belong to Decision Experience Design. These environments read no one in real time and apply uniform rules to all visitors. They are servicescapes and choice architecture, governed by the traditional principles established by Kotler, Bitner, and Thaler.
4. **Form 4 (The Physical Layer as the Site of Enforcement and Exit Pricing):** This claim is supported by the strongest empirical evidence, yet it represents the least technologically distinct insight. The physical layer is inescapably where decisions become material facts: where an airport gate closes, where a warehouse worker experiences exhaustion, and where a consumer without a smartphone is turned away at the door. But this principle holds equally for systems with no algorithm inside them. What makes Form 4 analytically vital for DXD is not the presence of computation, but the realization that **the physical world is where the ethical debts of digital design are called due**.

## 8. Design Implications

Extending the three core design-ethics principles established in Part 1 into physical and hybrid environments clarifies how systems must be reformed:

### 8.1 Structural Refusal in Built Space
In software, refusal means closing a tab or clicking an exit link. In physical space, refusal requires an unpriced, functioning physical alternative. A biometric border gate or an app-required store does not offer an authentic exit option if the alternative is secondary interrogation, public humiliation, or total exclusion from essential goods. A structural refusal in physical space must be operational, dignified, and costless.

### 8.2 The Declared Operating Envelope
As demonstrated by SFpark, dynamic and adaptive pricing models must publish their operational rules and respect discrete, intelligible temporal intervals. An algorithmic system operating in a public environment must not drift unannounced across opaque retraining cycles; its parameters must be legible and bounded.

### 8.3 Conspicuous Commitment over Seamless Extraction
The corporate obsession with "frictionless" interaction deliberately obscures the moment when authority is exercised. Consider Amazon’s recent retreat from its "Just Walk Out" ceiling camera tracking in grocery stores: shoppers actively rejected invisible automated billing, preferring the "Dash Cart"—a physical shopping cart equipped with a visible screen displaying a running receipt and item tally as items are placed inside. Human beings demand a visible, conspicuous commitment when their economic resources are on the line.

### 8.4 The Principle of the Accountable Human
In digital systems, user frustration is funneled into chatbot dead-ends and automated help forms. In physical space, platforms systematically deploy "human shock absorbers"—underpaid retail clerks and security guards who possess zero discretion to alter algorithmic decisions, yet are forced to face public outrage on behalf of an absent software architect. Authentic physical decision design requires that wherever an algorithmic ruling binds a human body, a human possessing the institutional authority to grant an exception, override the score, and answer for the system must be physically present.

### 8.5 The Two Research Voids
Finally, this investigation exposes two critical voids in the contemporary research landscape. First, **no empirical study has ever measured algorithmacy as an individual cognitive competence that moderates decision outcomes in physical space**. The field possesses hundreds of studies on technology acceptance and perceived usability, but zero measurements of the operational skill required to navigate adaptive physical environments. Second, **no counter-delegation tool exists for users in physical space**. A gig worker or retail shopper enters the physical encounter completely naked, facing a multi-billion-dollar algorithmic infrastructure with only their naked senses, while the platform deploys autonomous software agents to optimize every second of the encounter.

## 9. Propositions and Limitations

To ensure this critique remains grounded in rigorous empirical inquiry, I advance four falsifiable propositions:

1. In physical service environments, arrangements that dynamically read the individual and adapt pricing or access generate significantly higher rates of subjective entrapment and reported helplessness than fixed physical arrangements carrying an identical monetary exit cost.
2. An adaptive physical system operating under a published, bounded operating envelope (such as SFpark) produces significantly higher decision accuracy and calibration among users than an unannounced real-time adaptive system operating at the same average price level.
3. In physical checkpoints governed by algorithmic sorting (such as airport security or identity verification), the presence of a human officer possessing explicit administrative authority to grant an override significantly increases the proportion of contested rulings resolved without denial of service, compared to checkpoints where staff are restricted to procedural enforcement.
4. Empirical investigations that examine service environments by simultaneously tracking customer telemetry and frontline employee workflows will reveal that algorithmic determinations binding both parties at the point of encounter occur in only a small minority of commercial settings.

This investigation operates under clear methodological limitations. Due to publisher paywalls, Wided Batat’s two foundational framework papers (*Journal of Strategic Marketing*, 2024; *Journal of Services Marketing*, 2026) were reviewed at the abstract level and through secondary scholarly accounts; should full-text access reveal an explicit model of two-sided mutual binding, the characterization of the phygital literature must be revised. Furthermore, the analysis of electronic shelf labels and algorithmic retail pricing relies heavily on regulatory filings and legislative findings rather than proprietary corporate codebase inspections. Finally, the distinction between a "decision" and a "choice," and the rigorous separation of Decision Experience Design from choice architecture, represents the conceptual framework of this essay series; a reader may reject the taxonomy while accepting the empirical findings.

What the evidence demonstrates is that the physical world does not dissolve algorithmic power; it anchors it. When a digital system reaches into physical space, it does not create a frictionless paradise. It creates a physical junction where real human bodies must absorb the contradictions of an invisible machine. If we are to design those junctions with integrity, we must abandon the fantasy of seamlessness and begin the demanding work of building systems whose seams, boundaries, and humans can be seen and held to account.
